> From Quoc's Gemini chat on scaling sustainable agri-food systems ([[Q&A for values, connectivity & scaling|overview of all 27 questions]]). **Main points** condense Gemini's answers. **Where I landed** gives Quoc's own replies, lightly edited for typos. ## Question explored
Who gains when RA scales, and how can its principles survive the entry of powerful actors?
- **Q17 (first list).** When alternative food networks scale, how can local initiatives avoid co-optation by the state or capture by large corporations without losing their social and ecological values?
- **Q20.** To what extent does the depoliticised, carbon-centric framing of "regenerative agriculture" risk commodifying ecosystems, compared with socially transformative agroecological movements?
- **Q22.** What structural mechanisms can protect local PGS and smallholder cooperatives from corporate co-optation as large agri-food firms enter the "green" market?

Quoc framed this cluster as being about values, power, agency and governance mechanisms.

## Main points
**Finance and metrics are not neutral (Gemini, citing Candel et al., 2025, and HLPE, 2019).** Who gets transition finance depends on power. Where governance is weak, the money tends to go to large estates and well-capitalised agribusiness, not to the smallholders who need it most.

**The scaling paradox (Gemini).** RA cannot scale without corporate capital and reach, but inviting corporations in invites capture. Banning large firms is neither realistic nor effective, because they control aggregation, processing and retail. Corporate engagement tends to default to "value-chain capture": proprietary standards and carbon accounting that push compliance costs onto smallholders and keep the premium (Gemini cited Bless et al., 2025, and Bemelmans & Maertens, 2025). Large actors may also define "regenerative" by narrow carbon metrics that are easy to certify on paper (Tittonell et al., 2022). Gemini argued farmers should co-design those metrics through PGS and cooperatives.

**PGS under pressure (Gemini).** As PGS scale, commercial and administrative actors try to standardise them. Peer trust is then hollowed out and PGS become cheap audits for buyers. Gemini's answer: legally recognise PGS as market validation in their own right, not as a step towards third-party certification.

**"Just mechanisms" (Gemini).** The state acts as referee. It moves from voluntary CSR to mandatory due diligence and transparency on how value is shared, with:
- contract protection: fair trading rules, minimum contract lengths and price-sharing formulas (Gemini cited Peterson et al., 2022);
- national or regional definitions of RA based on measurable outcomes such as soil health and chemical reduction (Newton et al., 2020), to stop greenwashing.

**Q20, is RA depoliticised? (Gemini).** Critical agrarian scholars (Tittonell et al., 2022; "Giraldo & Rosset") argue that mainstream RA strips out land, labour and power. Gemini agreed with Quoc that *implementing* RA is political: it means negotiating land access, subsidies, irrigation budgets and bargaining power.

**Q20, commodifying ecosystems (Gemini).**

| Risks | Benefits |
|---|---|
| Soil carbon is reversible: ploughing, drought or a new owner can release it | Carbon markets, ESG bonds and PES bring private capital that public budgets cannot match |
| Additionality excludes farmers who were already good stewards, while past polluters get paid to change | Puts a price on externalities and rewards good management |
| High carbon prices can drive land speculation and push out tenants | Early income during the transition yield dip |

**Q22, safeguards (Gemini).**
- Write the definition and autonomy of PGS into food safety or organic law.
- Cooperatives keep ownership of processing equipment, trademarks and brands. Gemini suggested a "minimum 51% controlling stake" in joint aggregation ventures.
- Dual-track governance: firms may buy volume but may not change peer review or impose input packages.

## Where I landed
*My own conclusions.*
- The risk of favouring large-scale businesses is real and very difficult to tackle.
- Government is critical. Its direction will determine how true resilience and sustainability are incorporated.
- I discussed this in my PhD thesis: even PGS risk being taken over by more powerful actors. We can't "ban" large firms from joining. We should find just mechanisms and establish scientifically sound policies, so that benefits are distributed equally and the key principles are not corrupted.
- Q20: Is regenerative agriculture "depoliticised"? I don't think so. It takes a lot of political work to make it happen. Is commodifying ecosystems risky? It has some risks, but it also brings benefits.
- Q22 has already been addressed in the earlier discussion.
