## My first take
*Written by Quoc before reading the draft (2026-10-21). Never edited later.*

connectivity - connection among actors in the food systems, and between human and nature.

## Definitions
**As an agroecological principle.** Connectivity is the 11th of the 13 consolidated agroecological principles of the HLPE (2019), as set out by Wezel et al. (2020, p. 1). It means to "ensure proximity and confidence between producers and consumers" through fair and short distribution networks and by re-embedding food systems in local economies (Wezel et al. 2020, Table 1, p. 7). It acts at the food-system level, not the field or farm (Wezel et al. 2020, Fig. 4, p. 9). FAO's 10 Elements have no separate connectivity element. Wezel et al. (2020, Table 1, p. 7) map it to "circular and solidarity economy", which "reconnects producers and consumers" (Barrios et al. 2020, p. 236). Bezner Kerr et al. (2022, p. 3) gloss it as "building linkages and trust between producers and consumers" and group it with fairness and social values. In Gliessman's levels of transition, it is Level 4: a more direct connection between growers and eaters (Gliessman 2016, p. 188; Ewert et al. 2023, Fig. 1, p. 355). See [[Agroecology]].

**Earlier roots: re-connection in short food supply chains.** Short food supply chains create "new linkages between agriculture and society, producers and consumers" (Renting et al. 2003, p. 398). They are defined less by distance than by the product reaching the consumer "embedded with information" about place and production (Marsden et al. 2000, p. 425). Eriksen (2013, p. 48) splits proximity into three domains: geographical proximity, relations of proximity and values of proximity.

**Human–nature connectedness.** Sustainability science names "reconnecting people to nature" as one of three realms of deep leverage, alongside restructuring institutions and rethinking knowledge (Abson et al. 2017, p. 30). Ives et al. (2018, p. 1389) identify five types of connection to nature: material, experiential, cognitive, emotional and philosophical. See [[Leverage points]].

**Ecological feedback.** Sundkvist et al. (2005, p. 224) frame connectivity as "tightening feedback loops" between ecosystems, chain actors and consumers.

## Features
- **Four dimensions in the literature** (Claude's grouping, not a published typology): (1) producer–consumer, or market, connectivity; (2) actor and knowledge networks; (3) human–nature connectedness; (4) ecological or landscape connectivity. The HLPE definition covers only (1). The scaling literature relies on (2) and (3); see [[Scaling agroecology]].
- **The link to value.** Connection lets producers capture more value: they "can profit from getting a higher share of revenue" (Wezel et al. 2020, p. 6). Embedded information can command "a premium price" (Marsden et al. 2000, p. 425). "The more embedded and differentiated a product becomes, the scarcer it becomes" (Renting et al. 2003, p. 401). See [[Values of sustainable agri-food products]].
- **Trust as the mechanism.** For credence attributes, connectivity works mainly through trust (Bezner Kerr et al. 2022, p. 3).
- **Measurement.** Connectivity is rarely measured directly. Possible proxies are chain length, producer share of the price, type of guarantee (third-party, PGS, relational) and buyer–seller relationship terms. These proxies are Claude's suggestion, not taken from a source.
- **Debates.** The "local trap" is the tendency to "assume something inherent about the local scale" (Born & Purcell 2006, p. 195). Local food efforts can also "reinforce and widen inequities in access to fresh, local food" (Wezel et al. 2020, p. 6).
## Implications
- **Viet Nam: trust has a price.** Vegetable farmers in northern Viet Nam would "request a 21% premium to accept selling to an unknown buyer" (Enthoven & Van den Broeck 2021, p. 1).
- **Viet Nam: PGS as a connectivity institution.** Participatory guarantee systems strengthen "interactions between buyers, growers, and other stakeholders" (Grovermann et al. 2024, p. 2). PGS raised farm profitability by 117% and the range of sales channels by 23% (p. 1).
- **Agroecological territories.** Nested markets "thicken networks of solidarity and relations of reciprocity" (Anderson et al. 2019, p. 11).
- **Connectivity as a leverage point.** Material reconnections "may shorten feedbacks" and influence how systems are designed (Abson et al. 2017, p. 35).

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** Your two parts, actor–actor and human–nature, match two of the four dimensions in the literature.
- **Narrower in the official definition.** The HLPE principle is specifically about producers and consumers: proximity, confidence, fair short networks and local economies. It does not cover all food-system actors.
- **Extends.** The literature adds two things your take leaves out: ecological connectivity (feedback loops, landscapes), and the idea that connectivity is valuable because it carries information and builds trust.
- **Challenges.** Critics warn that connection or proximity does not guarantee good outcomes (the local trap, inequities).
