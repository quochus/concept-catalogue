## Definitions
Market systems development (MSD) began as a response to the limits of direct delivery in aid. Agencies that paid for training, inputs or services found that benefits often ended with the project. The UK Department for International Development (DFID) and the Swiss Agency for Development and Cooperation (SDC) backed an alternative called Making Markets Work for the Poor (M4P), later widely known as MSD.

The approach rests on one premise: "the poor are dependent on market systems for their livelihoods" (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 5), so changing those systems "to work more effectively and sustainably for the poor" should reduce poverty (PDF p. 5). M4P is described as "an overarching approach to development" aimed at "large-scale, sustainable change" (PDF p. 10), within which many tools, such as value chain analysis, can be used.

Its target is systemic change, defined as "change in the underlying causes of market system performance" (PDF p. 8), usually in rules and supporting functions rather than the core exchange. This marks the difference from value chain projects that work with one chain, and from [[Market linkages]] projects that build single buyer–seller relationships.

Definitions diverge on emphasis. Albu (2008, PDF p. 12) notes that M4P "sees poverty as a symptom of flaws" in market structures, while the sustainable livelihoods approach starts from households' assets and strategies; he argues the two are complementary.

**Working definition used here:** market systems development is an approach in which outside actors temporarily facilitate changes in the rules, services and relationships of a market system, so that it delivers better and lasting outcomes for poor producers and consumers at scale *(Claude's synthesis of The Springfield Centre, 2008; Albu, 2008)*.

## Features
**Core principles.** The Springfield Centre (2008) names four: a focus on market systems, priority to scale, priority to sustainability, and a facilitation role. It adds a fifth logic, "addressing underlying causes (rather than symptoms) of weak performance" (PDF p. 33).

**Facilitation.** Agencies should not become permanent market players: "facilitation is inherently a temporary role" (PDF p. 33). Facilitators work through firms, associations and government to change incentives and capacities, then step back.

**Scale and sustainability.** Scale does not require large budgets: the ambition of large-scale change "does not mean interventions themselves have to be large" (PDF p. 38). Sustainability is defined as the system's capacity to keep delivering, not only the benefits; yet "in practice, sustainability is often demoted to a secondary concern" (PDF p. 38).

**Tools.** MSD uses market system mapping, value chain analysis and constraint analysis. The M4P value chain toolbook chose "Pro-poor growth" as "the main objective of value chain analysis" (M4P, 2008, PDF p. 16).

**Debates.** MSD is criticised for slow, hard-to-attribute results and for relying on private-sector incentives that may bypass the poorest. Albu (2008, PDF p. 10) himself calls it "a work in progress". Its links to environmental goals and to food system transformation are less developed than its links to poverty and growth.

## Implications
**What the evidence shows.** Most published evidence comes from programme reviews rather than independent impact studies. The Springfield Centre (2008, PDF p. 27) chose three cases "on the basis of their performance": financial services in South Africa, radio in Uganda and vegetables in Bangladesh.

**Cases.**

| Case | Where | Market system | What the programme did | Reported results | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Katalyst vegetables | Bangladesh | Vegetable inputs and information | Worked with input firms to train retailers to advise farmers, rather than training farmers directly | Reached "nearly 3,000 retailers serving a farmer clientele of approximately 1 million farmers"; productivity gains of 20–30% | The Springfield Centre (2008, PDF pp. 28–29) |
| FinMark Trust | South Africa | Financial services | Changed rules and supporting functions for low-income finance | Growth in the core attributed to changes in rules and support | The Springfield Centre (2008, PDF p. 37) |
| Farmer-led irrigation dialogue space | Ghana | Small-scale irrigation | A multi-stakeholder dialogue linked firms, farmers and government | A space that can "trickle systemic change" via private investment | Minh et al. (2020, PDF p. 1) |

**Lessons.** The Bangladesh case shows the main MSD mechanism: changing an existing firm's business model so that benefits continue after the project. The Ghana case shows MSD ideas meeting scaling and multi-stakeholder approaches in CGIAR work. For IP1+, an MSD lens would ask which input suppliers, traders or certification bodies could profitably carry agroecological practices and products in Laos and Cambodia.
