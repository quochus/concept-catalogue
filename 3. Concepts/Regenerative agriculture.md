## Definitions
The term is older than its current popularity. "Regenerative" was attached to farming in the late 1970s, but "Regenerative Agriculture" "came into wider circulation in the early 1980s" through the US-based Rodale Institute (Giller et al., 2021, p. 14). Robert Rodale defined it as farming that, at rising productivity, "increases our land and soil biological production base" (Giller et al., 2021, p. 14). The problem it answered was soil degradation under intensive, input-dependent farming.

The idea returned to prominence after about 2015, and the reason it returned explains its present shape. Soil carbon became a climate solution, so soil health was linked to carbon drawdown: "Regenerative agriculture has at its core the intention to improve the health of soil" or restore degraded soil, which in turn can draw down atmospheric carbon (Rhodes, 2017, p. 80). Because the framing suited many interests, it was taken up by "many of the major multi-national food companies" as well as by civil society and NGOs (Giller et al., 2021, p. 13). The broader idea of regenerative food systems followed, aiming at "creating a net positive impact" on people and planet rather than only doing less harm (El-Sayed & Cloutier, 2022, PDF p. 1).

There is still no agreed core definition. A review of 229 papers and 25 practitioner websites found "no legal or regulatory definition of the term" and no widely accepted one in use (Newton et al., 2020, PDF p. 1). Definitions are based on processes such as cover crops, livestock integration and reduced tillage, or on outcomes, "to improve soil health, to sequester carbon, and to increase biodiversity", or on both (Newton et al., 2020, PDF p. 1). From 28 studies, Schreefel et al. (2020, PDF p. 1) propose a provisional definition: "an approach to farming that uses soil conservation as the entry point to regenerate" and contribute to multiple ecosystem services.

Definitions diverge because users want different things from them. Process-based definitions suit farmers and extension; outcome-based ones suit carbon markets, certification and corporate sourcing targets, which pay for measurable results (Newton et al., 2020). Some advocates add social and economic wellbeing of farmers and communities; others stop at soil and carbon.

The boundaries with neighbouring approaches are blurred. Giller et al. (2021, p. 13) argue that regenerative agriculture is "a re-framing of what have been considered to be two contrasting approaches", namely [[Agroecology]] and [[Sustainable intensification]], under one banner. Compared with agroecology, which stresses "reducing inputs and fostering diversity alongside social and political transformation" (HLPE, 2019, p. 15), regenerative agriculture is usually narrower: soil-centred and often market-led. Many of its practices overlap with [[Conservation agriculture]].

**Working definition used here:** regenerative agriculture is an approach to farming that uses soil conservation and soil health as its entry point to restore ecosystem functions, carbon and biodiversity, defined in practice either by a set of practices or by measured outcomes *(Claude's synthesis of Schreefel et al., 2020; Newton et al., 2020)*.

## Features
**Components.** The practices most often named are cover crops, reduced or no tillage, residue retention, crop diversity and the integration of livestock; the outcomes most often named are soil health, soil carbon and biodiversity (Newton et al., 2020, PDF p. 1). Many of these practices are long-standing good agricultural practice; others are contested (Giller et al., 2021, p. 13). How they are expected to work is the next question.

**Mechanisms.** The claimed mechanism runs through soil organic matter: more organic matter improves structure, water retention and nutrient cycling, raising yields and storing carbon (Rhodes, 2017, p. 80). Agronomists add a caution: soil organic matter supports yields mainly through the nutrients it supplies, and without external nutrients "this will degrade the soil resource base and lead to a decline in yields" (Giller et al., 2021, p. 20). The mechanism therefore depends on conditions.

**Conditions.** Biophysical conditions matter: gains in soil carbon are largest in low-yielding systems where more biomass returns to the soil (Giller et al., 2021, p. 20). Economic conditions matter as much. In Australia, "economic factors are the primary driver of the growth of regenerative agriculture" (Bless et al., 2025, PDF p. 1), linked to carbon and nature markets. In Cambodia, 90% of rice farmers using cover crops saw better soil fertility, but the same share named economic and climatic barriers to scaling up (À Hauariki et al., 2026, p. 1). These conditions feed into how it is measured.

**Measurement.** Measurement follows the definition used. Process-based schemes count practices adopted; outcome-based schemes measure soil carbon, soil health indicators and biodiversity, which links regenerative agriculture to carbon credits, certification and corporate sourcing targets (Newton et al., 2020). Long-term evidence on yield resilience is thin: Giller et al. (2021, p. 20) note that "evidence on this is scarce". These gaps drive the debates.

**Debates.** Two debates are live. The agronomic debate asks whether claims for soil carbon and yields are supported by evidence (Giller et al., 2021). The political debate asks whom the concept serves: in neoliberal settings, its growth risks "extending the commodification of nature" through carbon and nature markets (Bless et al., 2025, PDF p. 1). What remains unresolved is whether regenerative agriculture will stay a soil-focused, market-led label or take on the social principles of agroecology.

## Implications
- **Australia: markets drive adoption.** Policymakers and farmers are drawn by economic co-benefits and nature markets more than by ecological motives; the authors warn of extractive human–nature relations (Bless et al., 2025, PDF p. 1).
- **Cambodia: cover crops in rice systems.** A domestic cover-crop seed chain grew quickly; "90% of rice farmers observed notable improvements in soil fertility", but economic and climate constraints limit wider use (À Hauariki et al., 2026, p. 1).
- **Corporate supply chains.** Major food companies have adopted the term for sourcing commitments, which raises the stakes of how it is defined and verified (Giller et al., 2021, p. 13; Newton et al., 2020).

Together, the cases show regenerative agriculture spreading where it pays: through markets, carbon payments and corporate targets. For IP1+ in Cambodia, the question is which of its values local buyers and consumers will reward, and how it relates to the agroecology principles the programme also uses.
