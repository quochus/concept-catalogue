## Definitions
Willingness to pay (WTP) comes from welfare economics, where the value of a good is what people would give up to obtain it. Two strands shaped how it is measured. Environmental economists developed stated-preference methods for goods with no market price, used "where market transaction data have limitations or do not exist" (Holmes et al., 2017, p. 134). Consumer theory added the idea that people value products for their attributes: "Lancaster’s (1966) theory of consumer demand provided the basic conceptual structure" for valuing attributes separately (Holmes et al., 2017, p. 136). McFadden's random utility model then linked choices to welfare measures, so that WTP could be estimated for each attribute (Holmes et al., 2017, p. 136).

The method moved from the environment into food as credence qualities such as organic, safe or fair trade became selling points. Because these qualities cannot be seen, researchers asked how much consumers would pay for them, and the WTP estimate came to stand for "the price premium" a product could earn (Li & Kallas, 2021, PDF p. 1). Concerns about whether people pay what they say led to non-hypothetical methods such as experimental auctions, where participants use real money (My et al., 2018, p. 283).

In current use, WTP is the maximum price a person would pay for a product or an attribute, usually reported as a premium over a conventional product. In meta-analyses it is "the percent payment increased over conventional food price" (Li & Kallas, 2021, PDF p. 3). Three families of methods dominate *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: contingent valuation, which asks directly about a price; choice experiments, which infer WTP from trade-offs between attribute bundles; and experimental auctions or field experiments, which observe real bids or purchases.

Definitions diverge on whether stated WTP measures value or only intention. Stated-preference researchers treat it as a valid welfare measure when well designed. Critics note that "CE responses are stated preferences, and concerns about strategic behavior or hypothetical bias arise" (Holmes et al., 2017, p. 135). The difference follows purpose: welfare analysis needs a value, while market planning needs a prediction of sales.

WTP needs to be kept apart from its neighbours. Willingness to accept is the price a seller requires, as when farmers ask a premium to sell to an unknown buyer (Enthoven & Van den Broeck, 2021). [[Intention–behaviour gap]] describes why stated WTP often exceeds what people actually pay. [[Discrete choice experiments]] are one method of estimating WTP, not the concept itself.

**Working definition used here:** willingness to pay is the maximum amount a buyer would give for a product or attribute, usually estimated as a premium over a conventional alternative with contingent valuation, choice experiments or non-hypothetical methods such as auctions *(Claude's synthesis of Holmes et al., 2017; Li & Kallas, 2021)*.

## Features
**Components.** A WTP study has four elements *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: (1) the good and its attributes, for example a label, certifier or traceability information; (2) a payment vehicle, such as a price level or a bid; (3) an elicitation format, from open questions and bidding games to choice sets and auctions; and (4) a statistical model that turns answers into money values. In choice experiments, the goal is "to estimate economic values for characteristics (or attributes)" of a good (Holmes et al., 2017, p. 133). The procedure follows from these elements.

**Procedure.** Researchers define attributes and levels from qualitative work, design the choice sets or bids, pilot the survey, collect data and estimate a model. In contingent valuation, a double-bounded format asks a second, higher or lower bid after the first answer; in Hanoi, this format estimated WTP for safe pork (Nguyen et al., 2019, p. 378). In choice experiments, respondents choose among bundles, and WTP is the ratio of an attribute's coefficient to the price coefficient. In auctions, people bid real money, sometimes after receiving more information in rounds (My et al., 2018, p. 283). Each format rests on assumptions.

**Assumptions.** Stated methods assume that people understand the good, answer truthfully and would behave as they say. Choice experiments also assume that people trade off attributes rather than follow simple rules. Researchers sometimes delete answers that look "irrational", but doing so "may result in the removal of valid preferences" and bias the sample (Lancsar & Louviere, 2006, p. 797). Because these assumptions often fail, quality criteria matter.

**Quality criteria.** Good practice includes realistic attributes and price ranges, piloting, tests for consistency, and reporting of model fit. Using real money where possible reduces hypothetical bias. The choice of method changes the result: "the WTP estimate conducted by hypothetical approach" is higher than non-hypothetical estimates, and contingent valuation gives higher values than choice experiments (Li & Kallas, 2021, PDF p. 1). This leads to the method's main limits.

**Critiques and limits.** The central critique is the gap between stated and actual payment. A review of sustainable wine found that none of the studies "examined purchase behaviour in real market scenarios with real market transactions" (Schäufele & Hamm, 2017, p. 379). Hypothetical bias inflates premiums, so a stated 30% premium cannot be read as a price the market will pay. WTP studies also measure demand at one moment and among people who can afford the product, which may miss low-income consumers. What remains unresolved is how far calibration or non-hypothetical designs can close the gap.

## Implications
| Study | Context | How WTP was used | Insight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Li & Kallas (2021) | Meta-analysis of 80 studies worldwide | Pooled premiums for sustainable food | Average premium of 29.5%, highest for organic and fruit and vegetables; Asian premiums similar to Europe's |
| My et al. (2018) | Mekong Delta rice, Viet Nam | Experimental auctions with information rounds | 9% premium for certified sustainable rice, rising to 33% with information on certification and traceability |
| Nguyen et al. (2019) | Safe pork, Hanoi | Double-bounded contingent valuation | Most consumers would pay more; mean WTP 49% and 38% above market price for two cuts |
| Ha et al. (2019) | Organic vegetables, rural and urban Viet Nam | Interval regression on bids | Trust in labels and income raised WTP in both regions |

Across these studies, WTP for sustainable or safe food is positive and often large: "The results suggest that the overall WTP premium for sustainability (in percentage terms) is 29.5% on average" (Li & Kallas, 2021, PDF p. 1). In Viet Nam, the rice study shows that information and trust drive the premium: consumers paying premiums had "greater trust in food quality certiﬁcation" (My et al., 2018, p. 283), and promoting sustainable rice "crucially hinges on strengthening consumers’ knowledge of and trust in food quality certiﬁcation" (My et al., 2018, p. 283). In Hanoi, consumers pay most for an organic label certified by NGOs and least for VietGAP vegetables without a label (Thai et al., 2017, p. 401). Ecolabels also raise WTP in pooled choice experiments (Bastounis et al., 2021).

**Practical guidance.** Use WTP to compare attributes and to test which signals (label, certifier, traceability) raise value, not to set a market price. Prefer non-hypothetical methods, or calibrate stated results, when the aim is a business case. Pair WTP with data on actual purchases where possible, and report results by income group and region, since rural and urban consumers "should not be treated as a homogenous group" (Ha et al., 2019, PDF p. 1). For regenerative products in Cambodia or Laos, WTP studies are most useful for designing the signal and the information that goes with it.
