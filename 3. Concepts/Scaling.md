## Definitions
Scaling began as a practical question in development and social innovation: how can a programme that works in one place reach many more people? Early answers treated it as replication or expansion. Social-innovation scholars then separated three strategies: scaling out to more people and places, scaling up to change institutions and law, and scaling deep, "Changing relationships, cultural values and beliefs" (Moore et al., 2015, p. 75, Fig. 1). They argued that lasting change needs people's "hearts and minds, their values and cultural practices" (p. 74).

Because replication often failed outside its first setting, the meaning shifted from copying to system change. In agricultural innovation research, Hermans et al. (2013, p. 117) linked each direction to a network function: "upscaling and institutional entrepreneurship" and "outscaling and innovation brokerage", alongside learning. Transition researchers proposed patterns of upscaling, "growing, replication, accumulation, and transformation" (Naber et al., 2017, p. 342), and sustainability scientists widened the list to eight amplification processes (Lam et al., 2020, PDF p. 1). Over the past decade, thinking has "shifted from simplistic 'copy-paste' models" to recognising context, complexity and unpredictability (Kihoro et al., 2025, PDF p. 1). Agricultural reviews explain why. Existing methods follow the premise "find out what works in one place and do more of the same", and so "scaling initiatives often do not produce the desired effect" (Wigboldus et al., 2016, PDF p. 1). Woltering et al. (2019, PDF p. 1) criticise "a narrow focus on reaching numbers" and argue for a shift "from reaching many to a process aiming to achieve sustainable systems change at scale". Schut et al. (2020, PDF p. 1) describe this as a third wave, "outcome-oriented scaling", after technology adoption and the scaling of innovations.

The current core definition combines direction and process: scaling is the set of processes through which an innovation reaches more users, becomes embedded in institutions, and changes norms, in a way that is adaptive rather than planned. In CGIAR this is framed as adaptive scaling, with "four interconnected functions for adaptive scaling: niche, reach, accelerate and transform" (IWMI, 2021, PDF p. 3).

Definitions diverge on whether scaling can be planned. Programme-oriented approaches design pathways and measure readiness; grassroots studies find that distributed agency "poses a challenge to the idea of planned processes of outscaling and upscaling" (Hermans et al., 2016, p. 285). They also diverge on the goal: growth in numbers, or transformation of the system.

Scaling should be kept apart from [[Diffusion pathways]], which describe how adoption spreads among users, and from [[Scaling science]], the field that studies and designs scaling. [[Scaling agroecology]] is its application to one approach; [[Innovation bundling]] concerns what is scaled together.

**Working definition used here:** scaling is the adaptive set of processes by which innovations, practices or initiatives reach more people and places, become embedded in institutions and policy, and reshape the values and relationships that sustain them *(Claude's synthesis of Moore et al., 2015; Lam et al., 2020; IWMI, 2021)*.

## Features
**Components.** Three directions (out, up, deep); several processes within them (growing, replicating, transferring, spreading, stabilising, accelerating); and the actors who perform them, including brokers, institutional entrepreneurs and intermediaries (Hermans et al., 2013). How these combine is the mechanism.

**Mechanisms.** Scaling works through networks and learning. Different network roles carry different directions: brokerage carries outscaling, institutional entrepreneurship carries upscaling (Hermans et al., 2013, p. 117). Adaptive scaling adds orchestration: "research-for-development organizations act as boundary spanners" that align incentives, build trust and enable collaboration (Minh & Schmitter, 2025, PDF p. 1). See [[Intermediaries]].

**Conditions.** Kohl (2023, PDF p. 1) identifies ten factors, including participation, inclusion, leadership, adaptation, funding and implementation models, systems change and partnerships, whose weight varies with the innovation and context. Resources and time matter most: going to large scale needs longer horizons and "a portfolio rather than a project approach" (Kohl, 2023, PDF p. 1). Leadership also matters: in the scaling of orange-fleshed sweetpotato across Africa, "The role of committed leadership during all phases was critical for success" (Low & Thiele, 2020, PDF p. 1). And organisations differ in capacity: "Organizations are not automatically willing or able to adopt a systems approach" (Woltering et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).

**Measurement.** Scaling out is counted in users, area or reach; scaling up in policies, budgets and institutions; scaling deep is rarely measured. CGIAR tools assess readiness and track functions over time (IWMI, 2021). Scaling Readiness "encourages critical reflection on how ready innovations are for scaling" and on the actions that could accelerate it (Sartas et al., 2020, PDF p. 1), and the Scaling Scan tool was used in 54 workshops between 2017 and 2023 to support a systems approach (Woltering et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). The science of scaling is "defined as the design, testing, and validation of scientific theories, concepts, and methods" to guide scaling (Kihoro et al., 2025, PDF p. 1).

**Debates.** First, effectiveness: despite many proposals, "few have yet either gone to scale or been implemented sustainably at large scale" (Kohl, 2023, PDF p. 1). Second, responsibility: "many scaling efforts remain relatively unsustainable or irresponsible", with unintended effects on equity and emissions (Kihoro et al., 2025, PDF p. 1). Third, meaning: "upscaling itself remains a fuzzy concept" (Augenstein et al., 2020, p. 143). Fourth, framing: critics of the adoption paradigm argue that "adoption simplifies and mischaracterises what happens during processes of technological change" (Glover et al., 2019, p. 169), so counting adopters misses how practices are reconfigured. What remains unresolved is how to scale responsibly without losing the context that made an innovation work.

## Implications
**What the evidence shows.** Reviews agree that scaling is non-linear and context-dependent. A cross-country synthesis of agricultural innovations in Bangladesh, Brazil, India, Kenya, Senegal, Uganda and Zambia confirms the importance of participation, partnerships and systems change but finds limited quantitative evidence of success at scale (Kohl, 2023). Typologies have converged on out, up and deep plus several amplification processes (Lam et al., 2020).

**Cases.** Chosen to span the main scaling pathways: policy transfer, market aggregation, grassroots networks, research-for-development ecosystems, project networks, value chains and national programmes *(Claude's typology)*.

| Case | Where, when | Pathway | What was done | Outcomes reported | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Cover-crop seed value chain | Cambodia, 2017–2024 | Value chain for a practice | Domestic seed production to supply cover crops | Rapid growth in seed area, but farmers named economic and climatic barriers | À Hauariki et al. (2026, p. 1) |
| 1 Must Do, 5 Reductions (1M5R) for rice | Mekong Delta, Viet Nam, 2010s | National programme plus chain arrangements | Best management practices promoted through policy, certification, contract farming and consolidation | Adoption by "at least 104,448 smallholder rice farmers" in eight provinces | Flor et al. (2021, PDF p. 1) |
| Alternate wetting and drying | Philippines, 20 years | Innovation platform to national policy | Vertically integrated actors and locally adapted AWD | Reached national policy, but "the dominant focus on technology transfer" neglected context | Enriquez et al. (2021, PDF p. 1) |

**Lessons across cases.** Scaling succeeded where an actor stayed with the process across settings (Food for Life, sweetpotato), where intermediaries or boundary spanners connected actors (irrigation, Laos), where policy and markets converged (Mekong Delta rice, where scaling "was enabled through a convergence of different socio-technical systems"; Flor et al., 2021, PDF p. 1), and where a chain supplied the inputs a practice needed (Cambodia). It faltered where expansion was assumed to be good in itself (food hubs) or where local context was ignored. The cases do not show long-run impact at national scale, which matches the reviews' main gap.
