## Definitions
Values-based supply chains (VBSCs) were proposed in the United States in the 2000s by the Agriculture of the Middle task force. The problem they addressed was the decline of mid-sized farms, which are "too large for the short chain direct markets" but too small for large commodity markets (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016, PDF p. 2). Without suitable markets, these farms were failing, and the structure of agriculture was becoming "even more bifurcated" between very small and very large farms (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016, PDF p. 2). Stevenson and Pirog adapted the business idea of the value chain to this problem, describing "strategic alliances between midsize independent (often cooperative) food production, processing and distribution or retail enterprises" that seek to keep more value with farmers (quoted in Bloom & Hinrichs, 2011, p. 14). The Agriculture of the Middle group framed these chains as business models for a "third tier" between direct markets and commodity chains, in which "Farmers and ranchers are treated as strategic partners, not as interchangeable input suppliers" (Stevenson et al., 2011, p. 1).

The concept spread from a policy agenda into research on regional food systems. It borrowed from European work on alternative food networks and nested markets, and the overlap is acknowledged: "There are strong similarities between VBSCs and alternative food networks" (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016, PDF p. 2). What set VBSCs apart was scale. They were meant to operate "at a larger scale than direct marketing while deliberately embedding mechanisms to ensure social, environmental and economic benefits" (Bloom & Hinrichs, 2011, p. 14). Food hubs became one of their main organisational forms (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016, PDF p. 3); in Europe they are described as arrangements "bridging structural holes in the agri-food markets between small producers and the consumers" (Berti & Mulligan, 2016, PDF p. 1). The word "value" also changed meaning. Classic value chain analysis meant economic value, but Stevenson and Pirog gave it "the dual connotation of value, referencing both economic value and ethical or social value" (Diamond & Barham, 2011, p. 103), and the chains emphasise "vertical coordination rather than integration throughout the supply chain" (Diamond & Barham, 2011, p. 103). France developed a parallel model, mid-tier supply chains; in both countries these chains emerged as "viable alternatives for some small and mid-sized farms that had previously struggled" (Fleury et al., 2016, PDF p. 1).

The core definition today is functional. "Wholesale channels that provide marketing options for small and mid-scale producers and support these values are referred to as “values-based supply chains”" (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016, PDF p. 1). The values may be local origin, small or mid-scale production, or practices that protect the environment or workers. VBSCs have two goals: to "provide greater economic stability for producers and others along the supply chain" and to provide high-quality regional food (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016, PDF p. 1). Their distinguishing elements are differentiated products, commitment to the welfare of all partners, strategic partnerships and shared information (Bloom & Hinrichs, 2011, p. 14).

Definitions diverge on what drives the chain. US proponents put market differentiation at the centre: products earn a premium because they carry values. Critics argue that this formulation foregrounds "market-based differentiation as the driver of the sustainable food agenda", overshadowing "the primary and pivotal role of public policy" and public purchasing (Stahlbrand, 2017, p. 78). The first view treats VBSCs as business models; the second places them within public food strategies.

VBSCs need to be kept apart from their neighbours. [[Alternative food networks]] and short chains often rely on direct sales; VBSCs work through intermediaries at regional scale. [[Inclusive value chains]] focus on bringing smallholders into existing chains; VBSCs build new chains around shared values. [[Values of sustainable agri-food products]] are what the chain carries.

**Working definition used here:** values-based supply chains are regional wholesale chains of strategic partners that move differentiated products from small and mid-scale farms to buyers, while sharing information, fair prices and the values attached to the product *(Claude's synthesis of Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016; Bloom & Hinrichs, 2011)*.

## Features
**Components.** A VBSC has three components *(Claude's grouping, drawing on Bloom & Hinrichs, 2011)*: (1) a differentiated product whose values are communicated to buyers; (2) a set of strategic partners, typically a farmer cooperative, a processor or distributor, and a retailer or food service buyer; and (3) governance rules on price, information and decision-making. Farmers who best fit the model produce "significant volumes of high quality, differentiated food products" and "are willing to distribute profits among strategic partners" (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016, PDF p. 2). The way these components produce benefits is the next question.

**Mechanisms.** VBSCs aim to work through price and stability. "For farmers, earning a premium above commodity prices for the environmental and/or social benefits" of their practices is a central objective (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016, PDF p. 3). Shared information among partners, or transparency, helps them "improve productivity, enable rapid response to market changes and effectively engage customers" (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016, PDF p. 2). Collective organisation gives farms the scale they lack alone: "For farmers operating alone, the co-operative model is typically necessary to facilitate participation in VBSCs" (Hooks et al., 2017, PDF p. 2). Intermediaries such as food hubs do the aggregation; a hub "may be thought of as acting as an intermediary" between many suppliers and buyers (Morley et al., 2008, p. 2). Four US cases show that this requires acting collectively at three levels: "horizontally among producers, vertically within food value chains, and horizontally across food value chains" (Lev & Stevenson, 2011, p. 119). In return, farmers get prices based on their costs and "longer term contracts than standard for their products" (Lev & Stevenson, 2011, p. 121). These mechanisms depend on conditions.

**Conditions.** The chains rest on "strategic business partnerships based on trusting, transparent, and win-win relationships" (Stevenson et al., 2011, PDF p. 7), and they need capital, management, quality control and the ability to negotiate, including "Developing economic power for value chain negotiations" (Stevenson et al., 2011, PDF p. 6); see [[Trust in food chains]]. Infrastructure at the right scale is a recurring constraint. Feenstra and Hardesty (2016, PDF p. 3) call for more attention to how values are communicated along the chain and "how to develop appropriately scaled infrastructure for this mid-scale sector". Conventional distributors can carry local food, but they face difficulties "in pursuing equity outcomes for both producers and consumers" (Bloom & Hinrichs, 2011, p. 14). The infrastructure is social as well as physical. Stahlbrand (2015, p. 543) coined "infrastructure of the middle" for everything "from warehouses and processing plants to champions and nonprofit actors", and found a central role for "civil society organizations that act as catalysts, facilitators, and relationshipbuilders". Similarly, "investment in social infrastructure is crucial for maintaining the values and integrity of LFIs" (local food initiatives) as they scale up (Connelly & Beckie, 2016, p. 49). Power within the chain also matters: in an Irish beef case, the cooperative's autonomy "was challenged by VBSC chain members" (Hooks et al., 2017, PDF p. 1). These conditions shape how benefits are measured.

**Measurement.** Most evidence comes from case studies and surveys. Studies assess benefits for transparency, fair prices and ease of purchasing from small producers (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016), or use frameworks of farm viability, sustainability and resilience (Hooks et al., 2017). A national farmer survey compared VBSCs with other channels and found that "it was the larger farms that were more likely to perceive VBSC-specific benefits" (Peterson et al., 2022, p. 385). There are few quantitative studies of income effects.

**Debates.** The first debate is about who benefits. If larger farms gain most, VBSCs may not reach the small farms they were meant to support (Peterson et al., 2022). The second is about the role of the state. Stahlbrand (2017, p. 78) argues that public procurement and policy, not market differentiation alone, drive sustainable regional chains. The third is about scale: farmers see VBSCs as valuable but note "a limited volume of product that such regional supply chains can currently handle" (Peterson et al., 2022, p. 385). Scaling up means competing with a mainstream system "where price, efficiency, and convenience rule" (Connelly & Beckie, 2016, p. 49). What remains unresolved is whether VBSCs can grow without losing the values and partnerships that define them.

## Implications
- **United States: three chains in Northern California.** A specialty food manufacturer, a natural food cooperative and a regional food hub showed common benefits of transparency, fair prices and easier sourcing from small and mid-scale producers (Feenstra & Hardesty, 2016, PDF p. 1).
- **Ireland: a beef cooperative.** Integration into a VBSC improved "market orientation, price stability, and members’ capacities in responding to problems", but power struggles with chain partners cost members (Hooks et al., 2017, PDF p. 1).
- **United States: farmer views.** Farmers saw VBSCs as "a valuable marketing option that aligns with their own values and preserves their product’s identity", though larger farms gained most (Peterson et al., 2022, p. 385).
- **France and the United States: six chains compared.** Three French mid-tier chains and three US VBSCs (among them Organic Valley and Country Natural Beef) relied on products "differentiated by both sensory and credence characteristics" (Fleury et al., 2016, PDF p. 10). Premiums were fragile: in one French dairy cooperative, "providing even this small price premium remains a major challenge" (Fleury et al., 2016, PDF p. 8). See [[Credence attributes]].
- **Viet Nam: short chains with distributors.** A survey of 338 small farmers found chains with "two main actors: small farmers and distributors" that "helped to stabilize the input, output price, and revenue" and encouraged greener production (Bui et al., 2021, PDF p. 1). These chains are shorter than US VBSCs but share their logic of a values-carrying intermediary.
- **Canada and the UK: university procurement.** Public buyers drove local and sustainable purchasing, which led Stahlbrand (2017) to question market-centred definitions.

The cases show that VBSCs can stabilise prices and keep product identity for mid-scale farms, but only with organised farmers, intermediaries at the right scale and fair governance, and premiums stay fragile. The one Viet Nam study concerns short chains rather than VBSCs proper. The concept speaks to the [[Missing middle]]: in Viet Nam, cooperatives, collectors and safe-food enterprises between household farms and commodity chains could build values-based chains for regenerative or safe products.
