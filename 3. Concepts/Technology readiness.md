## Definitions
Technology readiness began as an engineering management tool. The technology readiness level (TRL) scale was *"developed at the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) in the 1970s"* (Olechowski et al., 2020, Abstract) as a standard way to judge how mature a technology is before it goes into a complex system. Mankins (2009) describes the early TRL as a measure that worked across disciplines and helped managers talk about maturity, and dates the first written definitions of each level, with examples, to 1995. The problem it answered was practical: programmes that relied on technologies that had not been proven ran into delays and cost overruns (GAO, 2020), so managers needed a common yardstick for how far along a technology was.

Because that yardstick was simple and portable, it travelled. The U.S. Government Accountability Office turned readiness assessment into a five-step audit procedure and lists further scales, such as manufacturing and system readiness levels, beside it (GAO, 2020). The European Union went further and made TRL a funding instrument. Héder (2017, PDF p. 1) traces how the scale became, *"through various mutations, an official innovation policy tool"* of the EU, and warns that without discipline-specific guidance TRLs would become *"a source of confusion and a subject of abuse"* in funding bids. A separate line of work, in services marketing, gave the same words a different meaning. Parasuraman (2000, Abstract) *"proposes the construct of technology readiness of people"*, and the revised Technology Readiness Index (TRI) measures *"people’s propensity to embrace and use cutting-edge technologies"* (Parasuraman & Colby, 2015, Abstract). Here the question moved from the object to the user. Agricultural research for development then joined the two lines, because research programmes struggled to take innovations to scale and needed more than a maturity score. Scaling Readiness, built in the CGIAR Roots, Tubers and Bananas programme, *"encourages critical reflection on how ready innovations are for scaling"* (Sartas et al., 2020, PDF p. 1). CGIAR's portfolio-wide Innovation Packages and Scaling Readiness (IPSR) system states that its readiness scale *"Builds on NASA technology readiness levels (TRL)"* (CGIAR, 2022, slide text). The editors of the Science of Scaling special issue place this work in a third wave of scaling thought, *"beyond technology adoption (first wave) and the scaling of innovation (second wave)"* (Schut et al., 2020, PDF p. 1).

In current use the term covers three related measures. *Technology or innovation readiness* is the position of a technology on an ordinal scale; in Scaling Readiness *"innovations evolve from an ‘idea’ (level 0) towards being ‘ready’ (level 9)"* through research, testing and validation in controlled and uncontrolled conditions (Sartas et al., 2020, PDF p. 5). *Innovation use* is a parallel nine-level scale of how widely the innovation is already used, and *"Scaling readiness then becomes a function of innovation readiness and innovation use"* (Sartas et al., 2020, PDF p. 3). *Technology readiness of people* is a trait with four dimensions, *"optimism, innovativeness, discomfort, and insecurity"* (Rusli et al., 2023, Abstract); the first two act as motivators and the last two as inhibitors of adoption intention (Omar et al., 2021).

Definitions split along discipline lines. Engineers treat readiness as a property of the technology that can be judged with little reference to who will use it (GAO, 2020; Olechowski et al., 2020). Marketing and adoption scholars treat it as a property of people (Parasuraman & Colby, 2015). Innovation-systems scholars insist it is relational, about how ready innovations are *"for scaling in a particular context"* (Schut et al., 2022, Abstract). The balanced readiness approach bridges the views by scoring TRL alongside *"Market Readiness Level (MRL), Regulatory Readiness Level (RRL), Acceptance Readiness Level (ARL)"* and organisational readiness (Vik et al., 2021, Abstract). Frija (2024, Highlights) goes furthest and calls for *"a paradigm shift from innovation-driven scaling to scaling activities that strengthen systems"*. The split follows purpose: a funder deciding whether to back a prototype needs a score for the object, while a programme trying to change practice needs to know about users and the wider system.

Technology readiness is not the same as [[Diffusion pathways|adoption or diffusion]]: TRI is an antecedent of adoption intention, not adoption itself (Omar et al., 2021). It is not [[Scaling]]: readiness and use are inputs to a scaling diagnosis (Sartas et al., 2020). It also differs from *appropriateness*, which asks whether a mature technology fits a place and crop (see [[Supply chain technologies (fruits and vegetables)]]). Finally, TRL (a maturity level of a technology) and TRI (an index of a person's readiness) are homonyms that are easy to mix up; even Sartas et al. (2020, PDF p. 5) describe their base scale as the *"technology readiness index developed by NASA"* while citing Parasuraman (2000), who built the people-side index *(Claude's observation, not from a source)*.

**Working definition used here:** technology readiness is the degree to which a technology is mature enough to work as intended (technology or innovation readiness), combined, where relevant, with the degree to which its intended users and the surrounding system are able and willing to use it (user and system readiness); scaling readiness adds evidence on current use *(Claude's synthesis of GAO, 2020; Parasuraman & Colby, 2015; Sartas et al., 2020; Vik et al., 2021)*.

## Features
**Structure**
- The engineering scale has nine levels, each tied to defined evidence, from basic principles to a system proven in operation (Mankins, 2009; GAO, 2020).
- CGIAR adds a level 0 (idea) and a matching 0–9 scale for innovation use, from no use to common use by beneficiaries (CGIAR, 2022, slide text; Sartas et al., 2020, PDF p. 6).
- The people-side index began as a 36-item scale and was cut to *"TRI 2.0, a 16-item scale"* (Parasuraman & Colby, 2015, Abstract).
- Composite tools stack several ladders: the balanced readiness assessment scores five readiness levels together (Vik et al., 2021), and Duong et al. (2023) group 18 readiness factors for blockchain into technological, inter-organisational, intra-organisational and environmental conditions.

**Logic**
- The core logic is stage-gating: a technology is funded, integrated or promoted only when evidence shows it has passed a level. GAO (2020) frames the assessment as a systematic, evidence-based check before a technology enters an acquisition programme.
- Scaling Readiness adds a weakest-link rule: the potential of a package at scale is set by *"the innovation in that package with the lowest innovation readiness and innovation use score"* (Sartas et al., 2020, PDF p. 6).
- Scores feed investment choices: packages that make no progress *"can be put on hold, under closer monitoring, or stopped"* (Sartas et al., 2020, PDF p. 9), and CGIAR stresses that moving from level 0 to 2 matters as much as moving from 7 to 9 (CGIAR, 2022, slide text).
- In the people-side logic, motivators raise and inhibitors lower the intention to adopt, with the motivators weighing more (Omar et al., 2021).

**Assumptions**
- Most scales assume that maturity grows in a roughly linear sequence. Gebreyes et al. (2021, Abstract) find instead that *"scaling is more complex than a linear rolling out of innovations towards diffusion"*.
- They assume that a level means the same thing across technologies. Héder (2017) shows that meanings drifted as the scale moved from space hardware to all EU research.
- They assume that readiness can be judged largely apart from context. Schut et al. (2022) stress that readiness only has meaning for a given context and goal, and Induli et al. (2026, PDF p. 1) find that *"strengths in one domain do not translate into broader system readiness"*.
- They assume honest evidence. Scaling Readiness therefore states that documented evidence *"are required to support claims of innovation readiness and innovation use levels"* and prefers independent assessors (Sartas et al., 2020, PDF p. 7).

**Operationalisation**
- Expert-led technology readiness assessments check evidence against level definitions, following GAO's five steps (GAO, 2020).
- Scaling Readiness workshops score a core innovation and its complementary innovations, as in the cassava flash dryer case of the RTB Scaling Fund (Schut et al., 2022).
- User surveys apply the TRI items; most Malaysian paddy farmers showed a moderate readiness level (Rusli et al., 2023), and in Sarawak both motivators and inhibitors predicted intention to use a finance app (Omar et al., 2021).
- Ex-ante user readiness frameworks score capabilities, opportunities and motivations, as with 690 Rwandan banana farmers (McCampbell et al., 2021).
- In supply chains, readiness can be scored by stage: in West Java, downstream fruit and vegetable actors were more ready for traceability technology than producers and storage operators (Rifani et al., 2026).

**Critiques**
- Practitioners interviewed by Olechowski et al. (2020) named 15 challenges in three groups (system complexity, planning and review, validity of assessment), and found that *"system complexity challenges are most critical to TRL users"* (Abstract).
- A high technical score can hide user gaps: McCampbell et al. (2021, Abstract) found *"a mismatch between expected UR and actual UR"*, and in Northern Ghana farmers named the lack of digital literacy as the main barrier to digital advisories, which Abdulai (2024) traces to the political economy of access.
- Readiness scores can be inflated by project support: Schut et al. (2020, PDF p. 5) argue that if adopters are paid by the project, the result *"would score low in terms of the ‘readiness’ of that innovations"* to scale.
- In postharvest horticulture, Hayuningtyas and Djatna (2018, Abstract) note that *"only a few technologies are applicable and useful to a wider community"* despite an abundant supply of technologies, and Pawera et al. (2024, Abstract) add that readiness has been *"largely overlooked in earlier frameworks on farmer participation"*.
- Unresolved: whether one composite score can serve both investors and implementers, or whether technology, user and system scores should be reported side by side.

## Implications
Evidence from Viet Nam is thin: one study builds a stakeholder readiness scale for blockchain in Hue's pork chain (Duong et al., 2023), and no Vietnamese study applying TRL or Scaling Readiness to fruit and vegetable technologies was found. Southeast Asian applications come first in the table.

| Study | Context | How the framework was used | Insight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rifani et al. (2026) | Fruit and vegetable supply chain, West Java, Indonesia | TRI plus technology acceptance, compared by chain stage for IoT and blockchain traceability | Downstream actors were more ready; *"discomfort and insecurity regarding technology remain significant challenges in the initial stages"* (Abstract) |
| Duong et al. (2023) | Pork supply chain, Hue, Viet Nam | A 62-item stakeholder readiness scale for blockchain, built on the TOE framework | Relative advantage, trust and trading-partner pressure mattered most |
| Hayuningtyas & Djatna (2018) | Horticultural postharvest technologies, Indonesia | TRL 1–9 ranking with scheduling of the time to full readiness | The top technology could pass level 7 but needed an optimistic 7.9 years to reach full readiness |
| Schut et al. (2022) | Cassava flash dryer, Africa and Central America | Scaling Readiness diagnosis within the RTB Scaling Fund | The approach shaped how the fund selected and supported scaling-ready innovations |
| McCampbell et al. (2021) | Phone-based advisory tools, Rwandan banana farmers | Ex-ante user readiness survey of 690 farmers | Recommends blending digital and analogue tools rather than assuming smartphone readiness |

The framework has been adapted in three directions: from technologies to all innovations, including policies and services (Sartas et al., 2020); from objects to users (Parasuraman & Colby, 2015; McCampbell et al., 2021); and from single scores to balanced or system scores (Vik et al., 2021; Frija, 2024). WorldVeg uses readiness and use levels to set how much farmers take part in vegetable projects, recommending *"increasing participation with higher levels of technology readiness and use"* (Pawera et al., 2024, Abstract). The limits seen in use are the ones named in the critiques: scores drift between fields, depend on who supplies the evidence, and miss users and systems unless these are scored separately.

**Practical guidance** *(Claude's synthesis, not from a source)*. When assessing supply chain technologies for fruits and vegetables, such as cold rooms, evaporative coolers or traceability apps, report at least three scores rather than one: technology readiness (does it work in this climate and at this scale?), user readiness by chain stage (are farmers, collectors, traders and retailers able and willing to use it?) and system readiness (finance, energy, regulation, buyer demand). Score them against documented evidence and repeat the scoring as a project advances. Where technology readiness is high but user or system readiness is low, the bottleneck lies in complementary innovations such as training, finance or business models, not in more engineering.
