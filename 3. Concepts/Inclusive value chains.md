## Definitions
The idea of inclusive value chains spread in the 2000s and 2010s, as development agencies looked to companies rather than aid to reach the poor. It grew from the base-of-the-pyramid argument that serving the poor could be profitable, which *"departed from the perspective that investments in corporate responsibility are largely recuperated indirectly"* (Schoneveld, 2020, PDF p. 2). In agriculture, the concern was that smallholders were being left out of fast-growing modern chains: buyers *"have been biased towards the reliability and consistency of large farmers and suppliers"* (Lundy et al., 2012, PDF p. 10).

Early definitions came from business and donor networks. Inclusive business was described as *"A profitable core business activity that also tangibly expands opportunities for the poor"* (Lundy et al., 2012, PDF p. 6). In academic work, inclusive business models are *"ways of doing business that benefit the poor as producers or consumers"* (van Westen et al., 2019, p. 64), and inclusive agribusiness integrates smallholders *"in commercial value chains"* so they gain markets, inputs and services *"in ways that are commercially viable"* (p. 64). Agricultural research centres linked the idea to innovation, stressing *"the importance of combining value-chain approaches with other approaches"*, including innovation systems and livelihoods frameworks (Devaux et al., 2018, p. 99).

As the term moved into policy, scholars noted that *"remarkably little literature sheds light on the operationalization of the concept"* (Ros-Tonen et al., 2019, p. 10). Reviewing inclusive business, inclusive value chain and inclusive development work, they identified economic, social, relational and environmental dimensions of inclusiveness. Schoneveld (2020, PDF p. 1) went further, arguing that *"inclusive businesses necessarily prioritize value creation over value capture"* and should be judged by the net value they create for income-constrained groups.

Definitions diverge on whose perspective counts. Business-led definitions emphasise viability for the firm; development definitions emphasise outcomes for the poor; critical definitions ask who sets the terms. Lead-firm models dominate practice, but recent work warns against *"excessive reliance on instrumental and lead firm-centric perspectives on value creation"* (Schoneveld & Weng, 2023, PDF p. 1).

Inclusive value chains need to be kept apart from their neighbours. [[Market linkages]] are the single relationships that inclusive chains depend on. [[Market systems development]] works on the wider system of rules and services. [[Cooperatives]] are one organisational route to inclusion but are not automatically inclusive themselves.

**Working definition used here:** an inclusive value chain integrates smallholders and other marginalised actors into commercial chains on terms that improve their incomes, voice, relationships and environment, not only their participation *(Claude's synthesis of Ros-Tonen et al., 2019; van Westen et al., 2019; Schoneveld, 2020)*.

## Features
**Components**
- Inclusive chains combine a business model that is viable for the buyer, an organisation of producers that can meet buyer needs, and services such as finance, training and certification *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*.
- The LINK methodology, developed by CIAT, maps these elements and treats inclusive trading relationships as *"the result of business models that do not leave behind small holder farmers"* (Lundy et al., 2012, PDF p. 6). These components work through several mechanisms.

**Mechanisms**
- Collective action, contract farming, intermediaries and supportive public policy carry inclusion. Karatepe and Scherrer (2024, p. 317) find *"the importance of collective action by key stakeholders, including smallholders and a supportive state"*.
- Danse et al. (2020, PDF p. 1) find *"a need for intermediaries to overcome cultural and institutional gaps"*.
- In Viet Nam, cooperatives act as the *"consolidated bodies of farmers"* that manage supermarket requirements on behalf of members (Sakata & Takanashi, 2022, p. 57). These mechanisms meet barriers.

**Conditions**
- Smallholders *"often struggle to connect with actors in the middle of the food supply chain"* due to limited land, inputs and capacity (Vos & Cattaneo, 2020, p. 14).
- Low participation is *"the result of constrained choice in a risky environment"*, not a lack of commercial interest (Arias et al., 2013, PDF p. 6).
- Organisations can also exclude: *"When cooperatives evolve from communityorientation towards market-orientation, they are likely to become less inclusive"* (Bijman & Wijers, 2019, p. 74).
- Standards raise the bar: in Viet Nam, suppliers to supermarkets must *"at the very least, acquire Vietnam Good Agricultural Practice (VietGAP) certification"* (Sakata & Takanashi, 2022, p. 57). These conditions shape how inclusion is measured.

**Measurement**
- Studies measure inclusion through participation rates, income and price effects for those who take part, and sometimes through voice in governance.
- Ros-Tonen et al. (2019) propose economic, social, relational and environmental dimensions.
- A common gap is that studies look only at participating farmers and miss others in the community (van Westen et al., 2019).
- Value network approaches try to trace how *"different types of value are created and captured"* across chain and non-chain actors (Schoneveld & Weng, 2023, PDF p. 1). Measurement gaps fuel the debates.

**Debates**
- Critical work questions whether inclusion is realistic: German et al. (2020, PDF p. 1) find *"the overall trend is towards more exclusive agribusiness"* as standards rise and state support falls.
- A second debate is about certification: its effects on well-being are *"positive for some farmers under certain conditions"* but *"insignificant or adverse for others"* (Oberlack et al., 2023, PDF p. 1), and inclusive business strategies may address some barriers but *"come with their own challenges and limitations"* (Oberlack et al., 2023, PDF p. 1).
- What remains unresolved is whether firms can be expected to include the poorest farmers without public support and strong producer organisations.

## Implications
- **Viet Nam: supermarket vegetable chains.** As supermarkets expanded, cooperatives organised production, packing and delivery to meet VietGAP and buyer requirements; the authors conclude that *"the government has a significant role in supporting cooperatives’ capacity building"* (Sakata & Takanashi, 2022, p. 57).
- **Producer cooperatives as inclusive business.** A review finds that open membership and representative governance favour inclusion, while a shift to market orientation tends to narrow it (Bijman & Wijers, 2019, p. 74).
- **Peru–Switzerland cacao chains.** Actors combined certification, inclusive business and solidarity economy instruments into portfolios that reflect their role in the chain (Oberlack et al., 2023, PDF p. 1).
- **CIAT's LINK methodology.** A participatory tool to design business models that link smallholders to markets, used across Latin America, Africa and Asia (Lundy et al., 2012).

Together, the cases show that inclusion depends on organised producers, buyer commitment and public support, and that it can erode as chains become more demanding. For IP1+, the concept frames how value chains for agroecological and regenerative products in Laos and Cambodia can include women and poorer farmers (see [[GEDSI]]) rather than only the best-resourced ones, and points to cooperatives and intermediaries as the places where inclusion is won or lost.
