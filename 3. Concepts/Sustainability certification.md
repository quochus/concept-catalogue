## Definitions
Sustainability certification grew out of the organic and fair trade movements. From the 1980s these movements sought to "re-embed" production in "natural processes" and trade in "equitable social relations" (Raynolds, 2000, p. 297). In practice they became labelling projects: products that meet a set of production or trade criteria "can be labeled as organic and/or fair trade", "with compliance verified by a third party" (Raynolds, 2000, p. 299). The problem they answered was that buyers far from the farm could not see how food was produced, so a market for sustainable practices needed a trusted signal.

In the 1990s and 2000s the model spread and changed. Food scares and the rise of retailer standards moved responsibility for checking food claims to private bodies, so that third-party certifiers became central to how the global food system is governed (Hatanaka & Busch, 2008, p. 73). Standards multiplied beyond organic and fair trade to Rainforest Alliance, UTZ, GlobalGAP and many others, and governments, NGOs and donors began to promote them as development tools (Meemken et al., 2021, p. 760). National public standards followed, such as VietGAP, which Viet Nam adopted in 2008 (Pham, 2017, PDF p. 1).

The current core definition is broad. Sustainability standards are "rules that supply chain actors may follow to demonstrate their commitment to social equity and/or environmental protection" (Meemken et al., 2021, p. 758). They have three parts: a standard (the rules), certification (checking compliance, usually by an independent auditor) and a label or claim that carries the result to buyers. "Supplier adoption of sustainability standards is generally voluntary, with compliance verified through certification and regular monitoring" (Meemken et al., 2021, p. 758). Producers are rewarded through "premiums and other benefits" for following the criteria (DeFries et al., 2017, PDF p. 2).

Definitions diverge on what certification is. Economists treat it as a technical fix for information asymmetry: a way to make [[Credence attributes]] visible. Sociologists argue that third-party certification "is not merely an objective or impartial technical tool" but something that "reorganizes, transforms and disciplines people and things" along the chain (Hatanaka & Busch, 2008, p. 74). The first view asks whether certification works; the second asks who it works for and who sets the rules.

Certification also needs to be kept apart from its neighbours. [[Participatory guarantee systems]] are an alternative form of assurance based on peer review rather than independent audit. Public standards such as VietGAP are set by the state, while most sustainability standards are private. [[Trust in food chains]] is what certification tries to create; a label without trust in the certifier carries little value.

**Working definition used here:** sustainability certification is the use of voluntary standards for social and environmental production practices, with compliance checked by an independent body and signalled to buyers through a label or claim, so that buyers can reward practices they cannot observe *(Claude's synthesis of Meemken et al., 2021; Raynolds, 2000; Hatanaka & Busch, 2008)*.

## Features
**Components.** A certification system has four parts *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: (1) a standard-setting body; (2) the standard itself, covering environment, labour and sometimes trade relations; (3) an assurance system of audits, internal controls or group certification; and (4) a market interface, usually a label or a business-to-business claim. Standards differ in what they cover: most "do not specify farmer output prices", while Fairtrade does (Meemken et al., 2021, p. 759). How these parts are meant to produce change is the next question.

**Mechanisms.** The intended mechanism is a price signal. Consumers pay more for labelled products, retailers source them, and producers adopt practices to earn premiums. On the demand side there is some support: ecolabels "can increase the selection and purchase of more environmentally sustainable food" (Bastounis et al., 2021, PDF p. 2). On the supply side, certification can raise prices: certified farmers gain "positive effects on prices and income from the sale of produce" (Oya et al., 2018, p. 282). But a second mechanism works against the first. Producers who already meet a standard have "strong incentives to join", so programmes that attract them have "a limited additional effect on producer behavior" (Blackman & Rivera, 2011, p. 1177). Whether either mechanism works depends on conditions.

**Conditions.** Certification costs time, money and paperwork, so size and support matter. "Many smallholder farmers cannot meet certification requirements without financial and/or technical assistance and group-based certification" (Meemken et al., 2021, p. 759). In Thailand, support from exporters and farm scale were "the most important factors influencing the renewal of the GlobalGAP certificate" (Holzapfel & Wollni, 2014, p. 731). Market structure matters too: in Peru, certification "leads to vertical integration" and reduces sourcing from small producers (Schuster & Maertens, 2013, p. 291). Because effects vary so much with context, measuring them is hard.

**Measurement.** The main problem is selection: certified farms differ from others before they are certified. Rigorous studies construct "a credible counterfactual outcome" through randomisation or quasi-experimental methods (Blackman & Rivera, 2011, p. 1177), but they are few. On the demand side, discrete choice experiments estimate the marginal willingness to pay for a label (Bastounis et al., 2021). Reviews of food security effects find that outcomes beyond income remain "a blind spot in the literature on certiﬁcation impacts" (Schleifer & Sun, 2020, PDF p. 1). The thin evidence base shapes the debates.

**Debates.** The first debate is about impact. The largest systematic review found "limited and mixed evidence" on socio-economic effects, with "no evidence that total household income improves with certification" (Oya et al., 2018, p. 282). Meemken et al. (2021, p. 758) conclude that standards are "insufficient to ensure food system sustainability at scale". The second is about exclusion: high-level production standards especially reduce sourcing from small-scale producers (Schuster & Maertens, 2013, p. 291). The third is about legitimacy: standard-setting tends to be dominated by "multinational businesses, NGOs and international experts" (Meemken et al., 2021, p. 759), yet more inclusive governance did not explain whether southern producers accept a standard (Schouten et al., 2022, PDF p. 1). What remains unresolved is whether certification should be judged as a market tool, a development tool or a form of private regulation.

## Implications
- **Viet Nam: VietGAP as public certification.** VietGAP is "one of the important policy tools" for moving from a traditional to a modern agri-food system, but "less than 5% of farmers" have adopted it (Hoang, 2020, p. 3641). Uptake requires "changes to the socio-technical regime of the traditional agri-food system" (Hoang, 2020, p. 3641), not only farmer training. In Hanoi, despite subsidies, "farmers don’t seem having much incentives to apply standards" (Pham, 2017, PDF p. 2).
- **Viet Nam: small shrimp producers.** In central Viet Nam, aquaculture standards were found "not appropriate for small producers", whose farms make up most of the sector, and a separate national standard for small producers was proposed (Marschke & Wilkings, 2014, p. 197).
- **Thailand: GlobalGAP for smallholders.** Smaller farmers and those in exporter-managed groups "do not experience comparable income increases", and many did not renew their certificates (Holzapfel & Wollni, 2014, p. 731).
- **Peru: exclusion through integration.** Certified asparagus exporters integrated vertically and bought less from outside producers, "with a larger effect for small-scale producers" (Schuster & Maertens, 2013, p. 291).

Across the cases, certification works best for larger, organised and export-linked producers and least for the smallholders it often claims to serve. For regenerative or agroecological products in Laos and Cambodia, this points to group certification, [[Participatory guarantee systems]] or buyer-backed schemes rather than individual third-party audits, and to building [[Trust in food chains]] at the same time.
