## My first take
*Written by Quoc before reading the draft (2026-10-21). Never edited later.*

Scaling agroecology - Agroecology requires scaling - not only by expanding and replicating agroecological production, but also by changing norms, cognition, and institutions that foster systems change.

## Definitions
The language of scaling came to agroecology from social innovation. Moore et al. (2015, p. 75, Fig. 1) distinguished three strategies that non-profits use to change systems: scaling out (reaching more people and places), scaling up (changing institutions, policy and law) and scaling deep, "Changing relationships, cultural values and beliefs". Their argument was that durable change needs to reach people's "hearts and minds, their values and cultural practices" (Moore et al., 2015, p. 74), not only their numbers.

Agroecology adopted and adapted this vocabulary because its own successes were scattered. Islands of good practice had not changed regional or national food systems, so movements and researchers asked how they could. Mier y Terán et al. (2018, p. 639) gave the agroecological definition: scaling is a process that "leads ever-greater numbers of families to practice agroecology over ever-larger territories" and engages more people in processing, distribution and eating agroecological food. Because this is a social process as much as a technical one, the same authors listed eight drivers, beginning with the "recognition of a crisis that motivates the search for alternatives" and ending with favourable markets and policies (Mier y Terán et al., 2018, p. 637). Sustainability science, in turn, refined the typology: Lam et al. (2020, PDF p. 1) identify eight amplification processes, "stabilizing, speeding up, growing, replicating, transferring, spreading, scaling up, and scaling deep".

The core idea today is that agroecology spreads along three axes at once: to more farmers and territories, into institutions and policy, and into values and relationships. Organisation is the medium for all three: "organization and social fabric are the growth media" on which agroecology advances (Mier y Terán et al., 2018, p. 637).

Definitions diverge on what is being scaled. Nicholls and Altieri (2018) prefer "amplification" and stress the technical side: they note that "Most research analyzing factors needed for scaling up agroecology focuses on the social" and policy dimensions, while a key challenge "lies in the translation of agroecological principles" into soil, water and biodiversity strategies (PDF p. 2). Movement scholars stress the political side, and transition researchers find the term itself unclear (see Debates).

Scaling agroecology is related to, but narrower than, [[Scaling]] in general and the field of [[Scaling science]], which deal with any innovation. It overlaps with [[Diffusion pathways]], which describe how a practice spreads, but adds institutional and cultural change.

**Working definition used here:** scaling agroecology is the process by which agroecological practices, knowledge and food spread to more people and territories (out), become embedded in institutions and policy (up), and reshape the values and relationships that sustain them (deep) *(Claude's synthesis of Moore et al., 2015; Mier y Terán et al., 2018)*.

## Features
**Components.** The three axes (out, up, deep) are the structure; the eight drivers of Mier y Terán et al. (2018, p. 637) are the content: crisis, social organisation, constructivist learning, effective practices, mobilising discourses, external allies, favourable markets and favourable policies. These components interact, which is the mechanism.

**Mechanisms.** Scaling advances through reinforcing loops: as it proceeds, "positive feedback and virtuous synergisms arise among drivers" (Mier y Terán et al., 2018, p. 644). Learning is the engine. Scaling up "depends heavily on human capital enhancement and community empowerment" (Altieri et al., 2012, p. 16), and networks carry the learning: "Multi-actor networks are pivotal in strengthening community self-organization" (Anderson et al., 2019, PDF p. 12). See [[Connectivity]]. These loops need conditions to start and to last.

**Conditions.** Markets and infrastructure alone are not enough. Local food initiatives that grow only their physical infrastructure lose their purpose; "investment in social infrastructure is crucial for maintaining the values" behind them (Connelly & Beckie, 2016, p. 49). Economics matter too: in Cambodia, 90% of rice farmers using cover crops "identified economic and climatic constraints as barriers to scaling-up" (À Hauariki et al., 2026, p. 1). These conditions make scaling hard to measure.

**Measurement.** Scaling out is usually counted in farmers, hectares or territories; scaling up in laws, programmes and budgets; scaling deep is rarely measured. Lam et al.'s (2020) eight processes give a checklist for tracing which kind of amplification an initiative achieves *(Claude's suggestion for use)*. The lack of agreed measures feeds the debates.

**Debates.** Two debates stand out. First, the concept is unclear: "upscaling itself remains a fuzzy concept", partly because of "the different understanding of the concept by researchers and practitioners" (Augenstein et al., 2020, p. 143). Second, scaling through the state or mainstream markets carries risks: social movements "face risks when they allow themselves to become absorbed" in state collaborations (Giraldo & McCune, 2019, p. 786). What remains unresolved is whether agroecology can be scaled up through the state and markets without losing the social principles that are meant to be scaled deep.

## Implications
- **Emblematic cases in Latin America and India.** Mier y Terán et al. (2018, p. 639) selected five cases for their territorial reach and "the thousands of families involved": the farmer-to-farmer movement in Central America, the national peasant agroecology movement in Cuba, organic coffee in Chiapas, Zero Budget Natural Farming in Karnataka, and Brazil's Rede Ecovida network. In each, organisation and learning came before policy.
- **City region (London).** Each pathway needed something different: land and learning platforms for scaling out, policy change for scaling up, and for scaling deep, "investment both in transformative learning opportunities" and networks (Nicol, 2020, PDF p. 1).
- **Laos.** Successive conservation agriculture projects built different actor networks; the lesson is that "a pluralistic approach is desirable to promote innovation" (Castella et al., 2022, p. 14).
- **Cambodia.** A domestic cover-crop seed chain scaled seed area quickly, but farmers' economic and climate constraints still limit uptake; stakeholders point to "practice and results-based reward mechanisms" as a way forward (À Hauariki et al., 2026, p. 1).

Across the cases, scaling out followed organisation and learning, scaling up depended on political openings that could close again, and scaling deep was named but seldom tracked. For Quoc's work in Laos, Cambodia and Viet Nam, the Mekong cases show scaling out through projects and networks, with scaling up still the weak link.

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** Your "expanding and replicating agroecological production" is scaling out; "changing norms, cognition and institutions" combines scaling up (institutions) and scaling deep (norms, values, cognition), as in Moore et al. (2015, p. 75) and Lam et al. (2020, PDF p. 1).
- **Extends.** The agroecology literature puts social organisation and learning networks at the centre of scaling and adds markets and policy as drivers (Mier y Terán et al., 2018, p. 637).
- **Challenges.** Scaling is not neutral: scaling through the state can absorb and weaken movements (Giraldo & McCune, 2019, p. 786), and the term itself is understood differently by researchers and practitioners (Augenstein et al., 2020, p. 143).
