## Definitions
The market systems idea grew out of development practice in the 2000s, when donors found that delivering services directly to poor producers rarely lasted. The starting point was that "the poor are dependent on market systems for their livelihoods" (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 5), so changing those systems should reduce poverty.

The standard definition comes from the Making Markets Work for the Poor (M4P) literature. A market system is "the multi-player, multi-function arrangement" of three sets of functions, core, rules and supporting, carried out by private firms, government, representative organisations and civil society "through which exchange takes place, develops, adapts and grows" (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 7). The core is the exchange of goods and services; rules include formal laws, standards and informal norms; supporting functions include information, skills, research and coordination.

Market systems differ from value chains. A value chain follows a product from producer to consumer; a market system adds the rules and services around each exchange. They also differ from market linkages, which concern one relationship within the system (see [[Market linkages]]).

**Working definition used here:** a market system is the set of exchanges, rules and supporting services, and the actors who perform them, that together determine how a market functions and whom it serves *(Claude's synthesis of The Springfield Centre, 2008)*.

## Features
**Components.** Core exchange, rules and supporting functions (The Springfield Centre, 2008). "Informal rules are generally a product of local culture and value systems" and shape whether formal rules are accepted (PDF p. 37).

**Performance and imperfections.** Developing-country markets show "pervasive imperfections" (Markelova et al., 2009, p. 1), such as high transaction costs, weak information and poor coordination. Groups, contracts and hybrid arrangements fill these gaps; Cadilhon et al. (2009) found several hybrid forms of organisation in Vietnamese vegetable chains.

**Systems view.** The approach tries to "connect economic, institutional and social dimensions in a joined up, systemic way" (Albu, 2008, PDF p. 10). It shares this aim with livelihoods approaches but starts from markets rather than households.

## Implications
Seeing a market as a system changes where projects intervene: often in rules and supporting functions rather than in direct delivery to farmers. This is the logic of [[Market systems development]]. For IP1+, mapping the market system for agroecological or regenerative products in Laos and Cambodia would show which rules (standards, certification) and services (information, finance) limit uptake.
