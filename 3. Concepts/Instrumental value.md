## Definitions
The distinction between valuing something as an end and valuing it as a means is old in philosophy, but it became a working tool in environmental policy when valuation of ecosystem services spread in the 1990s and 2000s. Early science–policy documents set intrinsic and instrumental value against each other: "something may either have dignity—intrinsic value—or a price—instrumental value" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 36). The problem this framing answered was practical. To compare a wetland with a shrimp farm, or a forest with a plantation, decision-makers needed values in a common unit, and instrumental value supplied it.

Instrumental values "represent the value of ecosystems as merely means to an end" and are "often measured in monetary terms" (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 1). A systematic review for the IPBES values assessment proposed a core meaning: "values of other-than-human entities, as means to achieve human ends or satisfy human preferences" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 30). The definition includes economic values "regardless of whether the entity is directly or indirectly used or not used" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 30), so existence and bequest values count as instrumental when they are justified by human satisfaction.

Two features define the category. It is anthropocentric: instrumental values are "strongly and explicitly associated with ecosystem services and anthropocentric worldviews" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 31). And it allows substitution: because the value lies in the end served, "the means might be substitutable" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 31). In management and economics the parallel idea is use value, the quality of a product "as perceived by users in relation to their needs" (Lepak et al., 2007, p. 181), which a market then turns into exchange value.

Definitions diverge on where the boundary lies. Some authors class any human-centred value as instrumental; critics reply that this has made relational values look instrumental, because "relational values have been conflated with instrumental values because of their anthropocentric nature" (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 9). The difference follows purpose: economic valuation needs a broad instrumental category to aggregate, while plural-values research needs a narrow one to see what money leaves out. In consumer psychology the same words name a different idea, instrumental versus terminal personal values; that sense is not covered here *(Claude's note; source not yet in Zotero)*.

Instrumental value should be kept apart from its neighbours. [[Intrinsic value]] is worth independent of human use. [[Relational values]] are worth that lies in meaningful relationships and is not reducible to a means. [[Willingness to pay]] is one way of measuring instrumental value, not the value itself.

**Working definition used here:** instrumental value is the worth of a product, practice or part of nature as a means to human ends, such as income, food, health, risk reduction or climate regulation; it is the value type that prices, premiums and cost–benefit analysis can capture, and in principle it can be substituted by other means to the same end *(based on Himes et al., 2024; Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017)*.

## Features
**Components.** Instrumental value has a means (a product, practice or ecosystem), an end (a human need or preference) and a valuer whose preferences count. In agri-food chains the ends include farm income, safety, nutrition and environmental services; zero-deforestation targets, for example, "recognize the value of nature in instrumental terms of carbon sequestration" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 4). How these values are combined is the mechanism.

**Mechanisms.** Because instrumental values are substitutable, they can be compared and added up. They "support high comparability and commensurability, which facilitates trade-off assessments" expressed in money (Himes et al., 2024, p. 36), and they "can be aggregated via the economic equivalent numbers involved" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 4). If a willingness to pay or accept can be estimated, "then it is possible to establish the proper monetary measures of the utility effects" (Parks & Gowdy, 2013, PDF p. 4). In food markets the same logic appears as a price premium: "Economic value of market transactions involving certified products denotes instrumental values" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 2). The mechanism depends on conditions.

**Conditions.** Instrumental framing works best where a market or a clear human benefit exists. It fits poorly where values are moral or spiritual: non-use values are hard to price because they relate to properties "for which markets usually do not exist" (TEEB 2010, quoted in Himes et al., 2024, p. 34). Who is asked also matters. In a Colombian watershed, "Instrumental values were only mentioned by 2.2% of respondents" (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 6), and rural respondents "were less likely to express instrumental values" (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 7) than urban ones. These conditions shape how instrumental value is measured.

**Measurement.** Instrumental value is measured in money or physical units: market prices, stated preference methods such as contingent valuation and [[Discrete choice experiments]], revealed preference, and cost–benefit analysis. For sustainable food, meta-analyses report an average sustainability premium of 29.5% (Li & Kallas, 2021, PDF p. 1), and auctions in Viet Nam found consumers "willing to pay a 9% price premium for certified sustainably-produced rice" (My et al., 2018, p. 283). Use value itself is harder to measure, because such judgements "are subjective and individual specific" (Lepak et al., 2007, p. 181). Measurement choices are at the centre of the debates.

**Debates.** The first debate is about dominance. In sustainability discourse "the language of instrumental value is increasingly dominant" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 36), and "Focusing only on monetary valuation emphasizes instrumental values while ignoring intrinsic and relational values" (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 1). The second is about hidden ethics: even inside economic valuation, the discount rate is "an ethical one not an economic choice" (Parks & Gowdy, 2013, PDF p. 7). The third is about certification: its assessment "often leans toward an instrumental perspective" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 2), although price premiums "are the most contested in the literature" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 3). What remains unresolved is how to use instrumental values for decisions without letting them crowd out the values that cannot be added up.

## Implications
- **Certified commodities.** Certification is usually judged by instrumental results, such as premiums, yields and market access, while its relational and intrinsic aims receive less attention (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 2).
- **Viet Nam: sustainable rice.** The 9% premium for certified rice is an instrumental value for consumers, and it rose with more information (My et al., 2018, p. 283).
- **Colombia: plural values in a watershed.** Residents expressed relational and intrinsic values far more often than instrumental ones (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 6), so a purely monetary valuation would have missed most of what they said.
- **Spain: agroecological initiatives.** Women farmers earn a living from their farms, an instrumental value, but "We found that instrumental and relational values often blended" (Vizuete et al., 2025, p. 684).

Together the cases show that instrumental value is real and measurable, and is what markets for sustainable food mainly reward, but it is rarely the whole story. Food in particular "may have instrumental and relational value, depending on the context" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 32). For regenerative or agroecological products in Cambodia, Laos or Viet Nam, the practical question is which instrumental values (safety, income, soil health) buyers can see and pay for, and how to avoid designing incentives that reward those alone.
