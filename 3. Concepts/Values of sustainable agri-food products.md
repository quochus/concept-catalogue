## My first take
*Written by Quoc before reading the draft (2026-10-21). Never edited later.*

Values of sustainable agri-food products - agri-food products could be connected many values: economic, social, environmental or health, and others. Values could be measurable or non-measurable.

## Definitions
"Value" entered this topic from economics and strategy, where it is a property of goods and exchanges. Lepak et al. (2007, p. 180) "define value creation in terms of use value and exchange value": what a product is worth to its user, and what it fetches in a market. Value capture is a separate question, about who appropriates the value created. The problem this framing answers is distributive: along a food chain, value can be created at the farm and captured by someone else.

Two other traditions widened the meaning, each because the economic framing left something out. Food studies argued that a product's worth depends on what it tells the buyer: a product "embedded with information" about place and production can "command a premium price" (Marsden et al., 2000, p. 425). This led to [[Values-based supply chains]], partnerships with "shared ethics or values related to the qualities of the food" and to the business relationships along the chain (Peterson et al., 2022, p. 385). Environmental valuation, meanwhile, built a typology of the values of nature that now applies to food as well.

That typology has three types. [[Instrumental value]] is the value of something "as merely means to an end", "often measured in monetary terms" (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 1). [[Intrinsic value]] is the value of entities "worth protecting as ends in themselves" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 29), independent of their use to people. [[Relational values]] lie in meaningful relationships and are "context dependent, often place-based, nontradable, and therefore largely not substitutable" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 31). Food sits across the types: it "may have instrumental and relational value, depending on the context" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 32). Certification shows the mapping in practice: price premiums from certified trade express instrumental value, the social commitments of the actors express relational value, and global goals for biodiversity and the commons express intrinsic value (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 2).

The result is that "value" is now used in three senses that are often mixed up *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: value created and captured (economics); values attributed to food and nature (instrumental, intrinsic and relational values); and values as principles that guide how chains are organised (values-based chains). For sustainable agri-food products the three meet. [[Credence attributes]] such as "sustainable", "safe" or "agroecological" create economic value only when they are signalled and trusted, and what is signalled reflects the values of producers and buyers. See [[Trust in food chains]] and [[Connectivity]].

The traditions diverge on whether value can be priced. Economics measures it, usually through willingness to pay; plural-values scholars hold that intrinsic and relational values cannot be traded or substituted, so a price will always miss part of them. The difference is one of purpose: market design needs a number, while transition research needs to understand motives.

The concept should be kept apart from its tools and forms: [[Willingness to pay]] is one way of measuring it, [[Sustainability certification]] is one way of signalling it, [[Incentives]] and [[Public procurement]] are ways of rewarding it, and [[Values-based supply chains]] are one way of organising around it.

**Working definition used here:** the values of sustainable agri-food products are the economic, social, environmental, health and relational worth that producers, buyers and consumers attach to how food is produced and traded, including how that worth is created, signalled, captured, and the parts that markets do not price *(Claude's synthesis of Lepak et al., 2007; Marsden et al., 2000; Himes et al., 2024)*.

## Features
**Components.** The values attached to sustainable products fall into economic (price, income, risk), health (safety, nutrition), environmental (soil, biodiversity, climate), social (fairness, relationships) and relational (care, identity, meaning) groups *(Claude's grouping, building on Himes et al., 2024)*. Most economic, health and many environmental values are instrumental; care, identity and fairness are mostly relational; and biodiversity protected for its own sake is intrinsic; zero-deforestation targets, for example, value forests for carbon "but also in relational and intrinsic terms of biodiversity to be protected" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 4). Only instrumental values aggregate easily: they "can be aggregated via the economic equivalent numbers involved", whereas relational values "can only be aggregated via the voices expressing them" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 4). How the non-priced values become economic value is the mechanism.

**Mechanisms.** The mechanism is signal plus trust. Because buyers cannot check how food was produced, sustainability is a credence quality: "consumers cannot determine the product’s quality even after they buy and consume it" (Caswell & Mojduszka, 1996, PDF p. 5). In such markets trust needs "the involvement of a credible third party, often the government" (Le et al., 2020, PDF p. 2). Where the signal is credible, consumers pay: a meta-analysis finds that "the overall WTP premium for sustainability (in percentage terms) is 29.5% on average" (Li & Kallas, 2021, PDF p. 1). For traceability the premium reaches "around 32%", and "Consumers in developing countries show a higher WTP for traceable food" (Tran et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). Embedded information about place and production adds to the premium (Marsden et al., 2000, p. 425; see [[Connectivity]]). On the supply side, the same values are rewarded through [[Incentives]]: market-based instruments work "by providing economic incentives through market signals" (Piñeiro et al., 2020, p. 810). Whether the mechanism works depends on conditions.

**Conditions.** Four conditions stand out. First, the label alone is weak. Across developing-country cases, buyers sought health and taste, and "there is not a clear demand for ‘agro-ecological’ products per se" (Loconto et al., 2018, p. 2). Second, information must be layered: in Viet Nam the premium for sustainable rice rose as more information was added (My et al., 2018, p. 283; see Implications). Third, stated values do not always become purchases. For sustainable food, "consumer attitudes are mainly positive" but "behavioral patterns are not univocally consistent with attitudes" (Vermeir & Verbeke, 2006, p. 169); see [[Intention–behaviour gap]]. Fourth, the point of choice matters: [[Nudges]] that change placement or convenience work better than those that only inform, because "Effect sizes increase as the focus of the nudges shifts from cognition" to behaviour (Cadario & Chandon, 2020, PDF p. 2). These conditions shape how value is measured.

**Measurement.** Economic value is measured with willingness-to-pay methods: [[Discrete choice experiments]], contingent valuation and experimental auctions (My et al., 2018). Non-market and relational values are studied qualitatively: producers in women-led agroecological initiatives "frequently mentioned the relational values of meaningful occupation, care of nature" and social relations (Vizuete et al., 2025, p. 684). Asking open questions changes the picture: in a Colombian watershed, "Instrumental values were only mentioned by 2.2% of respondents" (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 6), far fewer than relational or intrinsic ones. The two kinds of measurement rarely meet in one study, which is part of the debate.

**Debates.** The first debate is about markets. Values-based chains pay farmers premiums for products differentiated "by such values as quality", environment, place or relationships (Peterson et al., 2022, p. 386), but critics say they "focus too heavily on market-based differentiation" and so serve consumers who can pay (Stahlbrand, 2017, p. 77). The second is about which values certification rewards. Its assessment "often leans toward an instrumental perspective" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 2), and even on instrumental terms the evidence is mixed: a systematic review found "no evidence that total household income improves with certification" (Oya et al., 2018, p. 282). The third is about leverage: values "may act as leverage points for sustainability transformation" (Horcea-Milcu, 2022, PDF p. 1), and "Focusing only on monetary valuation emphasizes instrumental values while ignoring intrinsic and relational values" (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 1). What remains unresolved is how to reward the values that markets do not price without reducing them to a premium.

## Implications
- **Viet Nam: sustainable rice.** In experimental auctions, domestic consumers were "willing to pay a 9% price premium for certified sustainably-produced rice", and the premium "increases up to 33% when incremental levels of information" on certification and traceability are added (My et al., 2018, p. 283).
- **Viet Nam: the value of a trusted buyer.** Vegetable farmers in northern Viet Nam would "request a 21% premium to accept selling to an unknown buyer" (Enthoven & Van den Broeck, 2021, PDF p. 1): a relationship carries economic value for producers too.
- **Viet Nam: organic meat.** Consumers' "positive attitude did not necessarily translate into their actual purchase of organic meat", but stores' green marketing raised purchases (Nguyen et al., 2019, PDF p. 1).
- **United States: values-based supply chains.** A nationwide farmer survey found that farmers in such chains receive premiums "by such values as quality", environment, place and social relationships (Peterson et al., 2022, p. 386).
- **Spain: women-led agroecological initiatives.** Producers in the Madrid region valued their work for meaningful occupation, care of nature and social relations, values that no price captures (Vizuete et al., 2025, p. 684), and "We found that instrumental and relational values often blended" (Vizuete et al., 2025, p. 684).
- **Brazil: public procurement as a buyer of values.** The PAA used "price premiums and targeted agricultural extension programmes for agro-ecological" production (Wittman & Blesh, 2017, p. 86). A public buyer can reward environmental and social values directly, and such structured demand makes market engagement "easier, less costly and less risky" for smallholders (Sumberg & Sabates-Wheeler, 2011, p. 342).

Together the cases show two sides of value. Where information and trust are in place, consumers in Viet Nam and buyers in the United States pay measurable premiums for instrumental values such as safety and quality. Producers, though, value much more than price, and the relational and intrinsic values they hold rarely reach the market unless a buyer, often a public one, chooses to reward them. In Cambodia and Laos, where Quoc's IP1+ work sits, the question is which of these values local buyers will recognise and reward, and which need a public or institutional buyer instead.

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** You list economic, social, environmental and health values and note that some can be measured and some cannot. The literature makes the same split between priced value (Li & Kallas, 2021, PDF p. 1) and relational values that are "largely not substitutable" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 31).
- **Extends: measurable vs non-measurable.** Your split maps onto the value types: instrumental values "can be aggregated via the economic equivalent numbers involved", while relational values "can only be aggregated via the voices expressing them" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 4). Intrinsic values, held for their own sake, are the third type and the hardest to price.
- **Extends.** The literature separates three senses of value (created and captured, attributed, guiding) and shows that sustainability creates economic value only through signals and trust (Marsden et al., 2000, p. 425; Tran et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).
- **Challenges.** Demand for the label itself can be weak (Loconto et al., 2018, p. 2), and critics question whether market premiums are the right way to reward these values at all (Stahlbrand, 2017, p. 77).
