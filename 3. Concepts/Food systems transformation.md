## Definitions

"Transformation" was applied to food systems in two separate traditions before the two met. In development economics, it described large structural change in markets. Reardon and Berdegué (2008, p. 1) wrote of a retail-led transformation in which, from the 1990s, "there was a sudden and meteoric rise of supermarkets in developing countries." In this sense transformation was something that happened to food systems, a description of modernisation, and studies of Viet Nam used it the same way. IFPRI (2016, p. 22) concluded that, through land and market reforms, "Vietnam has vastly transformed its food system." In global environmental change research, by contrast, transformation meant a needed shift away from unsustainable paths. Feola (2015, p. 377) found broad agreement on transformation as "a major, fundamental change, as opposed to minor, marginal, or incremental change".

The meaning moved from description to prescription in the 2010s, for three reasons. First, evidence on diets, climate and biodiversity showed that incremental change would not meet the Sustainable Development Goals. Caron et al. (2018, PDF p. 1) built a strategic narrative "calling for a deep transformation of food systems at scale". The EAT–Lancet Commission asked for "nothing less than a Great Food Transformation" (Willett et al., 2019, PDF p. 2), defined as "the unprecedented range of actions taken by all food system sectors across all levels" towards healthy diets from sustainable food systems (PDF p. 4). Its 2025 successor added social foundations and justice to the planetary targets (Rockström et al., 2025). Webb et al. (2020, p. 584) concluded that "the case for fundamental transformation of food systems is now irrefutable". Second, international organisations adopted the term. FAO (2018, PDF p. 2) set out "20 practical and interconnected actions with the aim of transforming food and agriculture", and Feola (2015, p. 376) had already noted that "the term 'transformation' is gradually becoming institutionalized" in science and policy. Third, the 2021 UN Food Systems Summit made transformation the explicit goal of national policy. Von Braun et al. (2021, p. 748), writing for the Summit's Scientific Group, framed the aim as "food systems change and, in the long run, even food systems transformation". Viet Nam's Summit commitment used the term in this sense: its food systems "need to be transformed into green, sustainable, and low-emission agriculture", and "A National Action Plan for a sustainable food system will be built" (Dao & Nguyen, 2022, p. 67). In this sense transformation is a programme to be carried out, not a trend to be observed.

The core meaning today has three elements. Transformation is fundamental: it changes structures, rules, power relations and values, not only practices. It is systemic: it reaches across production, markets, food environments and consumption at once, and across scales. And it is normative and political. Juri et al. (2024, PDF p. 11) describe a recent shift towards acknowledging the "political (contested) and normative (intentional and goaloriented) nature" of food system transformation. Béné and Abdulai (2024, PDF p. 1) put it similarly: transformation is "increasingly understood as purposive and normative changes that improve food systems processes and outcomes". Patterson et al. (2017, p. 2) add that efforts to transform are "likely to be deeply political and contested", because some actors gain and others lose. The main components are therefore a vision or direction, the levers used to reach it ([[Leverage points]], policies, innovations, investment), the actors who push or resist it, and the [[Food system governance]] that coordinates them.

Definitions diverge widely. Juri et al. (2024, PDF p. 1) found that "what is meant by food system transformation remains opaque", and that the term covers everything "from mere reformation and adaptations to radical and deep overhauls". In their review, "contributions typically do not offer definitions for the use of the terms" (PDF p. 7). Divergence follows the community using the term. Economists and international agencies frame transformation as large investment with high returns. Social-ecological scholars treat it as a shift between system states, linked to resilience; for some, "transformability is a fundamental characteristic of a resilient system" (Feola, 2015, p. 377). Food sovereignty and food justice movements see transformation as a change in who controls food, and reject reforms that leave corporate power intact (Pendergrast et al., 2019). Patterson et al. (2017, p. 1) group the main academic lenses as "socio-technical transitions, social-ecological systems, sustainability pathways, and transformative adaptation".

The boundary with [[Food system transitions]] is the hardest to draw. Juri et al. (2024, PDF p. 11) found that "the terms transition and transformation are often used interchangeably". Patterson et al. (2017, p. 2) use sustainability transformations "as an umbrella term to encompass diverse perspectives on transitions and transformations". A workable distinction is one of emphasis. Transition research analyses how regimes change over time, often looking back. Transformation is used for deliberate, goal-directed change of the whole system, often looking forward and tied to policy. Transformation also differs from [[Food system resilience]], which stresses keeping functions through shocks, and from structural transformation in the economic sense, which describes modernisation without a sustainability goal.

**Working definition used here:** food systems transformation is a deliberate, fundamental and systemic change in how food is produced, traded, governed and consumed, directed towards agreed goals for nutrition, equity and the environment, and shaped by contested choices about direction and means *(Claude's synthesis, based on Feola, 2015; Patterson et al., 2017; and Juri et al., 2024)*.

## Features

**Components.** Most accounts of transformation contain four parts. The first is a direction: a vision of a desirable food system, often linked to the SDGs or to sustainable healthy diets. The second is a set of levers: policies, innovations, finance, market rules and changes in norms. The third is a set of actors: states, firms, farmers, consumers, civil society and science, each with different power and interests. The fourth is governance: the arrangements that coordinate actors and choices across sectors and scales. Caron et al. (2018) express the direction as four parts, from healthy diets to a renaissance of rural territories. Governance is the least developed part: Vignola and Oosterveer (2025, PDF p. 2) find that the "governance challenges to achieve concrete food system transformation remain under-conceptualized". How these parts combine to produce change is the work of mechanisms.

**Mechanisms.** Three main mechanisms appear in the literature. The first is innovation steered by a mission. Klerkx and Begemann (2020, PDF p. 1) argue that agricultural innovation systems should reflect on "a particular directionality in innovation, and whether these also may support exnovation", that is, the phasing out of harmful practices. The second is regime shift. Pereira et al. (2020, PDF p. 1) draw lessons from past shifts in the global food system, such as "the Haber-Bosch process of nitrogen fixation, the rise of the supermarket". The third is change in incentives. The Food System Economics Commission estimates that the hidden costs of current food systems are "currently estimated at 15 trillion USD a year" (Laderchi et al., 2024, p. 8), and that the net benefits of transformation are "worth 5 to 10 trillion USD a year" (p. 7). Whether these mechanisms work depends on conditions.

**Conditions.** Transformation is held back by lock-ins. Béné (2022, PDF p. 2) argues that "powerful incumbents have very strong financial interests to maintain the status quo", and that the way evidence is produced and used can itself slow change. It also depends on political will, coalitions, finance and institutions that can coordinate across ministries. It also depends on the existing food system. In many low- and middle-income countries, informal markets feed most people, and Vorley (2023, p. 3) warns that "focusing too closely on formalisation may price out or remove informal food systems". In China, Si and Scott (2019, p. 1) describe "top-down and bottom-up forces that shape and politicise the food system transformation". Viet Nam's earlier transformation came from land and market reform, but it also brought "modernizing food value chains that are presenting new challenges" (IFPRI, 2016, p. 3). These conditions make it hard to tell whether a transformation is happening, which is a problem of measurement.

**Measurement.** Studies measure transformation in three ways. The first is outcome tracking: changes in diets, nutrition, emissions or incomes over time, often with dashboards. The second is tracking commitments and policies. Jomo et al. (2025) coded the national pathways of five East African countries and reported that "a total of 199 commitments were found" (PDF p. 1). The third is process analysis: how coalitions, institutions and practices change. Each approach has gaps. Outcomes change slowly and have many causes; commitments may not be implemented; and process studies rarely show scale. Juri et al. (2024, PDF p. 3) also found that research on the topic is recent, with "90% (367) were published in the last 7 years since 2015", so long-term evidence is scarce. The largest analysis of commitments so far shows the limits of the second approach. After the Summit, "over 100 countries committed to building on these dialogues to draft national pathways", but "food production dominates these pathways" (Candel et al., 2025, p. 809). These gaps feed the debates.

**Debates.** Three debates stand out. The first is about depth: reform or radical change. Agencies and economists present transformation as achievable through better policies and investment (FAO, 2018; Laderchi et al., 2024). Critics argue that real transformation must change power relations, and that the UN Food Systems Summit did the opposite. Canfield et al. (2021, PDF p. 1) argue that its processes "fail to address significant conflicts of interest, and ignore human rights", and note that the call for the Summit "neither emerged from the CFS, nor even the UN Food and Agriculture Organization" (PDF p. 1). The second is about whose knowledge counts. Juri et al. (2024, PDF p. 3) report "a strong prominence of authorship based in the Global North". The third is about meaning: whether a term used for everything still guides action. Feola (2015, p. 376) found that when transformation is used as more than a metaphor, the literature relies on several different concepts. Leeuwis et al. (2021, p. 761) argue that food systems offer "limited possibilities for central steering and control", so change needs approaches that "move beyond rational engineering approaches to system change". Béné and Abdulai (2024, PDF p. 1) conclude that whether transformation happens is "essentially down to how food systems politics are enacted". What remains unresolved is how to steer transformation deliberately when its outcomes cannot be controlled, and how to judge progress before the long-term results are visible.

## Implications

**Evidence overview.** Reviews show a fast-growing but uneven body of work. Juri et al. (2024) reviewed 410 articles from 1994 to 2022 and found that most do not define the term, use transition and transformation interchangeably, and come from Global North institutions. Feola (2015) reviewed transformation concepts across global environmental change research and found eight concepts most used, with little agreement on how transformation happens. Lee et al. (2021) reviewed nutrition policy in high-income countries and found that most actions relied on education, while "policy actions targeting food environments" and regulatory reforms were rare (p. 766). Evidence on actual transformation, in the sense of systemic change achieved, is thin. Most studies describe visions, commitments or early processes, and few follow outcomes over a decade or more.

Candel et al. (2025, p. 809) found that national pathways "show limited variation in topics raised, closely following predefined global agendas", and that "food distribution, processing, consumption, environmental impacts, labour conditions and animal welfare receive minimal attention". They conclude that there is "a lack of the necessary political leadership to achieve such reforms" (p. 813).

**Cases.** The cases are chosen to span a typology of where transformation is driven from *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: national reform, post-Summit national pathways, mixed state and civil society, local participatory governance, policy in high-income countries, global governance, and informal markets.

**Cross-case lessons.** The cases suggest four patterns. National reform can transform a food system quickly, as in Viet Nam, but the result is not automatically sustainable or healthy, and new problems follow. Post-Summit pathways show strong commitment on paper but weak attention to nutrition and governance, as the East African case shows. Where the state works with civil society or local actors, as in China and Germany, change is more grounded but small in scale. And contested cases, such as the Summit and the formalisation of informal markets, show that the same word can serve opposite agendas. What the evidence does not yet show is a documented case of deliberate, system-wide food transformation achieving its goals; most cases are either past, emergent changes or plans whose results are not yet known.
