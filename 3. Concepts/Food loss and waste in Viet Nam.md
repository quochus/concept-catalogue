## Definitions
In Viet Nam the topic began as post-harvest loss, a problem of farm income and export quality rather than of waste. Weinberger et al. (2008, p. 289) record that Viet Nam's fruit and vegetable export revenue fell by a third between early 2003 and early 2004, a decline the government blamed on poor storage and post-harvest technology. The state answered with support policies: Decision 63/2010/QĐ-TTg on reducing post-harvest losses of agricultural and aquatic products, replaced by Decision 68/2013/QĐ-TTg on reducing losses in agriculture, and MARD Decision 1003/2014 on adding value in processing and reducing post-harvest losses (To & Le, 2025; Vu, 2019, PDF p. 7). The aim was explicit. A 2019 MARD presentation lists a goal of *"targeting at 50% reduction by 2020"* against losses then estimated at 5 percent for rice, 12 percent for vegetables and 10 percent for aquaculture (Vu, 2019, PDF p. 5). The instruments were investment incentives: interest support for loans to buy loss-reducing machinery, and *"Reducing land use tax for investment in cold storage"* (Vu, 2019, PDF p. 7).

The framing widened in the late 2010s, for two reasons. First, the global shift to food loss *and* waste (see [[Food loss and waste]]) reached Vietnamese policy through SDG 12.3, so the same 2019 MARD presentation already set loss and waste side by side and linked both to food security and climate change (Vu, 2019, PDF pp. 2, 13). Second, Viet Nam adopted a food systems agenda. Decision 300/QĐ-TTg of 28 March 2023 approved the National Action Plan for transforming the food system towards transparency, responsibility and sustainability by 2030, and FLW reduction is now reported under it (To & Le, 2025; see [[Food systems transformation]]). Climate research added a third lens: the CGIAR Mitigate+ initiative estimated FLW-related greenhouse gas emissions by product, and found that in rice chains *"6.4 million tons of FLW"* corresponds to 12.2 million tonnes CO2-eq (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 3). The waste side evolved separately, inside solid waste policy. The National Strategy on Integrated Management of Solid Waste (2009, revised 2018) treats waste as a resource, but *"more attention is usually given to plastic waste rather than organic waste"* (Nguyen et al., 2020, p. 1).

The core definition used in Vietnamese work follows FAO. MARD separates food loss, at production, post-harvest, processing and distribution, from food waste, at retail and consumption (Vu, 2019, PDF p. 2). The country profiles by Wageningen and CGIAR use FAO's 2019 definition: FLW is *"all food intended for human consumption that is finally not consumed by humans"* (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). As a topic, FLW in Viet Nam brings together five things: estimates of how much is lost, hotspots by product and chain stage, causes in fragmented smallholder chains (see [[Post-harvest handling and storage]], [[Cold chain]]), the policy response, and practical initiatives in firms and civil society.

Estimates diverge sharply, and the divergence is a matter of method. CEL Consulting (2018) estimated losses up to processing at 8.8 million tonnes, worth USD 3.9 billion (Ho et al., 2022, p. 45). The Wageningen mass-flow model estimates total FLW at 25.6 million tonnes, but its authors note that because CEL's method is unclear *"a proper validation is not possible"* (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 6). Sector surveys give loss rates per stage; environmental studies give food waste as a share of municipal waste; and the national report to APEC in 2025 calls its own figures an incomplete assessment that leaves out food waste (To & Le, 2025). The first field measurement shows how far method matters. The country model put rice loss at *"over 20% of rice produced"* between harvest and consumer, but measurements on 48 Mekong Delta farms found *"an average PHL% of 5.5% on-farm"*; the whole-chain total then swings between about 8 and 17 percent of production depending on whether an uncertain drying estimate is included (Kok et al., 2024, pp. 1, 3, 7). Behind these numbers sit two policy communities, agriculture measuring losses and environment managing waste, that rarely share data *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*.

This page has clear boundaries with its neighbours. The general concept, its definitions and global evidence sit on [[Food loss and waste]]; this page holds only what is specific to Viet Nam. It is wider than post-harvest loss, which is what most Vietnamese policy has actually targeted. It is not the same as food waste management, which deals with collection, landfill, compost and biogas once waste exists (Nguyen et al., 2020, p. 1). And it overlaps with, but is not the same as, food safety and quality standards, which shape how much produce is rejected or downgraded (Ho et al., 2022, p. 49).

**Working definition used here:** Food loss and waste in Viet Nam is the national evidence, causes and policy on food lost between harvest and distribution and wasted in retail, food service and households, defined in line with FAO's 2019 split between loss and waste (Claude's synthesis, based on Vu, 2019, and Axmann et al., 2024).

## Features
**Components**
- Hotspot products: across five criteria (tonnes, emissions, land, water and protein), the priority products are *"rice, pig meat, freshwater fish, vegetables and bovine meat"*, with rice *"ranking at the top for all five categories"* (Axmann, 2024, p. 6).
- Relative loss is highest in perishables: fruits and vegetables show *"average FLW of 54%"* along their chains in the model (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 3), against *"while for rice FLW is estimated at 23%"* (Axmann, Guo, et al., 2022, p. 2). Field studies of single crops give lower but still high figures: *"an average total post-harvest loss of 12%"* for lychee (Pham et al., 2025, p. 40), and for shallots in Trà Vinh *"Total shallot losses along the supply chain reach an estimated 31.7%"*, with *"21.7% occurring during the production stage"* (Ho et al., 2025, p. 76).
- Stage hotspots in the model are *"processing and packaging of fresh vegetables"* and the *"handling and storage of fish and seafood represent major bottlenecks"*, and *"the retail stage is also a hotspot for both product categories"* (Axmann, Guo, et al., 2022, p. 3).
- Urban food waste: *"The FW from Vietnam's urban areas is 0.29 kg"* per person per day, *"accounting for 31.7 % of total waste"* (Nguyen et al., 2020, p. 1). In Hanoi, self-reported household food waste *"averaged 1192 g/day/household in urban areas"*, and *"cooking waste generated during meal processing/preparation accounts for more than 70%"* (Liu & Nguyen, 2020, p. 1). In Đà Nẵng, per capita food waste rose from 0.39 kg in 2016 and *"reached 0.52kg in 2018"*, and in large hotels and resorts *"daily food waste reached 100–200 kg in large facilities"* (Pham et al., 2021, p. 1). Ho Chi Minh City households generate about *"13,000 tons of solid waste per day"*, of which *"50–60% is organic waste"* (Phan et al., 2026, p. 1859).
- Nutrition: because some groups lack calcium, vitamin A and zinc, cutting FLW in *"rice and freshwater fish would contribute the most"* to nutrient supply (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 5).

**Mechanisms**
- Fragmented chains: logistics firms rank *"Fragmentation of farmers and middlemen"* among the top causes of loss (Ho et al., 2022, p. 49); MARD names *"Small farm without support by cooperatives"* (Vu, 2019, PDF p. 6), *"Too many intermediary procedures"* and a *"Lack of storage warehouse"* (Vu, 2019, PDF p. 4).
- Cold chain treated as a cost: shippers still see refrigeration as a *"cost rather than an added value to the product"* (Ho et al., 2022, p. 48), and *"more than 90% of agricultural products transported to agricultural wholesale markets"* travel without refrigeration or cold storage (Ho et al., 2022, p. 48).
- Oversupply and failure to sell: middlemen *"ascribe loss to an oversupply of vegetables"* and transport damage, and *"retailers considered failure to sell all produce as a major reason for loss"* (Weinberger et al., 2008, p. 294).
- In rice, *"Weather, particularly rain and flooding, was the most frequently cited cause"* of loss, followed by poor equipment and *"Fast machine operation/short cutting"* by combine harvesters (Kok et al., 2024, p. 4).
- Losses show up downstream of their cause: in dragon fruit and longan chains, hotspots are *"pre-harvest and b) sorting & grading"*, and grading losses are *"the result of poor initial quality and/or improper handling earlier"* in the chain (Axmann, Soethoudt, & Thúy, 2022, PDF p. 11).
- Weather and climate: farmers' vegetable losses rise *"by 20% during the wet season"* (Weinberger et al., 2008, p. 292); in the Mekong Delta, salinity, drought and poor forecasts push farmers to over-plant as a hedge, which creates surplus and loss (Ho et al., 2022, p. 50).
- Missing information: *"Farmers in Vietnam have little access to current market information"*, so they plan on past prices and over-produce (Ho et al., 2022, p. 50).
- Household routines: in Hanoi, *"expiration dates and deteriorating quality were cited as primary reasons"* for waste at home, against *"larger portions and over-ordering outside the home"* (Liu & Nguyen, 2020, p. 1); in Ho Chi Minh City, intention does not reduce waste, while cooking skills and knowing family preferences do (Phan et al., 2026, p. 1858; see [[Intention–behaviour gap]]).

**Conditions**
- Cold storage is small, young and southern: *"Vietnam's cold storage business is quite young"*; *"Vietnam had only 53 cold storage warehouses"* in 2021, and the South holds *"80 percent of the total intended capacity"* (Ho et al., 2022, p. 48); around Ho Chi Minh City most capacity is *"used for aquaculture"* (Ho et al., 2022, p. 49).
- Mechanisation and organisation lower losses. Mechanised combine harvesting and off-farm mechanical drying explain why measured on-farm rice loss in the Mekong Delta is low; the authors expect *"actual on-farm PHL fractions are higher than the fractions measured"* elsewhere in the country, where farms are less mechanised (Kok et al., 2024, p. 6). For lychee, *"agricultural cooperative participation demonstrate the largest effect magnitudes"* alongside Good Agricultural Practice (Pham et al., 2025, p. 40), and a vegetable case study concluded that *"cooperative SCs are the most appropriate for Vietnamese farmers"* (Yang et al., 2022, p. 54).
- Market power matters: lychee losses include *"economic losses from farmers' weak market power"* (Pham et al., 2025, p. 40), and shallot growers who sell mostly through traditional markets face *"price fluctuations, inadequate storage facilities, and transportation issues"* (Ho et al., 2025, p. 76).
- Low product value limits demand for cold chains, given *"the low value of agricultural and aquatic products in Vietnam"* (Ho et al., 2022, p. 50).
- Standards are weak: *"there is no formal general standard for evaluating the quality of domestic food products"*, so firms set their own (Ho et al., 2022, p. 49).
- The legal frame is thin. Viet Nam has no law, plan or strategy solely on FLW, no "Good Samaritan" protection for food donors, and confusion over date labels leads donors to discard safe food (Harvard Law School Food Law and Policy Clinic & The Global FoodBanking Network, 2024). Food waste *"has not yet become the subject of municipal, provincial, or national policy discussions"* (Nguyen et al., 2020, p. 1).
- Informal reuse is fading: in Hanoi, food scraps collected to feed pigs, chickens and fish *"were under pressure due to metropolization"* (Nguyen et al., 2020, p. 2). In Đà Nẵng, two-thirds of surveyed households gave food waste to pig farmers, but demand fell *"due to the African swine fever epidemic"* (Pham et al., 2021, p. 1).
- Viet Nam starts from a relative advantage in the region: Cambodia *"lacked the technological expertise of Vietnam"* in vegetable post-harvest handling (Weinberger et al., 2008, p. 293).

**Measurement**
- Viet Nam is better covered than most low-income countries but still thin. A global review found studies of immediate post-harvest loss *"mainly in a few developing countries"*, naming India and Viet Nam (Xue et al., 2017, p. 6619). Yet *"There was no formal literature found on FLW data"* for the whole country (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 6), and researchers report *"a dearth of data on food loss and cold chain management"* (Ho et al., 2022, p. 45).
- The country profiles are modelled, not measured: a mass-flow model of production and trade in which *"Estimates of losses per chain stage are derived from Porter et al."* (2016), a global dataset (Axmann, Guo, et al., 2022, p. 2).
- The first field measurement of a national hotspot was done in 2024: Cần Thơ University combined *"direct field measurements and interviews"* on rice farms, harvester operators, traders and millers in the Mekong Delta (Kok et al., 2024, p. 1). Its weakest link is drying, estimated by a single miller, and *"It is unknown if moisture loss is included or excluded"* (Kok et al., 2024, p. 3).
- Other primary evidence comes from actor surveys: perceived losses reported by farmers, middlemen and retailers (Weinberger et al., 2008, p. 291) and *"interviews with 30 shippers and food logistics units"* (Ho et al., 2022, p. 46). These measure what actors report, mostly upstream.
- The government's own 2025 progress report to APEC covers losses only, leaves out food waste, and calls for a systematic method in line with international standards (To & Le, 2025).
- A 2026 workshop of the Ministry of Agriculture and Environment, FAO and Denmark proposed a national strategic framework with clear responsibilities and a baseline data system built on a unified method (Nông nghiệp và Môi trường, 2026).

**Debates**
- Where most food is lost. Actor surveys point upstream: farmers lose about twice as much per tonne as middlemen (Weinberger et al., 2008, p. 292), and a logistics survey attributes *"25% of agricultural product loss comes from cultivation and processing activities"* against *"14% comes from storage, handling, and transportation activities"* (Ho et al., 2022, p. 49). Recent crop studies agree: two-thirds of shallot losses occur at production (Ho et al., 2025, p. 76). The model instead flags processing and packaging, and retail, as hotspots for vegetables (Axmann, Guo, et al., 2022, p. 3), and for rice the measured on-farm loss is small next to the possible losses in drying and milling (Kok et al., 2024, p. 7).
- Loss or waste. Since 2010 almost all policy has targeted losses; food waste has stayed in solid waste policy, where plastic gets more attention (Nguyen et al., 2020, p. 1; To & Le, 2025).
- Technology or organisation. One view stresses cold storage investment and tax incentives (Vu, 2019, PDF p. 7; Ho et al., 2022, p. 50); another stresses cooperatives, contracts and standards that give farmers stable demand (Ho et al., 2022, p. 50).
- Which products first. Ranked by tonnes, vegetables lead; by emissions, rice leads; by nutrition, rice and freshwater fish lead (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF pp. 3, 5).
- Still unresolved: with no national baseline, no FLW target can be tracked; whether the 2020 goal of halving losses was met cannot be assessed *(Claude's inference, not from a source)*.

## Implications
**Evidence overview.** There is no systematic review of FLW in Viet Nam. A global critical review of 202 FLW publications found that *"Most existing studies on FLW are conducted in industrialized countries"*, and placed Viet Nam among the few developing countries with studies of immediate post-harvest loss (Xue et al., 2017, p. 6619). The national picture rests on one modelled country profile, which its authors could not validate (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 6), and a growing set of single-crop or single-city studies. Upstream, these studies measure loss rates of roughly 5–32 percent depending on crop and stage; downstream, they measure household and food-service waste in Hanoi, Đà Nẵng and Ho Chi Minh City, mostly from self-reports. Viet Nam evidence on quality and nutrient losses, on retail waste, and on whether interventions work at scale is almost absent. The cases below are chosen to span the chain *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: staple grain, perishable fruit and vegetables, logistics firms, households, and food rescue.

**Cross-case lessons.** Upstream, losses concentrate at production and the first handling steps for perishables, and in drying and milling for rice, which supports the "first mile" view for fruit and vegetables but complicates it for rice. Where losses fall, the drivers are organisational as much as technical: mechanisation, cooperatives, GAP and coordinated buyers matter as much as cold storage, and a single firm can cut losses sharply when it combines technology with training. Downstream, household waste is substantial and dominated by cooking waste, but the one behavioural study finds that good intentions do not translate into less waste, and informal reuse as pig feed is shrinking. What the evidence cannot show is a reliable national total, the size of retail and food-service waste, quality and nutrient losses, or the effect of any policy at scale.

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** You are right that there is no harmonised method. The Wageningen country profile found no national FLW data and could not validate its model against the only other estimate (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 6), and the government's own 2025 report to APEC calls its figures incomplete and asks for a systematic method (To & Le, 2025). The data are fragmented too: researchers describe a dearth of data on food loss and cold chains (Ho et al., 2022, p. 45), and food waste data sit in a separate, environmental stream (Nguyen et al., 2020, p. 1).
- **Extends.** The evidence adds hotspots by product, not just by stage: rice, pig meat, freshwater fish, vegetables and bovine meat, with rice leading on emissions and fruits and vegetables on loss rates (Axmann, 2024, p. 6; Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 3). It also shows a policy history going back to Decision 63/2010 and a 2020 target to halve losses (Vu, 2019, PDF p. 5).
- **Challenges.** For fruit and vegetables the new field studies back your first-mile point: two-thirds of shallot losses occur at production (Ho et al., 2025, p. 76), and the main lychee losses arise on farm and at first sale (Pham et al., 2025, p. 40). For rice it holds less well: measured on-farm loss in the Mekong Delta was only 5.5 percent, and the larger and more uncertain losses sit in drying and milling (Kok et al., 2024, pp. 3, 7). The national model also flags processing, packaging and retail for vegetables (Axmann, Guo, et al., 2022, p. 3). And because food waste is barely measured, part of the upstream dominance may reflect what gets measured rather than where food is actually lost *(Claude's inference, not from a source)*.
