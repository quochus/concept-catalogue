## My first take
*Written by Quoc before reading the draft (2026-10-21). Never edited later.*

Agroecology - a science and a movement, agroecology seeks to reconnect nature in agri-food production and foster the transformation of food production and food systems towards diversity, balance and resilience.

## Definitions
**Science, movement, practice.** Wezel et al. (2009, p. 503) trace three meanings of agroecology: a scientific discipline, a movement and a practice. The mix differs by country, with "a stronger emphasis on movement and agricultural practice in Brazil". The scale of the science widened from plot and field to "investigations covering the whole food system" (p. 503).

**The ecology of food systems.** Francis et al. (2003, p. 100) argue for "defining agroecology as the ecology of food systems", reaching beyond the farm gate into landscapes and communities.

**Levels of transition.** Gliessman (2016, p. 188) describes five levels. Levels 1–3 cover efficiency, substitution and redesign on the farm. Level 4 is a more direct connection between growers and eaters (see [[Connectivity]]). Level 5 is a new global food system based on equity and justice.

**Principles and elements.** The HLPE (2019) consolidated "13 consolidated agroecological principles": recycling, input reduction, soil health, animal health, biodiversity, synergy, economic diversification, co-creation of knowledge, social values and diets, fairness, connectivity, land and natural resource governance, and participation (Wezel et al. 2020, p. 1). FAO's 10 Elements are a parallel framework built around the "wholeness, interconnectedness and interdependence" of the elements (Barrios et al. 2020, p. 232).

## Features
- **Social and human values are part of the definition**, not an add-on (Bezner Kerr et al. 2022, p. 1).
- **Contested meanings.** Holt-Giménez and Altieri (2013, p. 90) distinguish "reformist and radical versions of agroecology". The reformist version tries to "co-opt agroecology into the Green Revolution"; the radical one ties it to peasant food sovereignty. Levidow et al. (2014, p. 1127) make a similar point: agroecology can play roles "either conforming to the dominant regime, or else helping to transform it". See [[Food sovereignty]].
- **Moving into the mainstream.** An economics review concludes that enhancing agroecology "will require a fully integrated multiscale systems approach" from farm to region to globe (Ewert et al. 2023, p. 351).
- **Overlapping labels.** Organic, regenerative and conservation agriculture overlap with agroecology but are narrower or differently governed. See [[Regenerative agriculture]]; this needs its own sourcing.

## Implications
- **Viet Nam.** Farmers have few chances to reach markets and play "only a small role in adding value to agricultural products" (Dao & Nguyen 2022, p. 3). The paper argues for an agroecology transition within national food-system strategies.
- **Laos.** Agroecological innovation systems are "actor networks involving a diversity of individuals", such as farmers, traders and experts (Castella et al. 2022, p. 1). The authors recommend that "a pluralistic approach is desirable to promote innovation" (p. 14).
- **Knowledge.** In the Latin American movement, farmer-to-farmer learning follows a "Freirian horizontal communication methodology" (Rosset & Martínez-Torres 2012, PDF p. 6).

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** Science and movement, reconnecting with nature, and food-system transformation all match Wezel et al. (2009), Francis et al. (2003) and Gliessman (2016).
- **Extends.** Most frameworks add a third meaning, practice. They also make the social and political side explicit: fairness, participation and governance (HLPE principles 10–13).
- **Challenges.** "Diversity, balance and resilience" are ecological aims. The literature stresses that agroecology is also contested politically: it can conform to the dominant regime or transform it (Holt-Giménez & Altieri 2013; Levidow et al. 2014).
