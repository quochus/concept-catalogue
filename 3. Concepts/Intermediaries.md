## Definitions
The term comes from innovation studies, where research on technology transfer found a set of actors working between firms, researchers and users. Howells (2006, p. 715) noted that many labels were in use, such as third parties, bridgers and brokers, and proposed a working definition: an organisation or body that "acts an agent or broker in any aspect of the innovation process" between two or more parties (p. 720).

As innovation came to be seen as a system rather than a pipeline, attention moved from bilateral brokering to coordinating whole networks. Van Lente et al. (2003, p. 247) identified "a new type of intermediary organization, which functions at system or network level", which they called systemic intermediaries, and linked them to long-term transitions. In agriculture, the same shift appeared as extension broadened from technology transfer: "Traditionally, extension services were considered the main intermediary actor" (Kilelu et al., 2011, p. 85). Studying the Dutch agricultural sector, Klerkx and Leeuwis (2009, p. 849) showed why the literature "persistently points at the need for intermediary organizations to fulﬁll bridging and brokerage roles", and called those whose main task is brokerage "innovation brokers" (p. 850).

Sustainability transition research then asked which intermediaries speed up change. Kivimaa et al. (2019, p. 1062) reviewed the field, defined transition intermediaries and proposed five types: systemic, regime-based, niche, process and user intermediaries. They argued that "systemic and niche intermediaries are the most crucial forms of intermediary actors" in transitions, supported by a wider ecology of others (p. 1062). Later work added time: "Intermediary functions change from supporting experimentation and articulation of needs in predevelopment" to network building and institutional support as transitions accelerate (Kivimaa, Hyysalo, et al., 2019, p. 110).

A second, older meaning comes from agricultural marketing, where intermediaries are the traders, collectors, wholesalers and processors who move products between farmers and buyers. In Viet Nam's coastal central south, middlemen play "the dominating part" in connecting fishers and farmers to processors, and also supply inputs and set prices (Thanh, 2009, PDF p. 2). Governance research now treats such traders as actors in sustainability: Grabs and Carodenuto (2021, p. 1314) highlight "the critical, but underresearched role of traders" in linking corporate commitments to impacts on the ground.

Definitions diverge on neutrality and on scope. Many studies describe innovation intermediaries as "neutral or honest brokers without clear normative interests" (Kivimaa et al., 2019, p. 1063), while transition studies find that many hold strong goals and push for particular niches. Market intermediaries are often cast either as exploiters of small producers or as providers of services that farmers cannot get elsewhere.

Intermediaries differ from [[Multi-stakeholder platforms]], which are spaces where intermediation happens, and from boundary spanners, a broader term for anyone who links organisations. Market intermediaries connect to [[Market linkages]], and intermediaries of both kinds perform many of the supporting functions in [[Market systems]].

**Working definition used here:** intermediaries are actors or organisations that connect, translate between and coordinate other actors, either in innovation and transition processes, from single-link brokering to system-level alignment, or in the movement of products, information and finance between producers and buyers *(Claude's synthesis of Howells, 2006; van Lente et al., 2003; Kivimaa et al., 2019; Thanh, 2009)*.

## Features
**Components.** The literature points to two families *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*. Innovation intermediaries include technology brokers, systemic brokers, extension services and research-for-development projects. Market intermediaries include collectors, traders, wholesalers, processors and producer organisations that aggregate supply. In Kenya, Kilelu et al. (2011, p. 84) found "a typology of four innovation intermediation arrangements": technology broker, systemic broker, enterprise development support and input access support; Kivimaa et al. (2019) give five transition types by level and goal. Many organisations combine both roles. The functions they perform are the next question.

**Mechanisms.** Intermediaries bridge actors "where direct interaction is difficult" because of transaction costs (Kivimaa et al., 2019, p. 1063). Howells (2006) lists functions such as foresight, information scanning, knowledge processing, testing, accreditation and commercialisation; systemic intermediaries add the articulation of needs and options, the alignment of actors and support for learning (van Lente et al., 2003). In scaling, brokerage supports outscaling and institutional entrepreneurship supports upscaling (Hermans et al., 2013), and in CGIAR scaling "research-for-development organizations act as boundary spanners" (Minh & Schmitter, 2025, PDF p. 1). Market intermediaries provide credit, inputs and price information, but their power can cut both ways: in Viet Nam, the middlemen's "leveraged position has come to the detriment of small-scale producers" and processors (Thanh, 2009, PDF p. 2). These mechanisms depend on conditions.

**Conditions.** Intermediaries need legitimacy and funding. Innovation brokers "have difﬁculty in becoming embedded" because clients and funders find it hard to grasp the value of their work (Klerkx & Leeuwis, 2009, p. 849). In Indonesia, the public sector dominates research and extension, but "insufficient routes to transfer local needs to the public sector impede efficient feedback", and village cooperatives that link to extension "face serious financial distress" (Fukugawa et al., 2018, PDF p. 1). Research projects can act as intermediaries but only partly: an Indonesian cattle project "partially succeeded in performing intermediary innovation functions" (Valerio et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). Recent work shows intermediaries often act together: in Spanish public food procurement, "collective intermediation is orchestrated by pivotal intermediaries" (Gaitán-Cremaschi et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). These conditions shape how intermediation is studied.

**Measurement.** Studies use function lists to assess what intermediaries do, network analysis to map their position, and case studies to trace their effects over time. Valerio et al. (2024, PDF p. 1) "qualitatively analysed data from the R4D project using six innovation intermediary functions". Market intermediaries are studied through value chain mapping, margins and price transmission. Because intermediation is indirect, its effect on outcomes is hard to isolate.

**Debates.** First, boundaries: the literature "lacks clarity in how intermediation is defined, where it begins and ends" (Kivimaa et al., 2019, p. 1063). Second, neutrality versus advocacy. Third, funding: brokering is hard to pay for because its outcomes are indirect (Klerkx & Leeuwis, 2009; Kilelu et al., 2011). Fourth, for market intermediaries, whether projects should bypass traders to shorten chains or work through them, since traders often carry functions that farmers cannot. What remains unresolved is how to support intermediaries without making them dependent on project funding.

## Implications
**What the evidence shows.** Reviews agree that intermediaries are important but not sufficient. Van Lente et al. (2003, p. 247) found systemic intermediaries "useful and necessary, but not sufficient" in a fuel cell partnership. Agricultural studies show that existing organisations, especially producer organisations, extension and traders, often do intermediary work without naming it.

| Case | Where | Intermediary type | What they did | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kenyan agricultural intermediaries | Kenya, 2010s | Mixed (22 organisations) | Technology, systemic, enterprise and input brokering | Brokering is pervasive and often a side activity | Kilelu et al. (2011, p. 84) |
| Vegetable producer organisations | Uruguay | Producer organisations (regime and niche) | Organic inputs, market access, knowledge sharing | "Producer organizations mostly function as implicit transition intermediaries" | Groot-Kormelinck et al. (2022, PDF p. 1) |
| R4D cattle project | Nusa Tenggara Barat, Indonesia | Research-for-development project | Brokered knowledge and links for beef technologies | Partly succeeded; needs continuous farmer and private sector involvement | Valerio et al. (2024) |
| Agricultural innovation system | Indonesia | Public agencies, village cooperatives, firms | Divided technology transfer and extension | Weak feedback from farmers; cooperatives in financial distress | Fukugawa et al. (2018) |
| Middlemen in processing supply | Coastal central south, Viet Nam | Market intermediaries | Supplied inputs and set prices for fishers and farmers | Dominant role, to the detriment of small producers | Thanh (2009) |
| Sustainable public food procurement | Spain, four cases | Networks of transition intermediaries | Coordinating procurement change | Pivotal intermediaries orchestrate others | Gaitán-Cremaschi et al. (2024, PDF p. 1) |
| Conservation agriculture networks | Laos | Projects, government and farmer groups | Building actor networks around CA | A pluralistic approach supports innovation | Castella et al. (2022) |

**Lessons.** Intermediaries work best when they hold legitimacy with the actors they connect, as producer organisations do in Uruguay, and when several intermediaries play complementary roles. Southeast Asian cases add two warnings: research projects are temporary intermediaries, and market intermediaries can hold power over small producers. For IP1+ in Laos and Cambodia, this points to mapping who already brokers inputs, knowledge and markets, including traders and seed suppliers, before creating new platforms, and to planning how intermediary roles will be funded after projects end.
