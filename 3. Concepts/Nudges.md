## Definitions
The idea that behaviour changes when the setting of a choice changes is old in psychology, but it became a policy approach with Thaler and Sunstein's book *Nudge* in 2008. Choice architecture *"received considerable impetus from the publication in 2008 of the book"* and then *"gained traction in policy circles"* (Hollands et al., 2013, PDF p. 1). The problem it answered was that people often act against their own stated goals, and that information campaigns and regulation are either weak or politically costly. Behavioural economics offered a third route: design the choice so that the better option is easier.

Thaler and Sunstein define a nudge as *"any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people’s behavior in a predictable way"* without forbidding options or significantly changing economic incentives; in their words, *"Putting fruit at eye level counts as a nudge; banning junk food does not"* (quoted in Cadario & Chandon, 2020, PDF p. 2). Because this definition was broad, public health researchers narrowed it for practice. Hollands et al. (2013, PDF p. 1) proposed *"an evidence-based definition and typology of choice architecture interventions"* in micro-environments such as shops, canteens and restaurants, and found that weak evidence partly reflected *"a prior lack of definitional and conceptual clarity"*. The use then spread from health to sustainability, where *"green nudges"* are meant to *"leverage cognitive biases to elicit sustainablebehaviors"* (Petel, 2020, PDF p. 2).

The core definition now has two parts. A nudge changes the presentation of a choice, for example its default, position, size, salience or the information attached; and it leaves options and prices in place. Price changes or promotions are not nudges *"because they provide a direct economic incentive"* (Cadario & Chandon, 2020, PDF p. 2), and education that does not change the choice environment is a complement rather than a nudge. Such interventions *"typically require minimal conscious engagement"* and can influence many people at once (Hollands et al., 2013, PDF p. 3).

Definitions diverge on how broad the category should be. Some studies count labels and information prompts as nudges; others restrict the term to changes in the physical setting. The difference follows discipline and purpose: policy units use *"nudge"* for any behavioural insight, while reviewers need a narrow category to compare effects.

Nudges need to be kept apart from their neighbours. [[Incentives]] change the costs and rewards of a choice; nudges change its presentation. The [[Food environment]] is the wider setting in which nudges act. Labels from [[Sustainability certification]] can work as nudges when they make a quality more salient at the point of choice.

**Working definition used here:** nudges are changes in how choices are presented, through defaults, placement, size, labels or prompts, that steer behaviour in a predictable direction without banning options or changing economic incentives *(based on Thaler & Sunstein's definition as quoted in Cadario & Chandon, 2020, and Hollands et al., 2013)*.

## Features
**Components**
- Hollands et al. (2013, PDF p. 3) group choice architecture interventions into nine types, some altering the properties of products (such as size or presentation) and others *"altering their placement"*.
- Cadario and Chandon (2020, PDF p. 2) group healthy eating nudges by what they target: cognition (labels and information), affect (appeals and presentation) and behaviour (convenience and size). How these components change behaviour is the next question.

**Mechanisms**
- Nudges work by lowering the effort of the target choice or making it more salient, so that habits and quick judgements favour it.
- The more a nudge acts on behaviour rather than thinking, the larger its effect: *"Effect sizes increase as the focus of the nudges shifts from cognition (d = 0.12, −64 kcal) to affect (d = 0.24, −129 kcal) to behavior (d = 0.39, −209 kcal)"* (Cadario & Chandon, 2020, PDF p. 2).
- Moral or social prompts can also add to price signals: in Italian grocery delivery, *"moral green nudges outweigh financial incentives and amplify the effect"* (Rizzitello et al., 2026, PDF p. 1). These mechanisms depend on setting.

**Conditions**
- Nudges are stronger where choices are made quickly and repeatedly. Effects are *"larger in the United States than in other countries, in restaurants or cafeterias than in grocery stores"* (Cadario & Chandon, 2020, PDF p. 2), and nudges are better at reducing unhealthy eating than at increasing healthy eating.
- Who is nudged also matters: in an online supermarket, 23% of low-income Italian shoppers accepted a swap away from ultra-processed food, and younger men with lower reactance were most likely to follow it (Sapio et al., 2025, PDF p. 1). These variations raise the question of how effects are measured.

**Measurement**
- Most evidence comes from field or online experiments that compare selection or consumption with and without the nudge, summarised as standardised effect sizes.
- Across more than 200 studies, choice architecture interventions promote change *"with a small to medium effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.45"* (Mertens et al., 2022, PDF p. 2), and *"Food choices are particularly responsive"* (Mertens et al., 2022, PDF p. 2). Measurement choices, however, are at the centre of the main debate.

**Debates**
- The first debate is about whether nudges work at all. Re-analysing the same data, Maier et al. (2022, PDF p. 3) argue that *"after correcting for this bias, no evidence remains that nudges are effective"*, although they found little evidence of publication bias in the food domain when using only the most precise estimates.
- The second debate is political. Critics argue that by *"targeting individual behaviors, rather than systemic reform"*, behavioural approaches avoid harder choices, and that nudging alone *"is insufficient to address environmental issues that are deeply politically and structurallyembedded"* (Petel, 2020, PDF p. 2).
- What remains unresolved is how large real effects are once publication bias is removed, and whether nudges are best used alone or as part of policy packages.

## Implications
- **Healthy eating in cafeterias and restaurants.** The best-designed behaviourally oriented nudges can be about six times more effective than the typical cognitive one: *"one implementing the best nudge scenario can expect a sixfold increase in effectiveness"* (Cadario & Chandon, 2020, PDF p. 2).
- **Low-income shoppers online.** A swap prompt reduced the choice of ultra-processed foods, but only about a quarter of shoppers accepted it (Sapio et al., 2025, PDF p. 1). Nudges can reach groups that information campaigns miss, but effects are partial.
- **Sustainable delivery choices.** Combining moral prompts with financial incentives worked better than incentives alone (Rizzitello et al., 2026, PDF p. 1), which suggests that nudges are most useful as part of a package.
- **Ecolabels at the point of choice.** Labels can raise the selection of more sustainable products and willingness to pay for them (Bastounis et al., 2021), acting as cognitive nudges.

Together, the cases show nudges producing modest, setting-dependent effects, largest when they change behaviour directly and weaker when they rely on information. No study from Viet Nam or ASEAN was found in the library. For regenerative or agroecological products, nudges such as shelf placement, default options in school or workplace meals, and salient labels are cheap to test, but they are unlikely to shift demand on their own without [[Incentives]], trust in the label and supply that makes the product available.
