## Definitions
Public food procurement is as old as school meals and army rations, but its use as a lever for sustainable food systems is recent. Through most of the twentieth century, public catering was judged mainly on cost. From the 2000s, scholars and governments began to argue that the state's purchasing power could do more: "Public food procurement has an enormous potential role to play in mitigating the impacts of diets and food on the environment as well as promoting social justice" (Sonnino, 2019, PDF p. 8). The problem it answered was that public money already bought large amounts of food, often from distant suppliers at the lowest price, while local farmers lacked stable markets.

Two traditions shaped the concept. In Europe, the focus was on sustainable and healthy public catering, with school meals reform as the flagship. In the Global South, it grew from social protection: school feeding came to be seen as a way to provide "structured demand", so that buying from family farms "is easier, less costly and less risky" for them (Sumberg & Sabates-Wheeler, 2011, p. 342). Brazil turned this idea into law. Since 2009, 30% of the federal funds for school meals "must be used to procure food from family farmers", with priority for organic or ecological products (Sidaner et al., 2013, p. 991). Home-grown school feeding (HGSF) then spread through WFP and FAO programmes, including in Lao PDR (WFP, 2025).

In current use, public food procurement means government purchases of food for schools, hospitals, prisons and other public services, designed "with the objective of advancing social, economic and environmental development through government purchases" (FAO et al., 2021, PDF p. 9). Its defining feature is that procurement rules can decide "what food will be purchased (such as local, diverse, nutritious" and from whom (Swensson & Tartanac, 2020, PDF p. 1). Inclusive procurement is the variant aimed at specific groups such as smallholders.

Definitions diverge on the main goal. Nutrition and education actors see procurement as a way to improve diets and school attendance; agricultural and rural development actors see a market for smallholders; food sovereignty movements see a way to restructure markets and support agroecology (Wittman & Blesh, 2017, p. 82). The difference matters because the goals can conflict, for example on cost, scale and food safety.

Public procurement needs to be kept apart from its neighbours. It is a demand-side [[Incentives]] tool used by the state. It differs from private [[Market linkages]] because the buyer is public and bound by procurement law. It shapes the [[Food environment]] of children and patients directly, and it often needs [[Intermediaries]] such as cooperatives or NGOs to connect small producers to public buyers.

**Working definition used here:** public food procurement is the use of government food purchasing, for schools, hospitals and other public services, to pursue social, economic and environmental goals such as healthy diets, sustainable production and stable markets for local smallholders *(Claude's synthesis of FAO et al., 2021; Swensson & Tartanac, 2020; Sumberg & Sabates-Wheeler, 2011)*.

## Features
**Components.** A procurement programme has four parts *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: (1) the public demand itself, usually school meals; (2) the rules: what to buy, from whom and at what price, set in law, regulation and tender criteria; (3) the supply side, often farmer organisations and cooperatives; and (4) support services such as credit, extension and certification. In Brazil, most programmes "require farmers to belong to an association or a cooperative" (Wittman & Blesh, 2017, p. 86). The mechanisms follow from how these parts are linked.

**Mechanisms.** The main mechanism is structured demand: a large, predictable buyer reduces market risk for farmers. Brazil's PAA aimed to "ensure a price floor and increase marketing capacity" (Wittman & Blesh, 2017, p. 86), and buying from family farms gives them "a secured market with pre-negotiated prices" (Sidaner et al., 2013, p. 991). Procurement can also reward practices: by "providing incentives (including price premiums and targeted agricultural extension programmes for agro-ecological and organic production)", the PAA sought to shift production (Wittman & Blesh, 2017, p. 86). On the consumption side, menus shape what children eat. These mechanisms depend on conditions.

**Conditions.** The regulatory framework is decisive. Despite its promise, public procurement is often "a tale of untapped potential", partly because of "a lack of a conducive regulatory framework" (Swensson & Tartanac, 2020, PDF p. 1). The buyer also needs enough power to structure demand; devolving management to local level "may at the same time reduce the ability to structure demand" (Sumberg & Sabates-Wheeler, 2011, p. 348). Practical barriers are common: municipalities "face challenges when purchasing from family farmers", including meal preparation and resistance from former suppliers (Sidaner et al., 2013, p. 992). Intermediaries help: in Spain, collective intermediation was "orchestrated by pivotal intermediaries who mobilize diverse transition intermediaries" (Gaitán-Cremaschi et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). These conditions shape how effects are measured.

**Measurement.** Programmes report spending on local or family-farm purchases, the share of menus meeting nutrition guidelines and the number of farmers supplying. Brazil monitors "the percentage of executing entities that spend 30 % of resources on family agriculture procurement" (Sidaner et al., 2013, p. 992). Evidence on education and nutrition is strong, but "less is known about the potential agricultural and environmental effects" (Hunter et al., 2025, PDF p. 2). This gap fuels debate.

**Debates.** The main debate is about the "win–win" framing. Sumberg and Sabates-Wheeler (2011, p. 347) warn against presenting HGSF "as a single intervention" that can serve social protection and agricultural development at once, since the goals and assumptions differ. A second debate is about cost and trade-offs: in tight budgets, sustainable menus "will inevitably involve trade-offs between cost and the various aspects of sustainability" (Sonnino, 2019, PDF p. 8). A third, from food sovereignty scholars, is whether state-mediated markets empower farmers or make them dependent on public budgets (Wittman & Blesh, 2017). What remains unresolved is how far procurement can drive production change, rather than only creating a market for what farmers already grow.

## Implications
- **Brazil: the 30% rule.** The national school feeding programme reaches all public schools, and its 2009 law requires that 30% of federal funds buy from family farmers, with priority for organic or agroecological food (Sidaner et al., 2013, p. 991; Wittman & Blesh, 2017, p. 88). It is the reference case for procurement as a market for smallholders.
- **Lao PDR: home-grown school feeding.** A WFP programme targets 200 schools and "2,000 smallholder farmers engaged in local food production", with cash given to schools to buy food locally (WFP, 2025, PDF p. 2).
- **Spain: intermediaries in sustainable procurement.** Four cases showed that diverse intermediaries, working at both regime and niche levels, address barriers such as rules, logistics and supply (Gaitán-Cremaschi et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).
- **Global: climate and biodiversity.** School meals reach about 466 million children, and countries such as Brazil and Indonesia are designing procurement for environmental as well as social goals (Hunter et al., 2025, PDF p. 2).

The cases show that procurement works as a market for small producers when law sets clear targets, when farmers are organised and when intermediaries handle logistics and quality. For IP1+, school feeding in Cambodia and Laos is a possible public market for regenerative or agroecological products, but it would need procurement rules that favour such products, groups that can supply reliably, and a way to verify how the food was produced.
