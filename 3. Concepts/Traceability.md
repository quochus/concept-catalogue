## Definitions
Traceability spread through the food sector as food-safety legislation and buyer contracts required firms to know where a product had come from and where it had gone, and scientific work on food traceability has grown steadily since the late 1990s (Olsen & Borit, 2018, Fig. 1, p. 143). The problem it answered was an information gap: buyers and regulators cannot see how food was produced or handled, and once a hazard appears they need a way to find and recall the affected products. Hobbs (2004, Abstract) set out three functions that remain the core of the economic view: *"ex post reactive systems that allow the traceback of affected products"*, systems that help allocate liability, and *"information systems that provide ex ante quality verification"*.

The meaning widened as the concerns behind it widened. Food-safety laws, such as EU Regulation 178/2002 and the ISO 22005 standard, wrote traceability into rules and contracts (Olsen & Borit, 2018). Because labels alone could not prove what they claimed, traceability became a tool for quality and trust as well as safety: *"The current food labelling system cannot guarantee that the food is authentic"*, so traceability is used to assure safety and quality and to build consumer confidence (Aung & Chang, 2014, Abstract). More recently, sustainability claims, deforestation rules and digital technologies have pushed traceability towards *whole-chain* systems that carry data on origin, practices and environmental attributes, often on blockchain platforms (Feng et al., 2020; Bosona & Gebresenbet, 2023).

The most careful current definition comes from Olsen and Borit, who found that most legal and standard definitions *"suffer from recursion"*, defining traceability simply as the ability to trace, and proposed instead the ability to *"access any or all information relating to that which is under consideration"* throughout its life cycle by means of recorded identifications (Olsen & Borit, 2018, p. 144). Any traceability system then has three components: a way to identify traceable resource units (lots, cases, pallets), *"A mechanism for documenting transformations"* (how units are split, mixed and transformed), and a way to record their attributes (p. 144).

Definitions diverge on purpose and scope. Food-safety definitions stress tracing back and tracking forward through the chain (Aung & Chang, 2014). Logistics scholars treat traceability as part of supply chain management, and Bosona and Gebresenbet (2013, Abstract) note that a *"new conceptual definition of FTS has been provided"* that folds it into logistics. Development and governance scholars ask who bears the cost and who gains: traceability standards set by Northern buyers can exclude smallholders (van Vark et al., 2013). The split follows whether the main aim is recall, efficiency or market access.

Traceability is not the same as [[Transparency]], which is about what information is shared and with whom; a chain can be traceable to a firm but opaque to consumers. Nor is it [[Sustainability certification]], which verifies that standards were met; traceability is often the record-keeping that certification relies on. It also differs from authentication (testing whether a product is what it claims to be), although the two are often combined.

**Working definition used here:** traceability is the ability to identify a food product or lot and to access recorded information about its origin, transformations and attributes, backward to its sources and forward to its buyers, across the supply chain *(Claude's synthesis of Olsen & Borit, 2018; Hobbs, 2004; Aung & Chang, 2014)*.

## Features
**Components**
- Every system identifies traceable units, records how units are transformed, and records their attributes (Olsen & Borit, 2018, p. 144).
- Technologies range from paper records and QR codes to RFID, IoT sensors and blockchain. In Moc Chau, Son La, *"Farmers are now using QR codes to help trace the origin of vegetable crops"* supplied to retailers, backed by farm records (Sen, 2017, p. 101).
- Blockchain-based systems are a growing component; existing ones *"focus more on fruit and vegetables, meat, dairy, and milk"* (Bosona & Gebresenbet, 2023, PDF p. 1), and they address the weakness that *"most of the IoT solutions rely on the centralized server-client paradigm"* (Feng et al., 2020, Abstract).

**Mechanisms**
- Traceability reduces information asymmetry: it lets firms trace back and recall products, assign liability, and verify quality before sale (Hobbs, 2004).
- It supports trust and market access. Consumers reward it: a meta-analysis finds a premium *"reaching a price premium of around 32%"*, and *"Consumers in developing countries show a higher WTP for traceable food"* (Tran et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).
- In Viet Nam, attitudes towards traceable pork were driven partly by shopping habits; consumers' *"looking for the product origin incite the positive attitude toward traceable pork"* during the African swine fever outbreak (Dang & Tran, 2020, PDF p. 1).

**Conditions**
- Cost and who pays: in Moc Chau *"To use a QR code involves additional cost"*, so farmers used QR labels only for retailers that required them (Sen, 2017, p. 103).
- Skills and organisation: in Viet Nam's fruit chain, barriers include *"a lack of digital literacy amongst the stakeholders, poor organisational culture"*, while cooperatives help (Yap et al., 2023, Abstract).
- Readiness differs by chain stage: in West Java, upstream actors felt more discomfort and insecurity about traceability technology than downstream actors (Rifani et al., 2026); see [[Technology readiness]].
- Standards and power: *"For many African smallholders, those standards are a costly burden anyway"* (van Vark et al., 2013, p. 6).

**Measurement**
- System design is described by its components and by choices about identifiers, such as the granularity and uniqueness of the codes given to each unit (Olsen & Borit, 2018, p. 144).
- Demand is measured through willingness to pay and purchase intention, now synthesised in a meta-analysis (Tran et al., 2024; Dang & Tran, 2020).
- Adoption is measured through readiness and intention surveys, often using the TOE framework (Yap et al., 2023; Rifani et al., 2026).

**Debates**
- Traceability as a public good for safety: rapid traceback reduces the social costs of food-safety incidents (Hobbs, 2004; Aung & Chang, 2014).
- Traceability as a burden or barrier: buyer-driven standards shift costs onto producers and may exclude smallholders (van Vark et al., 2013); in Peruvian cocoa, digital tracing required *"investments that do not seem justified by corresponding increases in income"* (Steinke et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).
- Blockchain hype: reviews of blockchain projects take *"a critical view over the maturity of these projects"* (Kamilaris et al., 2019, PDF p. 1, preprint), and many systems remain pilots.
- Unresolved: who should pay for traceability in smallholder chains, and how much detail is worth recording for domestic as opposed to export markets.

## Implications
Viet Nam cases come first.

- **QR-code traceability in Moc Chau, Son La.** With ACIAR support, farmer groups kept records and used QR codes so that retailers and consumers in Hanoi could trace safe vegetables to the farm; QR labels were used only for retailers that required them, because of cost (Sen, 2017).
- **Blockchain for fruit in Viet Nam.** Interviews with 60 farmers, traders and consumers found trust, transparency, branding and cooperatives as facilitators and digital literacy, organisational culture, cost and data governance as barriers (Yap et al., 2023).
- **Digital tracing for Peruvian cocoa (contested).** Setting up digital traceability required investments not matched by income gains, and farmer-supplied data risked being inaccurate (Steinke et al., 2024).

Together the cases show that traceability works where a buyer needs it and pays for it, as with Hanoi retailers or export markets, and stalls where costs fall on farmers without a premium *(Claude's synthesis)*. The demand side is favourable, with consumers in developing countries willing to pay more for traceable food (Tran et al., 2024), but the premium must reach the producers who keep the records. For Vietnamese fruit and vegetable chains, simple, cheap identification (lot codes, QR labels) linked to cooperative record-keeping may give most of the benefit before blockchain is worth the cost.
