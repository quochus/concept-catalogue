## Definitions
The concept comes from the economics of information. Nelson (1970, p. 311) started from the observation that consumers know even less about the quality of products than about their prices, because quality information is costlier to obtain. He separated goods whose quality can be checked by inspection before purchase, search goods, from goods that consumers learn about by buying and using them, which he called experience goods (Nelson, 1970, p. 312). Darby and Karni (1973, as cited in Caswell & Mojduszka, 1996) added a third class, credence goods, whose quality cannot be judged even after use. Food economists then applied the three classes to the attributes of a product rather than to whole goods, using *"the distinction developed by Nelson (1970, 1974) and Darby and Karni"* (Caswell & Mojduszka, 1996, PDF p. 5).

The definitions are now settled. *"With credence attributes (or goods), consumers cannot determine the product’s quality even after they buy and consume it"* (Caswell & Mojduszka, 1996, PDF p. 5). A recent framework puts it in supply chain terms: credence attributes *"cannot be judged or assessed independently by buyers at the time of sale"* through search or experience *"without additional, often costly information"* (Schrobback et al., 2023, PDF p. 2). Typical food examples are *"organic foods, dolphin-safe tuna, free-range meat, and kosher foods"* (McCluskey, 2000, p. 1), and much of food safety and nutrition, where *"it is not practicable for individual consumers to assess the quality of the product"* (Caswell & Mojduszka, 1996, PDF p. 9).

The problem the concept names is information asymmetry: sellers know how food was produced and buyers do not. This creates room for *"opportunistic behavior such as fraud"* (Schrobback et al., 2023, PDF p. 1), and in the extreme, quality markets collapse; for credence attributes *"these markets for quality simply do not function well"* (Caswell & Mojduszka, 1996, PDF p. 9). Definitions diverge only at the edges. Some authors treat an attribute as credence when checking is impossible; others when it is too slow or costly to matter, so quality *"is observed too slowly or too late to matter or it is prohibitively costly"* (McCluskey, 2000, p. 1). The second, broader reading is the one used for sustainability claims.

Credence attributes should be kept apart from their neighbours. [[Trust in food chains]] is what makes credence claims believable; [[Sustainability certification]] and labels are tools for signalling them; [[Willingness to pay]] measures what they are worth to buyers. In food quality research, *"intrinsic"* and *"extrinsic"* attributes describe physical properties versus cues such as price, brand or label; that distinction cuts across the search–experience–credence one *(Claude's note; the source for this distinction is in Zotero without a PDF)*. It is different again from the [[Intrinsic value]] of nature.

**Working definition used here:** credence attributes are qualities of a food product, such as safety, nutrition, organic, fair or low-carbon production, that buyers cannot verify before or after purchase at reasonable cost, so their value depends on labels, certification, reputation and trust *(based on Caswell & Mojduszka, 1996; McCluskey, 2000; Schrobback et al., 2023)*.

## Features
**Components**
- A credence market has a hidden attribute, a seller with private information, buyers who cannot check it, and an assurance system that stands between them.
- Schrobback et al. (2023, PDF p. 1) describe two parts, *"the food supply chain and b) the attribute assurance system"*, linked in many ways.
- Credence attributes are added at every stage: processors supply qualities such as *"animal welfare at slaughter"* and traceability, and retailers use them *"as a form of product range differentiation"* (Schrobback et al., 2023, PDF pp. 7–8). How the assurance system works is the mechanism.

**Mechanisms**
- The main mechanism is to turn credence into something buyers can check. Labels do so *"by transforming former experience or credence attributes into search attributes"* (Caswell & Mojduszka, 1996, PDF p. 11).
- But a label is only a claim. A game-theoretic analysis of organic food found that *"repeat-purchase relationships and third-party monitoring are required for high-quality credence goods to be available"* (McCluskey, 2000, p. 1).
- Trust fills the remaining gap: *"consumers rely on trusted cues and information to help them verify the food quality and credence attributes they seek"* (Wu et al., 2021, PDF p. 1).
- Producers supply credence attributes because *"A potential premium price paid by buyers may be a key incentive"* (Schrobback et al., 2023, PDF p. 7), together with their own values. Which mechanism works depends on conditions.

**Conditions**
- Repeat purchase and reputation work when buyers and sellers deal with each other often; one-off or anonymous markets need third parties.
- In Viet Nam, consumers' trust *"will eventually be baseless if there is no third-party involvement"* (Le et al., 2020, PDF p. 2).
- The structure of the chain also matters: in China, information asymmetry and strict organic rules pushed organic milk into *"a highly integrated organization pattern"*, and *"a loose value chain discourages producing organic products because of transaction costs"* (Zhao et al., 2019, PDF p. 1).
- Public rules set the floor: governments set *"minimum benchmarks for food safety and health"* and labelling requirements (Schrobback et al., 2023, PDF p. 10). These conditions shape how credence attributes are studied.

**Measurement**
- Because the attribute cannot be observed, research measures beliefs and payments about it. Choice experiments, auctions and surveys estimate [[Willingness to pay]] for labelled claims; in one study, perceived consumer effectiveness raised willingness *"to pay a premium price for sustainable food attributes"* (Kovacs & Keresztes, 2022, PDF p. 1).
- Trust studies measure which cues consumers rely on (Le et al., 2020; Ngo et al., 2019).
- Fewer studies verify whether the attribute is actually present, which links to the main debates.

**Debates**
- The first debate is about who should assure credence claims. Economists see a public role, since *"Economic models of quality hit a dead end"* with credence attributes (Caswell & Mojduszka, 1996, PDF p. 9); private standards, though, are often stricter and set by retailers, processors and NGOs, who are *"influential stakeholders"* in these markets (Schrobback et al., 2023, PDF p. 1).
- The second is about cost and exclusion: private standards *"can be costly for standard owners"* (Schrobback et al., 2023, PDF p. 11), and the cost of proof falls on producers, which can shut out smallholders (see [[Sustainability certification]]).
- The third concerns fraud and [[Greenwashing]]: where premiums are high, *"one would expect for there to be some false labeling"* (McCluskey, 2000, p. 2).
- What remains unresolved is how to give credible assurance for sustainability claims at a cost that small producers and low-income buyers can bear.

## Implications
- **Viet Nam: safe vegetables.** Safe vegetables *"are typical credence goods"*, and Vietnamese consumers *"trust supply side indicators the most, followed by government certiﬁcation"* (Le et al., 2020, PDF p. 1). In Hanoi, *"brand trust plays the most vital role in building consumer trust in safe vegetables"* (Ngo et al., 2019, PDF p. 1).
- **Viet Nam: sustainable rice.** Consumers who paid a premium had *"greater trust in food quality certiﬁcation"* (My et al., 2018, p. 283), so the value of the credence claim rested on trust in the label.
- **China: organic milk.** Under third-party certification, *"A tight value chain is effective in the governance of organic food supply chain"* (Zhao et al., 2019, PDF p. 1).
- **Organic food markets.** Third-party monitoring is needed for honest organic labels, and the government can be one of the monitors (McCluskey, 2000, p. 2).

Together the cases show that a credence attribute has value only when an assurance mechanism makes it believable, and that in Viet Nam that mechanism is often a trusted seller or brand rather than a state certificate. For regenerative or agroecological products in Cambodia and Laos, whose main qualities (soil health, biodiversity, fewer chemicals) are all credence attributes, the design question is which mix of participatory guarantee, brand, trusted intermediary and public certification can make them credible at low cost.
