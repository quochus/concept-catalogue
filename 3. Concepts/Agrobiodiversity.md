## Definitions
The term agrobiodiversity took shape in the 1990s, when conservation science and agricultural development began to meet. Earlier conservation work had focused on wild species and protected areas, and crop genetic resources were mostly stored in gene banks. Scientists then recognised that *"protected areas are necessary but not sufficient"* to conserve biodiversity, and attention turned to farmed landscapes as places where diversity is kept alive (MAF, 2016, p. 3). In response, the Third Conference of the Parties to the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) *"established an Agrobiodiversity Programme in 1996"* (MAF, 2016, p. 3). Early working definitions were narrow and practical: Guo et al. (1996), as cited by Fu et al. (2009, p. 22), defined it as the *"management and direct use of biological species"*, including crops, semi-domesticated and wild species. Ecologists added a second idea that still shapes the field: in a farm system, *"The planned diversity is the suite of plants and livestock deliberately retained"* by the farmer, and this planned component *"strongly influences the nature of the associated biota"* such as soil organisms, pollinators and natural enemies (Swift et al., 2004, p. 121).

Because the CBD wanted one concept that could guide policy across crops, livestock, fish and forests, the meaning then widened. The CBD definition covers *"all components of biological diversity of relevance to food and agriculture"*, and also the diversity that keeps agro-ecosystems productive (MAF, 2016, p. 2). This prompted a shift from a list of species to a view of agrobiodiversity as a product of people and environments: it is *"the outcome of the interactions among genetic resources, the environment and the management systems"* that farmers use (MAF, 2016, p. 2). The CBD framing therefore includes five dimensions: genetic resources, the organisms that support [[Ecosystem services]], abiotic factors, socio-economic and cultural factors, and traditional knowledge (MAF, 2016, pp. 2–3). A third shift came in the 2010s, when the concept moved into food systems and nutrition debates. Global analyses showed that national food supplies were becoming *"species-rich in regard to measured crops at the national level, but species-poor globally"*, pointing to the *"establishment of a global standard food supply"* (Khoury et al., 2014, p. 4001). This turned agrobiodiversity from a conservation topic into a food security and diet topic.

Today the most used definitions combine these strands. Jones et al. (2021, p. 712) describe agrobiodiversity as *"the domesticated and undomesticated plants, animals and microorganisms that contribute to food and agriculture"*, including those that provide pollination, nutrient cycling and pest control. They also stress that *"agrobiodiversity exists on our plates, in production systems and in conservation"*, which gives three places to look for it: consumption and markets, production systems, and genetic resource conservation (Jones et al., 2021, p. 712). The Lao national programme, a good example of policy use in the region, defines it simply as *"the variety and variability of animals, plants, fungi and micro-organisms"* used directly or indirectly for food and agriculture, and explicitly includes non-timber forest products (MAF, 2016, p. 2). Its main components are therefore genetic diversity (varieties, landraces, breeds), species diversity (crops, livestock, fish, wild foods, [[Neglected and underutilised species]]), and ecosystem or landscape diversity (fields, home gardens, fallows, forests, water bodies), held together by local knowledge and management.

Definitions still diverge, mainly by discipline and purpose. Ecologists stress function, and they warn that a concept this broad is hard to test: for a definition *"as broad and inclusive as this"*, Swift et al. (2004, p. 115) note that *"it is highly unlikely that any clear and precise statements"* about biodiversity and function can be made. Nutrition researchers stress the foods that reach diets, so they measure species richness on farms and in markets. Geographers and political ecologists stress people and landscapes: agrobiodiversity is the *"resilience-enhancing biodiversity of food and agriculture"* that is carried by informal food chains and seed systems (Zimmerer & de Haan, 2020, p. 891). Policy documents, like the Lao programme, use the widest version because it lets one plan cover crops, livestock, fish and forests. These differences matter in practice, since a narrow genetic definition points to gene banks and breeding, while a food-system definition points to markets, diets and procurement.

Agrobiodiversity should not be confused with general [[Biodiversity]], of which it is a subset defined by its link to food and agriculture (MAF, 2016, p. 2). It is also not the same as [[Dietary diversity]], which is an outcome measured in diets, or crop diversification, which is one farm practice that can raise it. [[Agroecology]] and [[Agroforestry]] are approaches that use and build agrobiodiversity, and [[Neglected and underutilised species]] are one part of it.

**Working definition used here:** agrobiodiversity is the diversity of domesticated and wild plants, animals, fungi and micro-organisms, from genes to species to landscapes, that people use and manage for food and agriculture, together with the knowledge and practices that keep it in use *(Claude's synthesis of Jones et al., 2021, and MAF, 2016)*.

## Features
**Components**
- Agrobiodiversity spans genetic, species and ecosystem levels, and includes crop, livestock, forest and fish resources and the diversity of *"species, varieties landraces and / breeds"* used for food, fodder, fibre, fuel and medicine (MAF, 2016, p. 2).
- It has a planned part, chosen by the farmer, and an associated part, the wild biota that the planned part supports (Swift et al., 2004, p. 121).
- It also includes the organisms that provide supporting services, such as pollinators and soil life, not only the foods that are eaten (Jones et al., 2021, p. 712).
- Socio-economic and cultural factors and traditional knowledge are treated as part of the concept, not as context, in the CBD framing (MAF, 2016, p. 3).

**Mechanisms**
- Diversity spreads risk: overreliance on one crop or variety makes food systems vulnerable, as famines and disease outbreaks in single crops have shown (Jones et al., 2021, p. 712).
- Diverse farms can feed diverse diets. In Guatemala, *"Higher food self-sufficiency, nutritional functional diversity and dietary diversity scores were positively correlated"* with crop and animal species richness (Luna-González & Sørensen, 2018, p. 2128).
- The diet effect works through markets as well as own production: across six African countries, *"markets and market access are more important for dietary diversity than own production"* (Nguyen & Qaim, 2025, p. 205).
- Seed systems keep diversity in use. In northern Viet Nam, growing more vegetables was linked to using more seed sources, and *"Intermediaries created intermediate seed systems"* that linked farmers to formal seed (Nguyen et al., 2026, PDF p. 1).
- Ecological processes link planned and associated diversity: the crops and livestock a farmer keeps shape the wider biota and the services it provides (Swift et al., 2004, p. 121).

**Conditions**
- Land and labour matter: in northern Viet Nam, *"Smaller crop land area resulted in less diversity"*, and women's experience in vegetable growing was linked to more diversity (Nguyen et al., 2026, PDF p. 1).
- Market integration can erode diversity. In Xishuangbanna, as rubber spread, the number of upland rice varieties *"decreased from seven in 2001 to one in 2004"* (Fu et al., 2009, p. 22).
- Distance shapes the farm–diet link: the association between production diversity and diets *"increases with distance from urban centres"* (Nguyen & Qaim, 2025, p. 205).
- Policy can help or harm. In Southeast Asia, some of the *"greatest losses of agrobiodiversity have resulted from national directives"* that aimed to conserve biodiversity, such as bans on swidden (Rerkasem et al., 2009, p. 356).
- Local institutions, such as seed exchange networks and informal rules, carry much of the work of conservation (Rerkasem et al., 2009, p. 357).

**Measurement**
- The Agrobiodiversity Index uses 22 indicators across three pillars (consumption and markets, production systems, genetic resource conservation) and scores status, action and commitment (Jones et al., 2021, p. 712).
- National food supply data can track richness, evenness and similarity of crop species over time (Khoury et al., 2014, p. 4001).
- Farm and household studies count species or locally named vegetables, or calculate nutritional functional diversity, and link these to dietary diversity scores (Nguyen et al., 2026, PDF p. 1; Luna-González & Sørensen, 2018, p. 2128).
- Data gaps are large: there is *"an absence of globally consistent data for several important components"*, including varietal, functional and underutilised species diversity (Jones et al., 2021, p. 712).

**Debates**
- **Farm or landscape scale.** One side holds that diversifying each farm improves diets (Luna-González & Sørensen, 2018, p. 2128). Others find that the effect is small and that *"higher diversity on each individual farm may not be required"* when village or district diversity and markets supply foods (Nguyen & Qaim, 2025, p. 205).
- **Diet or health outcomes.** Diverse farms are linked to more diverse diets, but in Guatemala *"higher dietary diversity scores were not correlated with better child anthropometric status"*; sanitation, education and family size mattered more (Luna-González & Sørensen, 2018, p. 2128).
- **Markets as threat or tool.** Market integration often brings monocultures (Fu et al., 2009, p. 22; Rerkasem et al., 2009, p. 356), while market-based projects for diversity products can raise incomes and conserve species if collective action works (Kruijssen et al., 2009, p. 50).
- **Who conserves.** Gene-bank and protected-area approaches meet a view that *"Rich agrobiodiversity is often managed outside agricultural lands"* by farmers whose expertise is *"neither appreciated nor acknowledged"* (Rerkasem et al., 2009, p. 357).
- **Unresolved.** It is still unclear at which scale (farm, village, landscape, nation) diversity should be raised to improve diets and resilience, and global data are too thin to track several components (Jones et al., 2021, p. 712; Nguyen & Qaim, 2025, p. 205).

## Implications
**Evidence overview.** The largest synthesis of field experiments combined 95 meta-analyses and found that crop diversification raises crop production (median +14%) and associated biodiversity, but effects vary by strategy, with variety mixtures giving the smallest gains (Beillouin et al., 2021, abstract). At national level, the Agrobiodiversity Index for 80 countries shows moderate status and action but *"a low mean agrobiodiversity commitment score (21.4 out of 100)"* (Jones et al., 2021, p. 712). A scoping review of school gardens and home-grown school feeding (*"124 articles from 35 countries"*) found wide benefits but also that *"agrobiodiversity is underused"* in these programmes (Estrada-Carmona et al., 2025, PDF p. 1). Evidence on nutrition is mixed: diverse farms are linked to diverse diets, but the effect is often small and does not always reach child growth (Luna-González & Sørensen, 2018, p. 2128; Nguyen & Qaim, 2025, p. 205). Evidence is thin on livestock, aquatic and microbial diversity, and on long-term outcomes. Viet Nam and ASEAN evidence exists for seed systems, market-based conservation and land-use change, and comes first in the table below.

**Cross-case lessons.** Across cases, agrobiodiversity holds when it pays or is valued: price premiums, collective processing and procurement give farmers a reason to keep local varieties, but a premium alone may be too small without improved seed and organisation (Hue et al., 2016, p. 75; Kruijssen et al., 2009, p. 50). Collective action helps but is costly, and *"Alternative interventions may be less capital intensive and possibly more effective"* (Kruijssen et al., 2009, p. 51). Land-use change driven by markets and by state policy is the main threat in the region, and diversity often survives in small niches such as home gardens and field edges (Rerkasem et al., 2009, p. 356). Seed systems and intermediaries are an underused lever (Nguyen et al., 2026, PDF p. 1). Policy processes that involve farmers, in what Bragdon et al. (2009, p. 10) call *"a participatory, bottom-up approach"*, can link on-farm conservation to national law, as the Tamxoan case did through variety registration. What the evidence does not show is a reliable path from more diversity on farms to better child nutrition, or clear long-term results of market-based conservation once projects end.

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** You define agrobiodiversity as biodiversity pertaining to agriculture, fishery/aquaculture and forestry; the CBD and Lao definitions also treat it as a subset of biodiversity covering *"crop, livestock, forest and fish resources"* (MAF, 2016, p. 2).
- **Extends.** Current definitions also include wild and associated organisms that support production (pollinators, soil life) and the knowledge and management that keep diversity in use, so agrobiodiversity is seen as an outcome of people and environments, not only a set of resources (Jones et al., 2021, p. 712; MAF, 2016, p. 2). It is also tracked on plates and in markets, not only in fields.
- **Challenges.** Your first take frames it by sector. The literature increasingly frames it by where it sits in the food system (consumption, production, conservation) and shows that the farm may not be the right scale to raise it for diets (Nguyen & Qaim, 2025, p. 205).
