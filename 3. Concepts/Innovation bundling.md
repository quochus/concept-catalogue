## Definitions
The idea of bundling grew from the observation that single technologies rarely transform food systems on their own. Barrett et al. (2020, p. 974) argue that the emblematic technologies of past eras *"would probably have failed to scale and achieve impact without less-celebrated, ancillary policies"* and sociocultural accelerators. Their first principle for transformation is to build *"socio-technical innovation bundles of mutually reinforcing technologies, policies, knowledge, social institutions and cultural norms"* (Barrett et al., 2020, p. 974). A longer report by the same expert panel gives three reasons why the *"socio-"* and *"technical"* must go together: they are *"not independent in their origins or impacts"*; *"rarely will one innovation be appropriate at global scale"*; and trade-offs *"foster opposition to innovation"* (Barrett et al., 2022, PDF p. 9).

Scaling science reached a similar idea from another direction. Sartas et al. (2020, PDF p. 3) argue that *"innovations cannot be usefully scaled in isolation"* and must be seen as an innovation package with a core innovation and *"complementary innovations"* that enable or constrain it. CGIAR took the term into its scaling work: adaptive scaling starts by *"identifying the socio-technical innovation bundles that fit"* a context (IWMI, 2021, PDF p. 1), and bundles can mix seeds, irrigation, agronomy, extension, finance and market services, for example *"bundling drought-resistant seed varieties with crop insurance"* (IWMI, 2021, PDF p. 4).

Definitions diverge on what is bundled. In the Barrett tradition, the bundle joins technologies with policies, institutions and norms. In scaling readiness, the package is a set of innovations around one core, assessed for its weakest link. Kohl (2023, PDF p. 3) separates two ideas that are often merged: *"We use packaging to refer to funding and implementation models"* and *"bundling to refer to systems change"*. The differences follow purpose: transformation research asks what must change together, project management asks what must be delivered together.

Innovation bundling should be kept apart from its neighbours. [[Scaling]] is the wider process of reaching impact at scale; [[Scaling science]] includes scaling readiness, a method for assessing packages; a simple input package (seed plus fertiliser) is a bundle only if it is designed with the institutions and norms that make it work *(Claude's note)*.

**Working definition used here:** innovation bundling is the deliberate combination of complementary technical, social, institutional and policy innovations, co-designed for a specific context so that they reinforce each other, offset each other's trade-offs and can be scaled together *(Claude's synthesis of Barrett et al., 2020, 2022; Sartas et al., 2020)*.

## Features
**Components**
- A bundle has a core innovation, complementary innovations (technical, social, financial or market), and the policies and norms that let them work.
- Packages differ by place and by group: *"packages can change over time and are likely to differ across locations"* (Sartas et al., 2020, PDF p. 3).
- In practice the complements are often unglamorous; one scaling project had to *"bundle the machinery with value chain strengthening, such as arranging for repair services"* (Kohl, 2023, PDF p. 14). How the parts work together is the mechanism.

**Mechanisms**
- Bundles work through complementarity and through managing trade-offs. Because *"single technologies inevitably involve trade-offs across multiple desirable objectives"* (Barrett et al., 2020, p. 974), complementary measures can offset what one innovation does badly, and *"Bundling innovations can address tradeoffs to bring all parties along"* (Barrett et al., 2022, PDF p. 9).
- At global scale, modelling finds that *"individual measures in isolation are insufficient to achieve high-level environmental objectives"*, while *"bundling measures produces co-benefits"* (Sundiang et al., 2025, PDF p. 1).
- In scaling readiness, the bundle is only as strong as its weakest part, so analysis looks for *"the bottleneck innovation that limits scaling"* (Sartas et al., 2020, PDF p. 4). Whether bundles work depends on conditions.

**Conditions**
- Bundles must fit their setting: *"Communities of practice specific to each AFS must co-create locally contextualized socio-technical bundles"* (Barrett et al., 2020, p. 975).
- They also need actors who think in systems. Woltering et al. (2019, PDF p. 1) argue that scaling should move *"from reaching many to a process aiming to achieve sustainable systems change at scale"*, which means changing enough key drivers that the system that once perpetuated a problem *"now instead perpetuates a"* solution.
- Political support matters as well, since bundles span sectors and budgets. These conditions shape how bundles are designed and measured.

**Measurement**
- Bundles are designed with participatory foresight and assessed with models or case comparisons. Backcasting from a shared vision helps participants *"anticipate tradeoffs for multiple food system outcomes and population groups"* (Remans et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).
- Global models decompose bundle effects into individual, total and interaction effects to show *"complementarities and trade-offs that emerge when multiple measures are implemented simultaneously"* (Sundiang et al., 2025, PDF p. 1).
- Scaling readiness scores each component for maturity and use (Sartas et al., 2020).
- At farm level, adoption economists test whether practices are taken up together: in Tanzania, adoption of stress-tolerant crop diversification, fertiliser and pesticides was complementary, so *"inter-related technologies should be promoted as a package or bundled"* (Ogada et al., 2021, PDF p. 1).
- Field evidence that compares bundles with single innovations remains rare, which feeds the debates.

**Debates**
- The first debate is about evidence. Bundles are harder to design, fund and evaluate than single innovations, and the evidence on which combinations work where is still thin.
- A bundle can still pay off when partly taken up: *"it generates significant benefits even if the entire bundle is not fully adopted"* (Kohl, 2023, PDF p. 12), but that also makes it hard to know which part caused the effect.
- The second debate is about vagueness: the term is used loosely for any package of interventions, and *"the meanings of packaging and bundling often overlap"* (Kohl, 2023, PDF p. 14).
- The third is about priorities: the expert panel argues that bundles should focus *"on post-farmgate institutions and practices"* (Barrett et al., 2022, PDF p. 9), while most projects still bundle on-farm technologies.
- What remains unresolved is how to choose a bundle in advance, rather than discovering it after the fact.

## Implications
- **India: precision agronomy for cereal systems.** *"Bundling of conservation agriculture (CA) with sub-surface drip irrigation"* raised rice–wheat *"profitability by 29.2% compared to farmers’ practice"*, but without a subsidy for drip kits it gave *"similar profitability"* to conservation agriculture alone (Jat et al., 2019, PDF p. 1). The bundle worked agronomically, and its spread depended on policy.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa: farmer-led irrigation.** Irrigation was scaled by *"bundling solar pumps, drip irrigation and conservation agriculture for vegetable production"*, then testing pay-as-you-go finance (IWMI, 2021, PDF p. 6).
- **China: Science and Technology Backyard.** The model *"brings together millions of farmers with university-based researchers"* alongside extension, input firms and local managers (Barrett et al., 2020, p. 975).
- **Global food systems.** Combining diet shifts, lower food loss and waste, productivity gains and mitigation could avoid *"50% of projected agricultural greenhouse gas emissions by 2050"* (Sundiang et al., 2025, PDF p. 1).
- **Backcasting use cases.** In the backcasting cases, a social bundle of youth ambassadors and women's groups *"integrated several technological innovations"*, such as food safety tracing and cold chains (Remans et al., 2024, PDF p. 7).

Together the cases show that bundles that work combine a technology with the finance, services and institutions that make it usable, and that they are assembled for a place rather than copied. For IP1+ work in Laos and Cambodia, the concept suggests pairing practices such as cover crops with seed supply, finance and market incentives, and using scaling readiness to find the bottleneck component (see [[Scaling]] and [[Regenerative agriculture]]).
