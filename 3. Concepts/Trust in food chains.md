## Definitions
Trust became a research topic in food studies when food scares and new technologies showed how far consumers had moved from the farms that feed them. Writing on novel foods in Europe, Brom (2000, p. 129) noted that most consumers have no direct contact with producers, so *"Trust is no longer shaped by direct human interaction"*. Consumer concerns, in his reading, are signs of lost trust, and keeping trust is a public task, not only a matter for firms. The problem trust answered was practical: how can people buy food whose safety, origin or production method they cannot see?

The answer drew on the sociology of modernity. Trust is *"a means of bridging knowledge and uncertainty"*: where people lack information, they invest trust so that their choices become manageable (Meyer et al., 2012, p. 635). Because personal ties no longer carry this load, the literature moved from interpersonal to institutional trust. *"Institutionalized trust is attained by government control of standards"* and of production methods (Brom, 2000, p. 130). Later work added trust in brands and labels as a third layer between the person and the state (Ngo et al., 2019, PDF p. 1), and trust between firms in the chain as a fourth, studied in marketing and supply chain research (Cadilhon et al., 2009, p. 139).

Today's core definition separates trust from its basis and its effect. Trust rests on beliefs about the trustworthiness of actors, and the three beliefs most often measured are *"beliefs about their competence, their care and their openness"* (Macready et al., 2020, PDF p. 3). Its main effect is confidence in the food supply: in a five-country European survey, *"consumer confidence is largely determined by consumer beliefs about the trustworthiness"* of chain actors, and above all about their openness (Macready et al., 2020, PDF p. 1). Trust is also tied to what people expect: *"trust is fundamentally founded in expectation"*, so an incident damages trust when it breaks those expectations (Tonkin et al., 2021, p. 703).

Definitions diverge on how conscious trust is. For Brom (2000, p. 132), following Lagerspetz, *"Trust is not something we normally decide on"*; it is a background attitude, not a calculation of risk. Meyer et al. (2012, p. 635) treat it as a reflexive choice, which a consumer can make only with some knowledge; without it, people are not trusting but dependent. The difference follows discipline: ethics and sociology stress trust as a relation, while consumer research models it as a set of beliefs that predict behaviour.

Trust must be kept apart from its neighbours. [[Credence attributes]] are the problem: qualities buyers cannot verify. [[Sustainability certification]] and [[Participatory guarantee systems]] are institutions that try to make trust well-founded. Confidence is the outcome, *"related to a record of past performance"* (Macready et al., 2020, PDF p. 3), while trust also involves judgements about intentions. [[Connectivity]] describes the links through which trust is built.

**Working definition used here:** trust in food chains is the willingness of consumers and chain actors to rely on producers, traders, retailers, certifiers and the state for food qualities they cannot check themselves, based on beliefs about those actors' competence, care and openness *(Claude's synthesis of Macready et al., 2020; Brom, 2000; Meyer et al., 2012)*.

## Features
**Components**
- The literature points to four layers of trust *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: (1) interpersonal trust in a known seller or farmer; (2) system or institutional trust in regulators and the food system as a whole; (3) trust in brands, labels and certificates; and (4) trust between business partners along the chain.
- Each layer can substitute for or support the others: in Hanoi, brand trust mediates between system trust and trust in *"safe vegetables"* (Ngo et al., 2019, PDF p. 1). How these layers produce effects is the next question.

**Mechanisms**
- Trust works by standing in for knowledge. *"As people cannot recognize the characteristics of safe food directly"*, they rely on chain actors or on signals such as brands (Ngo et al., 2019, PDF p. 1).
- *"Trust in food chain actors could help consumers compensate for their lack of knowledge"* of how food is made (Macready et al., 2020, PDF p. 2), which lets them act on preferences for safe, healthy or sustainable food.
- Between firms, trust lowers the cost of exchange and holds relationships together: traders in Ho Chi Minh City stay with regular partners because of *"the difficulty and time needed to build trust between trader[s]"* (Cadilhon et al., 2009, p. 141).
- Trust also has a price. Vegetable farmers in northern Viet Nam would *"request a 21% premium to accept selling to an unknown buyer"* (Enthoven & Van den Broeck, 2021, PDF p. 1). These mechanisms depend on conditions.

**Conditions**
- Trust in signals is useful only when it is well-founded. In credence-good markets, trust can be sustained only with *"the involvement of a credible third party, often the government"* (Le et al., 2020, PDF p. 2).
- Where that party is weak, sellers can mislabel or mix products, and trust shifts back to personal relations.
- Experience matters too: rural Australians who produce some of their own food trust it more, because *"Consumers’ embeddedness in food production impacts their trust in food"* (Meyer et al., 2012, p. 634). Country context shapes which actors are trusted, so measurement has to be specific.

**Measurement**
- Most studies measure trust with survey scales. Macready et al. (2020, PDF pp. 3–4) rate each chain actor on competence, care and openness and link these to confidence in five dimensions of food integrity: health, safety, sustainability, authenticity and taste.
- Other designs rank cues by how far consumers trust them (Le et al., 2020), test paths between system and brand trust with structural equation models (Ngo et al., 2019), or put a price on trust through choice experiments (Enthoven & Van den Broeck, 2021).
- Qualitative work traces how people build trust in daily shopping (Figuié et al., 2019). Measurement choices feed into the debates.

**Debates**
- One debate is about where trust should sit. Some see a shift from personal to institutional trust as the price of modern food systems; others argue for re-embedding consumers so that they can trust reflexively rather than depend (Meyer et al., 2012, p. 635).
- A second is about misplaced trust. In Viet Nam, *"an excessive trust is granted to supermarket quality"* even where it is not safer than wet markets (Figuié et al., 2019, p. 158).
- A third is political: *"Public–private partnerships require trust, which itself relies on responsibility, accountability and transparency"*, and critics argue that *"Given its track record, Big Food does not engender trust"* (Yates et al., 2021, PDF p. 1).
- What remains unresolved is whether building trust should be a goal in itself or a means to make trust deserved.

## Implications
- **Viet Nam: from sellers to supermarkets.** Urban consumers in Hanoi long built trust through personal ties: *"We only trust sellers because they are familiar"* (Figuié et al., 2019, p. 153). With modernisation, *"consumers’ trust in food cannot longer be taken for granted"* (p. 146), and supermarkets gained trust from *"consumers who trust the quality of the products sold in them"* (p. 157), although supermarkets held only a small share of vegetable sales.
- **Viet Nam: brands and cues for safe vegetables.** In Hanoi, *"brand trust plays the most vital role in building consumer trust in safe vegetables"* (Ngo et al., 2019, PDF p. 1). A second study found that consumers *"trust supply side indicators the most"*, ahead of government certificates and information from friends (Le et al., 2020, PDF p. 1). Both suggest that the official certificate alone does not carry trust.
- **Viet Nam: trust between chain partners.** In vegetable chains to Ho Chi Minh City, all approaches to market linkage stressed *"a long-lasting and trusting relationship in the marketing linkages"* (Cadilhon et al., 2009, p. 139), and contracts with supermarkets served as proof of a relationship rather than its basis. Farmers' 21% premium to sell to an unknown buyer puts a number on the same trust (Enthoven & Van den Broeck, 2021).
- **Europe: openness matters most.** Across five countries, beliefs about openness, especially of food manufacturers, were most strongly linked to confidence (Macready et al., 2020, PDF p. 1).

Together, the cases show trust working at several layers at once and moving between them as food systems modernise. In Viet Nam, weak trust in public certification pushes trust towards sellers, brands and supermarkets, which is why guarantee systems and [[Connectivity]] matter for regenerative or agroecological products whose qualities buyers cannot see.
