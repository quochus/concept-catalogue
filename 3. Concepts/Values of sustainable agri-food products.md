## My first take
*Written by Quoc before reading the draft (2026-10-21). Never edited later.*

Values of sustainable agri-food products - agri-food products could be connected many values: economic, social, environmental or health, and others. Values could be measurable or non-measurable.

## Definitions
"Value" entered this topic from economics and strategy, where it is a property of goods and exchanges. Lepak et al. (2007, p. 180) "define value creation in terms of use value and exchange value": what a product is worth to its user, and what it fetches in a market. Value capture is a separate question, about who appropriates the value created. The problem this framing answers is distributive: along a food chain, value can be created at the farm and captured by someone else.

Two other traditions widened the meaning, each because the economic framing left something out. Food studies argued that a product's worth depends on what it tells the buyer: a product "embedded with information" about place and production can "command a premium price" (Marsden et al., 2000, p. 425). This led to [[Values-based supply chains]], partnerships with "shared ethics or values related to the qualities of the food" and to the business relationships along the chain (Peterson et al., 2022, p. 385). Environmental valuation, meanwhile, moved beyond intrinsic and instrumental values to relational values, which are "context dependent, often place-based, nontradable, and therefore largely not substitutable" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 31). See [[Relational values]].

The result is that "value" is now used in three senses that are often mixed up *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: value created and captured (economics); values attributed to food and nature (environmental and relational values); and values as principles that guide how chains are organised (values-based chains). For sustainable agri-food products the three meet. Credence attributes such as "sustainable", "safe" or "agroecological" create economic value only when they are signalled and trusted, and what is signalled reflects the values of producers and buyers. See [[Credence attributes]] and [[Connectivity]].

The traditions diverge on whether value can be priced. Economics measures it, usually through willingness to pay; relational-values scholars hold that much of it cannot be traded or substituted, so a price will always miss part of it. The difference is one of purpose: market design needs a number, while transition research needs to understand motives.

The concept should be kept apart from its tools and forms: [[Willingness to pay]] is one way of measuring it, [[Sustainability certification]] is one way of signalling it, and [[Values-based supply chains]] are one way of organising around it.

**Working definition used here:** the values of sustainable agri-food products are the economic, social, environmental, health and relational worth that producers, buyers and consumers attach to how food is produced and traded, including how that worth is created, signalled, captured, and the parts that markets do not price *(Claude's synthesis of Lepak et al., 2007; Marsden et al., 2000; Himes et al., 2024)*.

## Features
**Components.** The values attached to sustainable products fall into economic (price, income, risk), health (safety, nutrition), environmental (soil, biodiversity, climate), social (fairness, relationships) and relational (care, identity, meaning) groups *(Claude's grouping, building on Himes et al., 2024)*. Only the first can be read directly from prices. How the others become economic value is the mechanism.

**Mechanisms.** The mechanism is signal plus trust. Consumers pay for sustainability when it is credibly communicated: a meta-analysis finds that "the overall WTP premium for sustainability (in percentage terms) is 29.5% on average" (Li & Kallas, 2021, PDF p. 1). For traceability the premium reaches "around 32%", and "Consumers in developing countries show a higher WTP for traceable food" (Tran et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). Embedded information about place and production adds to the premium (Marsden et al., 2000, p. 425; see [[Connectivity]]). Whether the mechanism works depends on conditions.

**Conditions.** Two conditions stand out. First, the label alone is weak. Across developing-country cases, buyers sought health and taste, and "there is not a clear demand for ‘agro-ecological’ products per se" (Loconto et al., 2018, p. 2). Second, information must be layered: in Viet Nam the premium for sustainable rice rose as more information was added (My et al., 2018, p. 283; see Implications). These conditions shape how value is measured.

**Measurement.** Economic value is measured with willingness-to-pay methods: [[Discrete choice experiments]], contingent valuation and experimental auctions (My et al., 2018). Non-market and relational values are studied qualitatively: producers in women-led agroecological initiatives "frequently mentioned the relational values of meaningful occupation, care of nature" and social relations (Vizuete et al., 2025, p. 684). The two kinds of measurement rarely meet in one study, which is part of the debate.

**Debates.** The first debate is about markets. Values-based chains pay farmers premiums for products differentiated "by such values as quality", environment, place or relationships (Peterson et al., 2022, p. 386), but critics say they "focus too heavily on market-based differentiation" and so serve consumers who can pay (Stahlbrand, 2017, p. 77). The second is about leverage: values "may act as leverage points for sustainability transformation" (Horcea-Milcu, 2022, PDF p. 1), which suggests that changing values matters more than pricing them. What remains unresolved is how to reward the values that markets do not price without reducing them to a premium.

## Implications
- **Viet Nam: sustainable rice.** In experimental auctions, domestic consumers were "willing to pay a 9% price premium for certified sustainably-produced rice", and the premium "increases up to 33% when incremental levels of information" on certification and traceability are added (My et al., 2018, p. 283).
- **Viet Nam: the value of a trusted buyer.** Vegetable farmers in northern Viet Nam would "request a 21% premium to accept selling to an unknown buyer" (Enthoven & Van den Broeck, 2021, PDF p. 1): a relationship carries economic value for producers too.
- **United States: values-based supply chains.** A nationwide farmer survey found that farmers in such chains receive premiums "by such values as quality", environment, place and social relationships (Peterson et al., 2022, p. 386).
- **Spain: women-led agroecological initiatives.** Producers in the Madrid region valued their work for meaningful occupation, care of nature and social relations, values that no price captures (Vizuete et al., 2025, p. 684).

Together the cases show two sides of value. Where information and trust are in place, consumers in Viet Nam and buyers in the United States pay measurable premiums. Producers, though, value much more than price, and in Cambodia and Laos, where Quoc's IP1+ work sits, the question is which of these values local buyers will recognise and reward.

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** You list economic, social, environmental and health values and note that some can be measured and some cannot. The literature makes the same split between priced value (Li & Kallas, 2021, PDF p. 1) and relational values that are "largely not substitutable" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 31).
- **Extends.** The literature separates three senses of value (created and captured, attributed, guiding) and shows that sustainability creates economic value only through signals and trust (Marsden et al., 2000, p. 425; Tran et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).
- **Challenges.** Demand for the label itself can be weak (Loconto et al., 2018, p. 2), and critics question whether market premiums are the right way to reward these values at all (Stahlbrand, 2017, p. 77).
