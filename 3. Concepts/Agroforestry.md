## Definitions
Growing trees with crops and animals is an old farming tradition; trees were kept on farms because they gave fruit, medicine or fodder (Leakey, n.d.). Agroforestry as a modern science grew from a problem of land degradation in the tropics: as populations grew and fallows shortened, soils declined, and this downward spiral *"gave birth to modern agroforestry around the middle of the twentieth century"* (Leakey, n.d.). Foresters and agronomists began to plant trees on contours and in farm niches to control erosion and supply fuelwood, fodder and nutrients. ICRAF's working definition described agroforestry as a collective name for land-use systems in which *"woody perennials are deliberately integrated with crops and/or animals on the same land management unit"* (Leakey, 1996, abstract, reporting the ICRAF definition). This definition was structural: it said what the parts are and where they sit, in space or over time.

The meaning then widened, because a list of tree–crop arrangements said little about what agroforestry is for. Leakey (1996) argued that the ICRAF definition *"falls short of its ultimate potential as a way of mitigating deforestation"*, and proposed instead that agroforestry is *"a dynamic, ecologically based, natural resource management system"* that diversifies and sustains smallholder production for social, economic and environmental benefits (abstract). This shift, from structure to function and livelihoods, was prompted in part by farmer-made agroforests in Sumatra, where farmers had enriched fallows with useful trees (Leakey, n.d.). A second shift came with climate and biodiversity policy in the 2000s and 2010s. Agroforestry was re-framed as a low-carbon, multifunctional system and, more recently, as one of the *"nature-based climate solutions"* (Gonsalves et al., 2025, PDF p. 2).

Current definitions combine the two strands. Buratti-Donham et al. (2023, PDF p. 4) define agroforestry as *"a multifunctional land use approach"* that deliberately integrates trees or shrubs with crops and/or livestock to create ecological and economic interactions, and they add that this integration *"leads to increased agrobiodiversity, complexity, and thus resilience"*. The main components are therefore woody perennials, crops and/or animals, and the interactions between them, arranged in space (for example alley cropping, home gardens, silvopasture, shade-tree systems) or in time (improved fallows, rotational systems). In Southeast Asia, multi-storey home gardens are a widespread form; in Viet Nam they are part of the VAC (garden–pond–livestock) system (Mohri et al., 2013, p. e126).

Definitions diverge by discipline and by purpose. Agronomic and policy definitions focus on measurable arrangements, which suits subsidies and statistics; European policy, for example, supports some agroforestry types but *"No country assessed supports AF in its entirety"* (Buratti-Donham et al., 2023, PDF p. 2). Ecological and agroecological definitions focus on processes and see agroforestry as part of a wider agroecological transition (Buratti-Donham et al., 2023, PDF p. 4). Climate framings stress carbon and adaptation (Gonsalves et al., 2025, PDF p. 2). These differences shape what is counted: a timber plantation with intercrops in its early years may count in one framing but not in another.

Agroforestry should be distinguished from neighbouring terms. Forestry and plantations manage trees without integrating crops or animals. [[Agroecology]] is a wider approach of which agroforestry is one practice. [[Nature-based solutions]] is a policy umbrella under which agroforestry can sit when it addresses a societal challenge with biodiversity benefits. [[Agrobiodiversity]] is the diversity that agroforestry systems can maintain.

**Working definition used here:** agroforestry is the deliberate integration of trees or shrubs with crops and/or livestock on the same land, in space or over time, managed to create ecological and economic interactions that sustain production, livelihoods and ecosystem services *(Claude's synthesis of Leakey, 1996, and Buratti-Donham et al., 2023)*.

## Features
**Components**
- Woody perennials (trees, shrubs, palms, bamboo) integrated with crops and/or animals on the same unit of land (Leakey, 1996, abstract).
- Spatial or temporal arrangements, from alley cropping and silvopasture to home gardens and improved fallows (Buratti-Donham et al., 2023, PDF p. 4).
- In Southeast Asia, traditional home gardens are a major type: Mohri et al. (2013, p. e124) found that *"traditional homegardens maintain high ecosystem diversity especially in rural areas"* in Indonesia, Sri Lanka and Viet Nam.

**Mechanisms**
- Trees modify the farm environment: *"Micro-climates are modified, biodiversity is enhanced and soil health is built up"* (Gonsalves et al., 2025, PDF p. 2).
- Integration raises complexity and diversity, which supports resilience to shocks (Buratti-Donham et al., 2023, PDF p. 4).
- Trees and crops together reduce trade-offs between producing food and maintaining soil and water services, as meta-analytic evidence shows for yields, soil nutrients and erosion (Kuyah et al., 2019, abstract).
- Diverse products (fruit, timber, fodder, vegetables) spread income and risk for households (Catacutan et al., 2008, PDF p. 2).

**Conditions**
- Agroforestry is location-specific: systems *"must be developed according to specific contexts at the local level"* (Schaffer et al., 2019, PDF p. 10).
- Policy and markets matter. In Viet Nam and the Philippines, *"the benefits to smallholders have been limited"*, and in Viet Nam *"only commercial fruit and vegetable producers are actively involved"* in sector growth (Catacutan et al., 2008, PDF p. 2).
- Trees take years to pay back, so access to capital, secure tenure and stable timber prices shape adoption (Catacutan et al., 2008, PDF p. 2).
- Rural change can erode traditional systems, as markets and migration lead farmers to *"abandon their farmlands and homegardens"* (Mohri et al., 2013, p. e134).

**Measurement**
- Meta-analyses compare agroforestry with tree-less controls on yields and soil and water indicators; one synthesis drew *"1106 observations"* from 126 studies in sub-Saharan Africa (Kuyah et al., 2019, abstract).
- Ecosystem service assessments of home gardens use the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment categories of provisioning, regulating, cultural and supporting services (Mohri et al., 2013, p. e124).
- Policy reviews score which agroforestry types and stages (traditional, establishment, maintenance) receive support (Buratti-Donham et al., 2023, PDF p. 2).

**Debates**
- **Win-win or trade-off.** Meta-analytic evidence suggests agroforestry raises yields and soil services together (Kuyah et al., 2019, abstract); context studies stress that benefits depend on local design and that research and practice must co-learn (Coe et al., 2014, p. 73).
- **Scaling by package or by adaptation.** Scaling *"will not be achieved by wide scale promotion of a few iconic agroforestry practices"*, but by *"embedding research centrally within development praxis"* (Coe et al., 2014, p. 73).
- **Smallholder or commercial.** Policies often favour larger and commercial farmers, so agroforestry can widen inequality unless incentives target smallholders (Catacutan et al., 2008, PDF p. 2).
- **Unresolved.** Whether agroforestry can move from niche to mainstream depends on policy coherence; current European policies *"are currently not designed in a cohesive manner"* (Buratti-Donham et al., 2023, PDF p. 2), and evidence from Southeast Asia on long-term outcomes is thinner than for Africa.

## Implications
**Evidence overview.** The strongest quantitative evidence is regional. A meta-analysis of studies in sub-Saharan Africa found that *"agroforestry significantly increased crop yield, total soil nitrogen, soil organic carbon, and available phosphorus"* compared with tree-less practices, and reduced runoff and soil loss (Kuyah et al., 2019, abstract). Evidence from Asia is mostly descriptive or case-based, focusing on home gardens, policy and adoption (Mohri et al., 2013; Catacutan et al., 2008). Evidence on economic returns and on outcomes over long time spans is thin. No Viet Nam meta-analysis was found; Viet Nam evidence comes first in the table.

**Cross-case lessons.** Agroforestry is valued for diversity and resilience in all four settings, but its spread depends less on the technology than on policy, markets and knowledge systems. In Asia, traditional systems hold high diversity but are under pressure from commercialisation and migration (Mohri et al., 2013, p. e134), and policy benefits tend to reach larger farmers (Catacutan et al., 2008, PDF p. 2). In Europe, fragmented policy is the main barrier (Buratti-Donham et al., 2023, PDF p. 2). Across settings, local adaptation and co-learning matter more than promoting standard packages (Coe et al., 2014, p. 73; Schaffer et al., 2019, PDF p. 10). What the evidence does not show is how agroforestry performs economically over a full tree cycle for Southeast Asian smallholders.

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** You define agroforestry as combining agriculture and forestry; the classic ICRAF definition likewise centres on integrating *"woody perennials"* with crops and/or animals (Leakey, 1996, abstract).
- **Extends.** Agroforestry is defined by integration on the same land and by the interactions this creates, not only by having both sectors; it is also framed as a natural resource management system and a nature-based solution (Leakey, 1996; Gonsalves et al., 2025, PDF p. 2).
- **Challenges.** Calling it "solutions" assumes success; studies show that outcomes depend on local design, policy and who benefits, and that standard packages often fail to scale (Coe et al., 2014, p. 73; Catacutan et al., 2008, PDF p. 2).
