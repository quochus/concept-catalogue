## Definitions
Supply chain technologies is an umbrella term, not a coined concept, so it has no single originating author. Its oldest root is postharvest technology for perishable crops. Kitinoja et al. (2011, Abstract) framed the task for developing countries as finding simple, science-based handling methods and judging the *"cost effectiveness of scale‐appropriate postharvest technologies in each locale"* and crop. The problem it answered was loss. In Viet Nam, Cambodia and Laos, *"the average loss of the selected vegetables is about 17%"*, and *"farmers are the most vulnerable group compared to middlemen and retailers"* (Weinberger et al., 2008, p. 288). In the Greater Mekong Sub-region, *"Postharvest loss is due to lack of knowledge, techniques and facilities in produce handling"*, made worse by perishability and a hot, humid climate (Acedo & Weinberger, 2009, p. 103). Globally, fruits and vegetables are lost and wasted more than cereals and pulses at almost every stage of the chain (FAO, 2019).

The meaning then widened in three steps, each driven by a new problem. First came the *cold chain*. Because cold stores alone did not stop spoilage, attention moved to managing time and temperature along the whole chain, where *"temperature abuses above or below the optimal product‐specific temperature range occur frequently"* (Mercier et al., 2017, Abstract). Second came information and communication technologies. As mobile phones spread, they cut transaction costs and information problems and offered *"novel ways for improving agricultural supply chain management"* (Deichmann et al., 2016, Abstract). Third came the *Agri-food 4.0* agenda, which brings Industry 4.0 tools such as sensors, data platforms and blockchain into agricultural supply chains (Lezoche et al., 2020; Kamilaris et al., 2019). For fresh produce, Onwude et al. (2020) list imaging, spectroscopy, electronic noses, RFID, printed sensors and models, and name the Internet of Things and digital twins as emerging tools. Food-safety concerns, export standards and retailer demands for traceability pushed the last two steps (Yap et al., 2023; Fauziana et al., 2023). Lead firms downstream also play a part, as they *"design supply chain coordination strategies to induce farmers to adopt the needed technologies"* (Reardon et al., 2019, PDF p. 2).

In current use the term covers technologies applied after harvest at each of the stages that Kaur and Watson (2024) list (handling, pre-cooling, washing and sanitation, sorting and grading, packaging, storage and transport) plus the information flows that link them. Four groups can be distinguished *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: (1) **preservation technologies** that control temperature, humidity and atmosphere, from precooling and cold rooms to evaporative coolers, plastic crates and modified-atmosphere packaging, linked to [[Cold chain]] and [[Post-harvest handling and storage]]; (2) **monitoring and sensing technologies** that measure produce condition, such as temperature loggers, RFID and imaging; (3) **information and coordination technologies**, such as [[Traceability]] systems, QR codes, blockchain and market platforms; and (4) **automation and robotics**, such as mechanised grading and harvesting robots. Wageningen's food loss profile for Viet Nam frames interventions in a similar way, as combinations of hardware (packaging, cooling), orgware (arrangements in chains) and software (knowledge and information) (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 4).

Two framings pull against each other. A high-technology framing treats the chain as a data system to be digitised and integrated (Lezoche et al., 2020; Onwude et al., 2020). A scale-appropriate framing starts from smallholders' constraints and looks for low-cost, low-energy options; the AVRDC programme in the Mekong region worked by *"adapting simple and low-cost techniques and modifying and optimizing them"* to local conditions (Acedo & Weinberger, 2009, p. 104), and Defraeye et al. (2025, Abstract) note that of the many cooling technologies that exist, *"only a subset is sufficiently advanced to present commercially viable deployment options in LMIC"*. A third, critical framing asks who gains. Prause et al. (2021, p. 641) argue that digital technologies risk *"deepening certain features of the existing food regime such as the retail sector’s control"* over commodity chains. The split reflects where authors work: high-income chains with reliable power and capital, first-mile chains where most losses arise, or debates about power in the food system.

Supply chain technologies differ from production technologies such as precision farming, which act before harvest; from processing technologies, which turn produce into other products; from infrastructure (roads, grid power, markets), which they depend on; and from practices (careful handling, harvest timing), which need no new tool. They overlap with [[Food loss and waste]], but that is a goal, and technologies are one means to reach it. They should not be confused with [[Technology readiness]], which is a way of judging how ready these technologies and their users are.

**Working definition used here:** supply chain technologies for fruits and vegetables are the physical and digital tools used between harvest and the consumer to preserve produce quality (cooling, storage, packaging), to monitor its condition, to coordinate and trace its movement, and to mechanise handling, chosen and combined according to the produce, the climate and the capacities of chain actors *(Claude's synthesis of Kitinoja et al., 2011; Kaur & Watson, 2024; Onwude et al., 2020; Defraeye et al., 2025)*.

## Features
**Components**
- Basic preservation is still missing in many chains. Developing-country stakeholders *"often lack access, infrastructure, equipment, and capital"* for handling and storage, even as cold storage, artificial intelligence and tracking spread (Kaur & Watson, 2024, Highlights).
- Viet Nam's commercial cold chain is new and concentrated. *"Vietnam's cold storage business is quite young"*, and in the South *"36 cold storages account for 80 percent of the total intended capacity"*, mostly serving seafood (Ho et al., 2022, p. 48).
- Low-cost preservation tools tested in the Mekong region include plastic crates that reduced damage to tomatoes compared with cartons, a simple hydrocooler built by Vietnamese researchers, and modified-atmosphere packaging that cut weight loss (Acedo & Weinberger, 2009, pp. 105–106).
- Digital components are newer and often still at pilot stage. Kamilaris et al. (2019, Abstract) review blockchain projects *"with a critical view over the maturity of these projects"*, and Bac et al. (2014, Abstract) found that *"none of these 50 robots was commercialized"* among harvesting robots for high-value crops.

**Mechanisms**
- The first mechanism is biological: storing produce at controlled temperature and humidity preserves its quality and quantity (Makule et al., 2022). African indigenous vegetables kept in charcoal and brick evaporative coolers lost 64–75% less fresh weight and doubled their shelf life from 3 to 6 days (Elolu et al., 2025).
- The second is informational: digital tools reduce information problems that limit smallholders' market access and lower transaction costs (Deichmann et al., 2016).
- Traceability links origin to market. In Moc Chau, Son La, *"Farmers are now using QR codes to help trace the origin of vegetable crops"* supplied to retailers (Sen, 2017, p. 101), and in Viet Nam's fruit chain traceability is valued for trust, transparency and branding (Yap et al., 2023).
- Consumers can reward traceability. A meta-analysis finds willingness to pay for traceable food *"reaching a price premium of around 32%"*, and *"Consumers in developing countries show a higher WTP for traceable food"* (Tran et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).
- Monitoring links the physical and the informational: time–temperature data show where cold chains fail, so managers can act (Mercier et al., 2017).

**Conditions**
- Physical technologies depend on energy, climate and capital. Defraeye et al. (2023, Abstract) state that *"Unsatisfactory evaporative cooler performance is a potential cause for farmers' limited adoption"*, and map where coolers give useful gains. Without financial help, smallholders may not be able to bear the upfront costs of loss reduction (FAO, 2019).
- Business models matter as much as hardware. In Viet Nam, *"more than 90% of agricultural products transported to agricultural wholesale markets"* move without refrigerated transport or cold storage (Ho et al., 2022, p. 48), and *"Shippers consider the cold-chain as an expense, rather than an added value"* (p. 49).
- Knowledge and intermediaries shape uptake. AVRDC's postharvest training in Cambodia, Laos and Viet Nam found that *"Technology adoption was highest in Vietnam (60%) and lowest in Cambodia (27%)"*, and that *"market intermediaries have a strong influence on technology dissemination"* (Acedo et al., 2012, p. 47).
- Digital tools need skills, organisation and governance. In the Vietnamese fruit chain, barriers include *"a lack of digital literacy amongst the stakeholders, poor organisational culture"*, while cooperatives help (Yap et al., 2023, Abstract). Conditions differ by actor: in Indonesia's mangosteen chain, *"capabilities, infrastructure, and financial access were the critical factors for farmers"*, whereas exporters followed buyer specifications and food-safety standards (Fauziana et al., 2023, Abstract).
- Costs fall unevenly. In Moc Chau, *"To use a QR code involves additional cost"*, so farmers used QR labels only for retailers that asked for them (Sen, 2017, p. 103).

**Measurement**
- Physical performance is measured as temperature drop, humidity and added shelf life; Defraeye et al. (2023) estimate gains of roughly 2–15 days for apple, banana, mango and tomato in temperate conditions.
- Loss and economic outcomes are measured as loss reduction and payback. A passive evaporative cooling blanket in Kenya cut postharvest losses *"by up to 45 %"* and paid back in under three months (Wittkamp et al., 2025, Abstract). AVRDC adopters reported loss reductions of about tenfold and higher sales and income (Acedo et al., 2012).
- Loss baselines are measured by surveys of chain actors (Weinberger et al., 2008) and by national hotspot profiles; for Viet Nam, fruits and vegetables rank among the main food loss and waste hotspots (Axmann et al., 2024).
- Adoption is measured through intention and readiness surveys, often with the technology–organisation–environment (TOE) framework or technology readiness scales (Yap et al., 2023; Rifani et al., 2026), and sometimes with TRL ratings (Hayuningtyas & Djatna, 2018); see [[Technology readiness]].

**Debates**
- High-tech versus appropriate technology: some reviews call for IoT, digital twins and integrated cold chains (Onwude et al., 2020), while others argue that alternatives to mechanical refrigeration may suit LMIC better (Defraeye et al., 2025), and Patel et al. (2022, Abstract) observe that evaporative cooling *"did not make a dent in the production catchment rural areas"* of India despite many designs.
- Blockchain promise versus practice: Feng et al. (2020, Abstract) argue that blockchain fixes a weakness, since *"most of the IoT solutions rely on the centralized server-client paradigm"*; Menon and Jain (2024, Abstract) urge readers to *"sift through the hype and scrutinize limitations of the technology"*; and in Peruvian cocoa, digital tracing required *"investments that do not seem justified by corresponding increases in income"* (Steinke et al., 2024, Abstract).
- Inclusion and power: traceability standards set in the North can exclude smallholders, since *"For many African smallholders, those standards are a costly burden anyway"* (van Vark et al., 2013, p. 6). Digital tools *"may deskill or displace farmers and farm workers and exclude or discriminate"* against people who are not digitally literate (Klerkx et al., 2019, PDF p. 4), and may strengthen retailer control (Prause et al., 2021).
- Loss reduction versus environmental cost: refrigeration that prevents loss also emits greenhouse gases, and packaging can add plastic pollution (FAO, 2019).
- Unresolved: how to sequence investment, that is, whether to build integrated cold chains first or to spread decentralised, low-energy tools and add digital layers later, and who should pay for each.

## Implications
**Evidence overview.** The strongest syntheses are reviews, plus one meta-analysis on the demand side. A scoping review of 385 articles on developing countries found that newer technologies can improve produce quality but face *"financial, social, and regulatory obstacles limiting uptake"* (Kaur & Watson, 2024, Highlights). A review of fresh-produce cold chains still reports losses of about 50% during packaging, precooling, transport and storage (Onwude et al., 2020). Reviews of digital tools reach a similar verdict on scale: digital dividends *"have often not scaled up to the extent expected"* (Deichmann et al., 2016, Abstract), and in Africa *"the prevailing disconnect between pilots and scale-ups merits further evaluation"* (Abate et al., 2023, p. 1). The meta-analysis by Tran et al. (2024) shows that consumers will pay more for traceable food, which supports a business case for traceability where premiums reach farmers. No meta-analysis of the effect of supply chain technologies on fruit and vegetable losses was found.

**Cross-case lessons.** Technical performance was rarely the binding constraint for simple preservation tools: crates, precooling and evaporative coolers reduced losses where they fitted the produce and climate (Acedo et al., 2012; Wittkamp et al., 2025), yet uptake stalled where knowledge, finance, intermediaries and buyer demand were missing (Patel et al., 2022; Ho et al., 2022). For traceability, the business case decided: farmers adopted QR codes or blockchain where a retailer or exporter asked for them and paid for the quality (Sen, 2017; Fauziana et al., 2023), but costs were often not repaid at farm level elsewhere (Steinke et al., 2024). Intermediaries and cooperatives appear again and again as the link that turns a technology into a working chain (Acedo et al., 2012; Yap et al., 2023). Automation for fresh produce remains the least mature group (Bac et al., 2014). What the evidence does not show is long-run impact at scale: most studies are pilots, trials or intention surveys *(Claude's synthesis)*. For programme design, this suggests pairing each technology with a readiness assessment by actor and chain stage (see [[Technology readiness]]) and budgeting for the complementary pieces, such as training, finance, aggregation and buyer agreements, as part of the package.
