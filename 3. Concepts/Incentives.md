## Definitions
Incentives are an old idea in economics, but their use as a tool for sustainable food systems grew from two problems. Many sustainable practices pay off slowly or benefit others, so farmers have little reason to adopt them; and buyers cannot see how food is produced, so markets do not reward good practice. Public policy first answered with subsidies and regulation. As the limits of state regulation became clear, there was *"a shift toward regulation through private incentive mechanisms"* in value chains (Tolentino-Zondervan et al., 2016, p. 264).

The concept then widened. Development and agricultural economists began to classify incentives by who offers them and how. Piñeiro et al. (2020, p. 810) distinguish market-based incentives, which work *"by providing economic incentives through market signals"* such as prices, subsidies and transfers; non-market incentives such as training and technical support; and regulatory and cross-compliance measures that link payments to standards. FAO's work on innovative markets added a further idea: that markets themselves, through new institutions such as participatory guarantees and short chains, can act as incentives for sustainable practice (Loconto & Vicovaro, 2016, PDF p. 1).

In current use, an incentive is anything that changes the costs or rewards of a choice so that an actor is more likely to make it. For producers, incentives include price premiums, contracts, input support, payments and access to markets; for consumers, price reductions, vouchers, rewards and information that changes perceived value *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*. The key test in practice is whether incentives produce adoption that lasts. Piñeiro et al. (2020, p. 809) find that *"programmes linked to short-term economic benefit have a higher adoption rate than those aimed solely at providing an ecological service"*.

Definitions diverge on how broad the term should be. Economists often limit incentives to monetary rewards and penalties; governance scholars include any mechanism that aligns the interests of actors, including information and reputation. Bailey et al. (2016, p. 141) use principal–agent theory and speak of an *"incentive gap"* when the actors who want an outcome cannot reward those who must deliver it.

Incentives need to be kept apart from their neighbours. [[Nudges]] change how a choice is presented but not its costs; price changes *"provide a direct economic incentive"* and are therefore not nudges (Cadario & Chandon, 2020, PDF p. 2). [[Sustainability certification]] is one channel for market incentives, through premiums. [[Public procurement]] is a demand-side incentive from the state. [[Payment for ecosystem services]] are a specific type of conditional payment.

**Working definition used here:** incentives are rewards and penalties, economic, regulatory or social, that change the costs and benefits of a choice so that farmers, firms or consumers adopt more sustainable practices or products *(Claude's synthesis of Piñeiro et al., 2020; Loconto & Vicovaro, 2016)*.

## Features
**Components**
- Incentives can be sorted along three lines *(Claude's grouping, building on Piñeiro et al., 2020)*: (1) the type, market, non-market or regulatory; (2) the target, producers, intermediaries or consumers; and (3) the source, the state, private firms, NGOs or consumers.
- Market and non-market incentives *"tend to be the most prevalent mechanism"* in development programmes (Piñeiro et al., 2020, p. 810). The mechanisms by which they work follow from these components.

**Mechanisms**
- For producers, the main mechanism is profit. Linking programmes to *"economic benefits (productivity or profitability) is essential for farmers to adopt sustainable agriculture practices in the short term"*, while in the long term perceived positive outcomes for the farm or the environment keep practices going (Piñeiro et al., 2020, p. 810).
- In value chains, quality-focused buyers can reward suppliers with higher prices, but dependence on a few buyers can also trap them: Cadilhon et al. (2006, p. 112) suggest that modern retail could create *"a customer base willing to pay higher prices to rural suppliers for higher-quality produce"*.
- For consumers, price is a strong lever: in a meta-analysis of 14 studies, *"a price reduction of 20% resulted in increases in fruit and vegetable purchases by 16·62%"* (Huangfu et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).
- Incentives also combine with social signals, as *"moral green nudges outweigh financial incentives and amplify the effect"* (Rizzitello et al., 2026, PDF p. 1). Whether these mechanisms work depends on conditions.

**Conditions**
- Design and fit matter. *"Policy instruments are more effective if their design considers the characteristics of the target population"* (Piñeiro et al., 2020, p. 809).
- Support services matter too: agricultural extension services, public and private, *"have been shown to have a positive impact on adoption rates"* (Piñeiro et al., 2020, p. 814).
- Incentives also need someone to monitor compliance. In Indonesian tuna fisheries, *"information asymmetries cannot be overcome without the involvement of private actors"* (Bailey et al., 2016, p. 141).
- And incentives can be weaker than they look: in Hanoi, even with state support for VietGAP, *"farmers don’t seem having much incentives to apply standards"* (Pham, 2017, PDF p. 2). These conditions make effects hard to measure.

**Measurement**
- Studies measure incentives through adoption rates, changes in practice, and outcomes for income, productivity or the environment.
- Few follow the full chain from incentive to adoption to outcome: of 577 articles screened in depth, Piñeiro et al. (2020, p. 811) found only 44 papers *"that had a complete set of links"* across these pillars.
- Consumer studies use purchase data or experiments, but *"Few studies had maintained the price reduction for over 6 months"* (Huangfu et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). Weak measurement leaves room for debate.

**Debates**
- The first debate is about side effects. Market-based incentives *"can lead to negative social, environmental and economic changes that were unplanned"* (Piñeiro et al., 2020, p. 814).
- The second is about selection: payments or premiums often reward those who already comply, so producers who meet a standard *"have strong incentives to join"* without changing practice (Blackman & Rivera, 2011, p. 1177).
- The third is about inclusion: private incentive mechanisms can improve management but have also *"limited and marginalized developing country ﬁsheries’ participation"* in global chains (Tolentino-Zondervan et al., 2016, p. 265).
- What remains unresolved is how to combine market, regulatory and social incentives so that they reach small producers and last beyond the programme.

## Implications
| Case | Where, when | Incentive type | What was done | Outcomes reported | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sustainable rice label | Mekong Delta, Viet Nam, 2010s | Market (consumer premium) | Experimental auctions for VietGAP-labelled rice with added information | 9% premium, up to 33% with information on certification and traceability | My et al. (2018) |
| Tuna fisheries | Philippines, 2010s | Private incentive mechanisms | Compared WWF fishery improvement project, MSC certification and ISSF vessel register | Success depends on stricter requirements and on including more developing-country fishers | Tolentino-Zondervan et al. (2016) |
| Tuna fisheries | Indonesia, 2010s | Public–private incentives | Analysed the incentive gap with principal–agent theory | Private actors needed to overcome information asymmetries | Bailey et al. (2016) |
| Vegetable supply chains | Ho Chi Minh City, 2000s | Market (quality premiums) | Studied supplier dependence and quality incentives | Quality buyers may pay more, but dependence can trap suppliers | Cadilhon et al. (2006) |
| Healthy food price cuts | Mostly USA, 2000s–2020s | Consumer price reductions | Meta-analysis of targeted price reductions | 20% price cut raised fruit and vegetable purchases by about 17% | Huangfu et al. (2024) |

The cases show that market incentives can work for sustainable products in Southeast Asia, but only when buyers trust the signal and when someone monitors compliance. Incentives aimed at producers depend on profit in the short run, and those aimed at consumers need to be sustained. For regenerative products in Cambodia and Laos, this points to packages that combine a buyer premium or contract, support for the practice, and a trusted guarantee, rather than any single incentive. *"The future success of these incentive mechanisms depends for a large part on stricter sustainability requirements"* and on including more small producers (Tolentino-Zondervan et al., 2016, p. 264).
