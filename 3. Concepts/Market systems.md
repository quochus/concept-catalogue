## Definitions
The market systems idea came from development practice in the late 1990s and 2000s, when donors found that delivering services directly to poor producers rarely lasted. Its premise was that "the poor are dependent on market systems for their livelihoods" (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 5), so the system around each exchange, not the exchange alone, should be the object of analysis. It drew on two academic currents. Development economists had shown that getting prices right was not enough: "One thus has to get institutions and endowments, as well as prices, ‘‘right”" (Barrett, 2008, p. 300). And studies of collective action stressed the "pervasive imperfections" of markets in developing countries (Markelova et al., 2009, p. 1).

The standard definition comes from the Making Markets Work for the Poor (M4P) literature. A market system is "the multi-player, multi-function arrangement" of core, rules and supporting functions, carried out by firms, government, associations and civil society, "through which exchange takes place, develops, adapts and grows" (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 7). The 2015 operational guide restates it: "A market system is a multi-function and multi-player arrangement comprising the core function of exchange and the supporting functions and rules" (The Springfield Centre, 2015, PDF p. 7). The core is the exchange of goods and services between supply and demand; rules include laws, standards and informal norms; supporting functions include skills, technology, information, infrastructure and related services.

The concept has since been widened in two directions. First, market systems came to be seen as complex adaptive systems, whose resilience to shocks can be assessed. Resilience in this view is "an emergent property that is the product of a complex system" (Choptiany et al., 2021, PDF p. 2), and indices have been developed to measure it across market actors. Second, analysts paid more attention to how powerful actors reshape the system: supermarkets in developing countries treated local market institutions "as substantially ‘endogenous’", changing procurement and standards rather than adapting to them (Reardon et al., 2007, p. 399).

Definitions diverge on what a market system is for. In M4P writing, it is a means to poverty reduction, and analysis starts from the transactions of poor women and men. In resilience writing, it is a system whose capacity to absorb and adapt matters in itself. In institutional economics, it is a set of rules and organisations that shape transaction costs. Albu (2008, PDF p. 10) notes that the M4P approach tries to "connect economic, institutional and social dimensions in a joined up, systemic way", which sets it apart from narrower market analysis.

Market systems need to be kept apart from neighbouring concepts. A [[Value chain]] follows a product from producer to consumer; a market system adds the rules and services around each link. [[Market linkages]] concern one relationship within the system. [[Market systems development]] is the intervention approach that acts on market systems. [[Intermediaries]] are among the players that perform supporting functions.

**Working definition used here:** a market system is the set of exchanges, rules and supporting services, and the public, private and civic actors who perform them, that together determine how a market functions, how it adapts and whom it serves *(Claude's synthesis of The Springfield Centre, 2008, 2015; Choptiany et al., 2021)*.

## Features
**Components.** The market system has three parts: the core exchange, the rules and the supporting functions (The Springfield Centre, 2015, PDF p. 7). Rules are formal and informal: "Informal rules are generally a product of local culture and value systems" and shape whether formal rules are accepted (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 37). The state is part of the system: "Government is a key player in most market systems whether as regulator and policy-maker or providing supporting functions" (The Springfield Centre, 2015, PDF p. 15). How these parts interact is the next question.

**Mechanisms.** Rules and supporting functions shape the terms on which the poor transact. When information, finance or standards are missing or weak, transaction costs rise and smallholders stay out of markets; Barrett (2008, p. 300) argues that "market participation is a consequence as much as a cause of development". Relationships also hold systems together: in Vietnamese vegetable chains, "Buyer-seller partnerships represent a state of interdependence" based on cooperation, joint planning and trust (Cadilhon et al., 2009, p. 139). Groups and contracts fill gaps left by weak markets (Markelova et al., 2009). Such mechanisms depend on conditions.

**Conditions.** Market systems change when lead actors change their strategies. Supermarkets, for example, did not wait for supply conditions to suit them but reorganised sourcing and standards (Reardon et al., 2007, p. 399). Shocks also reveal how robust a system is: market systems interventions in Feed the Future "have contributed to climate resilience", but with "fundamental tensions between market system and resilience approaches" (Kuhl, 2018, p. 131). Because systems are complex, finding the cause of poor performance takes time: "Market systems are complex, so locating root causes can be difficult and time-consuming" (The Springfield Centre, 2015, PDF p. 17). These conditions make measurement hard.

**Measurement.** Practitioners map market systems by starting from the core transactions of poor groups and then identifying "the market system functions and rules that shape the terms of the transactions" (The Springfield Centre, 2015, PDF p. 18). Resilience tools such as the Market Systems Resilience Index survey multiple market actors to assess capacities to absorb and adapt (Choptiany et al., 2021, PDF p. 1). Yet "There is no systematic agreement on a single definition of resilience" (Choptiany et al., 2021, PDF p. 2), so metrics differ across programmes. Measurement debates feed broader ones.

**Debates.** The first debate is about boundaries: how much of a society's institutions belong in "the market system", and where analysis should stop. A second is about whose system it is. The M4P view centres the poor, but powerful firms and the state often set the rules (Reardon et al., 2007). A third is about goals: systems that are efficient and inclusive may not be resilient, as Kuhl (2018) shows for climate risk. What remains unresolved is whether the market systems lens can integrate environmental goals, such as regenerative production, as well as it handles poverty and growth.

## Implications
**Evidence overview.** Most evidence on market systems comes from programme reviews of [[Market systems development]], not from studies of market systems as such. Donor syntheses find that programmes achieve results but struggle to show change at the level of the system: market system analysis is "a ‘missing middle’ in the pathway from changes at the intervention level" to inclusive growth (Warner & Loveridge, n.d., PDF p. 7). Academic work adds case evidence on how actors reshape systems (Reardon et al., 2007) and how systems respond to shocks (Kuhl, 2018).

| Case | Where, when | Part of the system | What happened | Outcomes reported | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vegetable chains to Ho Chi Minh City | Viet Nam, 2000s | Core relationships | Long-term buyer–seller partnerships in traditional and modern channels | Commitment and trust helped manage risk and waste | Cadilhon et al. (2009) |
| Supermarket diffusion | Developing countries, 1990s–2000s | Rules and procurement | Retailers reorganised sourcing, standards and logistics | Local market institutions reshaped by retailers | Reardon et al. (2007) |
| Katalyst vegetables | Bangladesh, 2000s | Supporting functions (information) | Input firms trained retailers to advise farmers | About 3,000 retailers serving some 1 million farmers | The Springfield Centre (2008) |
| CAVAC | Cambodia, 2010s | Rules (pesticide policy) | MSD programme engaged government on pesticides | Policy change; but weak attention to gender | Warner & Loveridge (n.d.) |
| Feed the Future | Ethiopia and Honduras, 2010s | Whole system under climate risk | Market systems interventions assessed for resilience | Some resilience gains; tensions with growth goals | Kuhl (2018) |

**Cross-case lessons.** The cases show market systems changing through shifts in rules and supporting functions, often led by firms and governments rather than by projects. In Cambodia, "CAVAC I worked successfully to bring about changes in policies towards pesticides" (Warner & Loveridge, n.d., PDF p. 20). For IP1+, mapping the market system for agroecological or regenerative products in Laos and Cambodia would show which rules (standards, certification, procurement) and supporting functions (seed supply, information, finance) limit uptake, and who could change them.
