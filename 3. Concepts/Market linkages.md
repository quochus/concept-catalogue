## Definitions
Market linkages became a development theme when liberalisation and the spread of supermarkets changed how food was bought and sold in developing countries. The agrifood transformation was driven by rising incomes, urbanisation and liberalisation, and research on it produced *"the mix of cases of inclusion and exclusion"* of small farmers (Reardon et al., 2009, p. 1717). At the same time, economists found that higher prices alone did not bring smallholders into markets: getting prices right *"does not induce broad-based, welfare-enhancing market participation"* (Barrett, 2008, p. 300). The problem the concept answers is how to connect small producers to buyers who now demand volume, quality and reliability.

Practice gave the concept its shape. Donors, NGOs and extension services acting as *"linking organizations"* began promoting the *"linking farmers to markets"* approach, *"which usually involves organizing farmers into groups to supply identified markets"* (Shepherd, 2007, PDF p. 7). The aim moved from one-off sales to approaches that *"assume the development of long-term business relationships rather than support for ad hoc sales"* (Shepherd, 2007, PDF p. 9). Research then widened the focus from farmers to the relationship itself. In Vietnamese vegetable chains, Cadilhon et al. (2009, PDF p. 2) stressed *"characterizing the type of business-to-business relationship"* when trying to link farmers to markets.

Definitions diverge on where the emphasis lies. Agricultural economists speak of market participation, a household decision shaped by assets and transaction costs (Barrett, 2008). Practitioners speak of linkages, a relationship to be brokered and supported (Shepherd, 2007). Value chain scholars speak of governance and coordination between firms. The three are compatible: participation is the outcome, the linkage is the relationship, and governance is the way it is run.

Market linkages should be kept apart from their neighbours. [[Inclusive value chains]] describe whole chains designed to include the poor; [[Market systems]] are the wider set of exchanges, services and rules around a market; [[Contract farming]] and [[Farmer organisations]] are two common forms of linkage.

**Working definition used here:** market linkages are durable business relationships between producers and buyers, built through collective organisation, contracts, intermediaries and enabling policies, that give producers reliable access to markets *(Claude's synthesis of Shepherd, 2007; Markelova et al., 2009; Barrett, 2008)*.

## Features
**Components**
- A linkage has a producer side (often a group or cooperative), a buyer (trader, processor, retailer or public buyer), an agreement on volume, quality, price and timing, and supporting services such as credit, inputs, training and quality control.
- Linking organisations, public or private, often broker the first deal. The coordination they provide is both horizontal, among farmers, and vertical, between farmers, traders and service providers (Kilelu et al., 2017, p. 270).
- Whether a household can take part depends on its assets: those with *"access to adequate assets and infrastructure and faced with appropriate incentives"* engage actively in markets, while others largely do not (Barrett, 2008, p. 300). How the components connect is the mechanism.

**Mechanisms**
- Collective action is the main route: groups help smallholders reduce entry barriers *"by improving their bargaining power with buyers and intermediaries"* (Markelova et al., 2009, p. 2).
- In northern Viet Nam, pig producers overcame market barriers through *"institutional arrangements that build on collective action principles"* (Lapar et al., 2006, PDF p. 1), and cooperatives in general *"have been instrumental in improving market participation and increasing income"* (Ma et al., 2024, p. 1102).
- Contracts are a second route: a meta-analysis of contract farming found *"an overall pooled average effect size of 38%"* on smallholder income (Ton et al., 2018, p. 46).
- Standards are a third; in Viet Nam, *"Small firms particularly profit from the adoption of private international quality assurance programs"* (Mergenthaler et al., 2008, p. 221). These mechanisms work only under some conditions.

**Conditions**
- *"Trust seems essential for the establishment and continuation of linkages"* (Shepherd, 2007, PDF p. 10).
- Buyers' requirements set the bar: in Viet Nam, *"Poor farmers have no direct access to supermarkets"* because of safety, quantity and delivery demands, but they can supply indirectly through farmers' associations (Moustier et al., 2007, p. 2).
- Linkages also need flexibility, prior experience of collaboration, and an enabling environment set by government. Because these conditions vary, measuring the effect of linkages is not simple.

**Measurement**
- Studies measure market participation (share of output sold, net buyer or seller status), prices received and household income, and compare participants with non-participants.
- Selection is the main difficulty, since better-off farmers join first; reviews therefore keep only studies *"with an econometric design to reduce selection bias"* (Ton et al., 2018, p. 46).
- Even then, *"there are publication and survivor biases"* (Ton et al., 2018, p. 46), which leads to the debates.

**Debates**
- The first debate is about who benefits. Both biases *"lead to an overestimation of the average income effect"* of contract farming (Ton et al., 2018, p. 46), and transformation studies keep finding a mix of inclusion and exclusion (Reardon et al., 2009, p. 1717).
- The second is about reach: *"Linkage activities presently reach only a small proportion of farmers"* (Shepherd, 2007, PDF p. 12), and it *"is not yet clear whether linkage projects are replicable and up-scalable"* (Shepherd, 2007, PDF p. 11).
- The third is about causality: *"market participation is a consequence as much as a cause of development"* (Barrett, 2008, p. 300), so linkage projects may help those who are already on the way up.
- What remains unresolved is how to build linkages that reach poorer and remoter producers at scale, rather than replacing one group of suppliers with another.

## Implications
- **Viet Nam: vegetables and supermarkets.** Poor farmers could not sell directly to supermarkets but could reach them through farmers' associations that handled quality control, training and credit (Moustier et al., 2007, p. 2).
- **Viet Nam: smallholder pigs.** In Hai Duong, collective arrangements enabled smallholders to make *"a turnaround to overcome barriers to market participation"* (Lapar et al., 2006, PDF p. 1).
- **Viet Nam: safe vegetables.** A systems analysis named *"market actor linkages and quality control"* among the leverage points for safe vegetables in the north (Ha et al., 2015, PDF p. 1).
- **Kenya: dairy hubs.** A hub served as *"an intermediary institution that coordinates advisory services, input supply and smallholder access to markets"*; synergies emerged because it *"combined different types of horizontal and vertical coordination"*, though farmer organisations had capacity problems (Kilelu et al., 2017, p. 269).
- **Cambodia: vegetable cooperatives.** *"Producer cooperatives (PCs) could play a critical role in linking smallholder farmers to HVMs"* (high-value markets) (Tray et al., 2021, p. 905).

Together the cases show that in Viet Nam, Cambodia and Kenya the working linkage is usually indirect: a group, association or cooperative stands between small farmers and modern buyers and takes on quality control. For IP1+, the concept frames how agroecological and regenerative producers in Laos and Cambodia might reach buyers who reward these practices; see [[Market systems development]] for the wider system view and [[Credence attributes]] for why quality assurance sits at the centre of such linkages.
