## Definitions

The idea of food system transitions comes from the study of technological change. Geels (2002, p. 1257) defined technological transitions as "major technological transformations in the way societal functions such as transportation, communication, housing, feeding" are fulfilled. Feeding was in the list from the start. His point was that such shifts involve far more than new technology: they include "changes in elements such as user practices, regulation, industrial networks, infrastructure, and symbolic meaning" (p. 1257). To explain them he proposed the [[Multi-level perspective]] (MLP), in which change emerges from interactions between protected [[Niche|niches]], a stable [[Socio-technical regime]] and a wider [[Socio-technical landscape]]. He described mechanisms such as "niche-cumulation, technological add-on and hybridisation, riding along with market growth" (p. 1257). The problem he answered was why established systems change so rarely and, when they do, how.

The concept became "sustainability transitions" in the 2000s, and this shift changed its meaning. Historical transitions had mostly been emergent, but Geels (2011, p. 25) noted that "sustainability transitions are goal-oriented or 'purposive'", because they aim to solve environmental problems that markets do not reward. He named the domains where they are most needed, "such as transport, energy and agri-food" (p. 25). The widely cited definition followed: transitions are "long-term, multi-dimensional and fundamental transformation processes" through which established socio-technical systems shift to more sustainable modes of production and consumption (Markard et al., 2012, as quoted in El Bilali, 2019a, p. 1). Because agri-food systems differ from energy or transport (they depend on ecosystems, involve millions of small producers and touch health directly), food researchers began adapting the framework. Darnhofer (2014, p. 17) brought it to farming and stressed "the role of power in resisting and steering transitions". Lamine et al. (2019, p. 1) argued that most frameworks "tend to overlook ecological processes as well as social ones", and called for a territorial approach that joins sociology, ecology and nutrition.

The core meaning today has three parts. A food system transition is long-term: Köhler et al. (2019, p. 3) state that "transitions are long-term processes that may take decades to unfold." It is multi-dimensional: technologies, markets, practices, policies and culture change together. And it is a change of regime, the dominant set of actors, rules and technologies. In agri-food research, El Bilali (2019a, p. 1) finds that the "regime refers to industrial, conventional agriculture", challenged by niches such as organic farming, short food chains or new diets. The main components are therefore [[Niche|niches]], the regime, the landscape, the interactions between them, and the [[Transition pathways]] they produce.

Definitions diverge along three lines. The first is about whether transitions are described or steered. Historical studies, such as Nguyen-Minh et al. (2023) on Viet Nam, trace emergent transitions such as the move from collective to commercial farming; sustainability scholars study transitions that should happen. The second concerns place. The MLP was built on Western cases, and Wieczorek (2018, p. 212) notes that "regimes in developing countries are less uniform than in the Western world." Where traditional, informal and modern systems co-exist, there may be no single regime to replace. The third concerns politics. El Bilali et al. (2018, p. 1) report that transitions discourse has been "criticised for underplaying questions of power and politics", and Lamine and Marsden (2023, p. 1) call for "a deeper focus on power configurations and competing narratives".

Food system transitions overlap with three neighbouring ideas. The [[Nutrition transition]] is a dietary pattern: "the transition from traditional diets high in cereal and fibre" to Western diets high in sugar, fat and animal foods (Vorley, 2023, p. 14). It is one outcome of a food system transition, not the transition itself. Structural transformation in development economics, such as the rise of supermarkets and of the midstream, describes market change; Reardon (2015, p. 45) shows "this 'midstream' forms 30–40 per cent of the value added and costs" in developing-country food chains. And [[Food systems transformation]] is now often used for deliberate, large-scale change towards agreed goals. Many authors use transition and transformation interchangeably; on this page, transition refers to the process of regime change and its analysis.

**Working definition used here:** a food system transition is a long-term, multi-dimensional shift in the regime that organises food production, distribution and consumption, in which technologies, practices, markets, rules and meanings change together, whether the shift is emergent or purposively steered *(Claude's synthesis, based on Geels, 2002; Geels, 2011; and El Bilali, 2019a)*.

## Features

**Components.** The MLP gives the standard components. Niches are protected spaces where radical alternatives develop, such as organic farming, alternative food networks or new retail formats. The regime is the dominant configuration of farms, firms, retailers, policies, infrastructures and consumer routines. The landscape is the slow-changing context of demography, climate, prices and politics. In food systems research the landscape is the weakest part: El Bilali's (2019a, p. 1) review finds that the "landscape level is generally overlooked". Some authors add hybrid forms, "empowered niches which contribute significantly to fulfilling societal functioning" without yet replacing the regime (Nguyen-Minh et al., 2023, p. 2). How these components produce change is the work of mechanisms.

**Mechanisms.** Transitions happen when niche momentum meets regime weakness. Landscape pressure, such as a food safety crisis or rising incomes, can open the regime; niches then link up, gain market share or hybridise with regime actors. The direction depends on timing and on who acts. Geels et al. (2016, p. 896) list four pathways, starting with "technological substitution, based on disruptive nicheinnovations" and including transformation, reconfiguration, and de-alignment and re-alignment. Regime actors can also resist. Köhler et al. (2019, p. 2) point to "intensive agricultural systems and retail chains with locked-in production and consumption patterns", which create path dependence. Whether these mechanisms work depends on conditions.

**Conditions.** Food transitions are shaped by context. In low- and middle-income countries, regimes are often fragmented and informal, and the state plays a large role. Wieczorek (2018, p. 212) argues that such contexts call for adapting the MLP, not just applying it. Modern and traditional systems often grow side by side; in Indonesia "traditional markets still dominate and grow in parallel with the modern food retail sector" (Vetter et al., 2019, p. 2). Food security goals can work against sustainability goals: El Bilali (2019b, p. 559) notes that "the quest for food security (especially through intensification) may undermine transition". These conditions make it hard to know what to measure.

**Measurement.** Most studies use qualitative, historical case analysis. Researchers identify niches, regimes and landscape factors from documents and interviews, then trace interactions over periods of years or decades. Gaitán-Cremaschi et al. (2019, PDF p. 1) note that "an operational approach to characterize the diversity of food systems" was missing, and proposed classifying co-existing food systems as dominant, niche or hybrid by structural features and market share. Reviews are critical of measurement: El Bilali (2019a, p. 1) finds "poor empirical operationalisation of niche, regime and landscape concepts", and the regime is rarely described clearly. Durán et al. (2023, PDF p. 1) find that in Latin America the frameworks' use "has been more implicit than explicit". Measurement problems feed directly into the main debates.

**Debates.** Four debates run through the field. The first is about agency and power. Geels (2011, p. 24) listed criticisms including "(1) lack of agency, (2) operationalization of regimes", and replied that the MLP can include actors and politics. Critics such as El Bilali et al. (2018) and Lamine and Marsden (2023) argue that power should be central, not added. The second is about nutrition. El Bilali (2019b, p. 559) shows that "food security and nutrition are still marginal topics" in agri-food transitions research, although food security is the system's main outcome. The third is about geography: whether theory built in Europe fits countries where regimes are plural (Wieczorek, 2018). The fourth is about justice: who wins and who loses from a transition, a question taken up under [[Just transitions]]. What remains unresolved is how to analyse transitions where several food systems co-exist and none is clearly the regime, the setting that the [[Missing middle]] concept addresses.

## Implications

**Evidence overview.** Two systematic reviews map the field. El Bilali (2019a, p. 3) reviewed MLP-based agri-food research and found that "57 articles were selected for the systematic review", mostly on organic and alternative production niches challenging conventional agriculture. He concludes that the field borrows the MLP's generality but operationalises it poorly. Durán et al. (2023) reviewed socio-technical transitions in Latin American and Caribbean agriculture, starting from 2,868 hits and "reducing the list to 187 papers" (PDF p. 2), and found that frameworks were applied mostly implicitly. A separate review shows that nutrition and food security are marginal in this literature (El Bilali, 2019b). Across reviews the evidence is strongest on niche development and weakest on regime change, landscape pressure, consumption and health outcomes. Case evidence from Asia and Africa is thin.

**Cases.** The cases below follow the transition pathway typology of Geels et al. (2016), combined with the type of change studied (historical, niche-led, policy-framed) *(the grouping is Claude's, not from a source)*.

**Cross-case lessons.** The cases show that food system transitions are slow and uneven, and that the regime matters more than the niche. Where policy and retailers moved together, as in Switzerland, a niche practice became mainstream. Where policy backed the existing regime, as in Thailand and Egypt, niches survived but stayed small. The Vietnamese case shows a different pattern: a large, state-shaped historical transition in which smallholders and wet markets became the regime, rather than a niche replacing it. The Finnish case shows that purposive transitions meet political limits even where goals are agreed. What the evidence does not show is how transitions affect diets and nutrition, and how they work where traditional, informal and modern systems co-exist; the reviews by El Bilali (2019b) and Durán et al. (2023) both flag these gaps.
