## My first take
*Written by Quoc before reading the draft (2026-10-21). Never edited later.*

Scaling agroecology - Agroecology requires scaling - not only by expanding and replicating agroecological production, but also by changing norms, cognition, and institutions that foster systems change.

## Definitions
**Out, up, deep.** In social innovation, Moore et al. (2015, p. 75, Fig. 1) distinguish three ways of scaling. Scaling out is replication and reaching greater numbers. Scaling up is changing institutions, policy, rules and laws. Scaling deep is "changing relationships, cultural values and beliefs". Durable change needs people's "hearts and minds, their values and cultural practices" to be transformed (p. 74). Lam et al. (2020, p. 2) extend this to eight amplification processes. They define scaling deep as "changing values and mind-sets". Nicol (2020, p. 1) applies all three to agroecology in a city region.

**Taking agroecology to scale.** In the agroecology literature, scaling means more families and communities practising agroecology over larger territories, and more people processing, distributing and eating agroecological food. Mier y Terán et al. (2018, p. 637) identify eight drivers: crisis, social organisation, constructivist learning, effective practices, mobilising discourse, external allies, favourable markets and favourable policies. "Organization and social fabric are the growth media" (p. 637). Positive feedbacks and "virtuous synergisms arise among drivers" as scaling advances (p. 644).

**Scaling versus amplification.** Nicholls and Altieri (2018) prefer the term amplification. They argue that a key challenge "lies in the translation of agroecological principles" into practical soil, water and biodiversity strategies (PDF p. 2).

## Features
- **Farmer-led horizontal learning.** Scaling up "depends heavily on human capital enhancement and community empowerment" (Altieri 2012, p. 16), as in the campesino-a-campesino method (Rosset & Martínez-Torres 2012).
- **Networks as the medium.** "Multi-actor networks are pivotal" for agroecological self-organisation (Anderson et al. 2019, p. 12). See [[Connectivity]].
- **Markets and social infrastructure.** Local food initiatives that focus only on physical infrastructure risk losing their purpose: "investment in social infrastructure is crucial for maintaining the values" (Connelly & Beckie 2016, p. 49).
- **Dilemmas.** Upscaling "remains a fuzzy concept", and researchers and practitioners understand it differently (Augenstein et al. 2020, p. 143).
- **The state and co-optation.** When the state takes agroecology to scale, movements "face risks when they allow themselves to become absorbed" into state collaborations (Giraldo & McCune 2019, p. 786). Agroecology can be scaled in ways that conform to the dominant regime or transform it (Levidow et al. 2014, p. 1127). See [[Agroecology]].
- **Multiscale integration.** Large-scale adoption needs "a fully integrated multiscale systems approach" (Ewert et al. 2023, p. 351).

## Implications
- **Emblematic cases.** The farmer-to-farmer movement in Central America, the national peasant agroecology movement in Cuba, the organic coffee boom in Chiapas, Zero Budget Natural Farming in Karnataka, and Brazil's Rede Ecovida farmer–consumer network (Mier y Terán et al. 2018, p. 637).
- **City region (London).** Each scaling pathway needed something different. Scaling out needed land and learning platforms, scaling up needed policy change, and scaling deep needed "investment both in transformative learning opportunities" and networks (Nicol 2020, p. 1).
- **Laos.** A diversity of actor networks with different structures and governance supports innovation: "a pluralistic approach is desirable" (Castella et al. 2022, p. 14).
- **Leverage-point reading.** Scaling deep corresponds to the deep leverage points of values and paradigms. See [[Leverage points]] and [[Values of sustainable agri-food products]].

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees closely.** Your "expanding and replicating" is scaling out. "Changing norms, cognition and institutions" combines scaling up (institutions) and scaling deep (norms, values, cognition), as in Moore et al. (2015) and Lam et al. (2020).
- **Extends.** The agroecology literature puts social organisation and networks at the centre of scaling (Mier y Terán et al. 2018). It also adds markets and policy as drivers.
- **Challenges.** Scaling is not neutral. Scaling through the state or the mainstream market can co-opt agroecology (Giraldo & McCune 2019; Levidow et al. 2014). The term itself is fuzzy (Augenstein et al. 2020).
