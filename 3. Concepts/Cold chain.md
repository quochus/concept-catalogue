## Definitions
The cold chain grew out of refrigeration engineering and food logistics: once produce, meat and fish could be chilled and moved in refrigerated vehicles, the question became how to keep them cold at every link between harvest and the consumer. The problem it answered was spoilage. Perishable foods keep respiring and decaying after harvest, so the cold chain exists for the *"preservation and transportation of perishable foods in the proper temperature range"* to slow decay and keep food safe (Mercier et al., 2017, Abstract). In fruits and vegetables the need is greatest at the start of the chain: in sub-Saharan Africa most losses occur on the farm and in *"‘first-mile’ distribution"*, yet *"most developing countries currently lack the basic infrastructure and management skills"* to build integrated cold chains (Makule et al., 2022, PDF p. 1).

The meaning shifted in two steps. Because cold stores alone did not prevent losses, attention first moved from building capacity to managing time and temperature along the whole chain, since temperature abuse at any link undoes the benefit of the others (Mercier et al., 2017). Then, as refrigeration spread in middle-income countries and climate concerns grew, the cold chain was reframed as a sustainability problem. Chen et al. (2022, Abstract) define food cold chain logistics as *"a systematic engineering process involving the use of a low‐temperature environment"*, and stress *"the sustainability trade‐off between FLW and environmental impact"*. Practitioners now speak of *sustainable* cold chains, because *"Cold chains are currently largely dependent on fossil fuels"* and energy-intensive equipment (Peters & Sayin, 2021, Abstract).

The core definition today has three links: precooling soon after harvest, cold storage, and refrigerated transport and display, held together by temperature monitoring (Makule et al., 2022; Mercier et al., 2017). Its components range from large commercial warehouses and reefer trucks to small off-grid units such as solar cold rooms and passive coolers (Amjad et al., 2023; Defraeye et al., 2025), and to the sensors and data systems that record conditions along the way (Lamberty & Kreyenschmidt, 2022). It is one part of the preservation family in [[Supply chain technologies (fruits and vegetables)]].

Definitions diverge on scope. Engineers define the cold chain by temperature control and its effects on product quality (Mercier et al., 2017). Environmental scientists define it as a system whose energy use and emissions must be weighed against the losses it prevents (Heard & Miller, 2018; Chen et al., 2022). Development practitioners include low-energy cooling, such as evaporative coolers, that does not reach refrigerated temperatures but keeps produce cooler and more humid than ambient air (Defraeye et al., 2025). The difference follows the problem each group sets out to solve: product quality, climate impact, or access for smallholders.

The cold chain is narrower than [[Post-harvest handling and storage]], which also covers harvesting practices, packaging and non-cooled storage, and it is a means to reduce [[Food loss and waste]], not the same as that goal. It is not to be confused with [[Traceability]], although temperature records are often stored in traceability systems.

**Working definition used here:** the cold chain is the linked set of precooling, cold or cool storage, and temperature-controlled transport and display, with the monitoring that connects them, that keeps perishable food within a product-specific temperature range from harvest to consumer *(Claude's synthesis of Mercier et al., 2017; Makule et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022)*.

## Features
**Components**
- Precooling removes field heat soon after harvest, followed by cold storage and refrigerated transport; Makule et al. (2022) review the range of precooling and cold storage methods for fruits and vegetables in sub-Saharan Africa, with their suitability and energy demands.
- In Viet Nam the commercial cold chain is young and concentrated: *"Vietnam's cold storage business is quite young"*, and *"36 cold storages account for 80 percent of the total intended capacity"* in the South, mostly serving seafood (Ho et al., 2022, p. 48).
- Off-grid options fill the first-mile gap. *"Conventional cold-storage solutions have not taken off at the smallholder level"* for lack of reliable grid power, so decentralised solar cold rooms are spreading (Amjad et al., 2023, p. 635). Of the many cooling technologies, *"only a subset is sufficiently advanced to present commercially viable deployment options in LMIC"* (Defraeye et al., 2025, PDF p. 1).
- Monitoring links the parts: IoT sensors can record temperature and humidity along the chain, although *"large-scale implementation in fresh fruit and vegetable supply chains is still hindered"* (Lamberty & Kreyenschmidt, 2022, PDF p. 1).

**Mechanisms**
- Low temperature slows respiration, ripening and microbial growth, so produce keeps its quality and weight for longer (Makule et al., 2022; Mercier et al., 2017).
- Cooling changes markets as well as shelf life. In northeast Nigeria, *"cold storage significantly increased horticulture sales volumes and revenues of market agents"*, reduced food loss and raised prices received by farmers (Takeshima et al., 2023, Abstract).
- At scale, the gains are large: fully optimised cold chains could *"save over 100 Mmt of fruit and vegetable loss in South & Southeast Asia"* (Friedman-Heiman & Miller, 2024, PDF p. 2).

**Conditions**
- Energy and finance are the binding constraints for smallholders; the main operational constraint for solar cold storage is users' economic situation and lack of finance (Amjad et al., 2023).
- Business models decide use. In Viet Nam, *"more than 90% of agricultural products transported to agricultural wholesale markets"* move without cold storage or refrigerated transport (Ho et al., 2022, p. 48), because *"Shippers consider the cold-chain as an expense, rather than an added value"* (p. 49).
- Coordination between chain actors matters as much as equipment. In India, *"hardly any technology has been implemented in the Indian FSC"* for tomatoes, and the authors recommend *"temperature-controlled storage facilities and collaboration among FSC partners"* (Mohan et al., 2023, PDF p. 1).
- Willingness to adopt can be high: in Ghana, *"more than two-thirds of smallholder farmers were willing to adopt"* solar-powered cold storage (Kansanga et al., 2025, PDF p. 1).

**Measurement**
- Engineering studies measure temperature, humidity, time–temperature history and remaining shelf life; field studies show that temperature abuse is common at precooling, transport, retail and home stages (Mercier et al., 2017).
- Economic studies measure loss shares, sales, prices and payback, sometimes with quasi-experimental designs (Takeshima et al., 2023).
- Environmental studies use life-cycle assessment to compare the emissions of refrigeration with the emissions embodied in food that would otherwise be lost (Heard & Miller, 2018; Chen et al., 2022).

**Debates**
- Cold chains reduce loss: optimised chains could save large volumes of fruits and vegetables in South and Southeast Asia (Friedman-Heiman & Miller, 2024), and solar cold rooms raised market incomes in Nigeria (Takeshima et al., 2023).
- Cold chains add emissions: modelling for sub-Saharan Africa estimates that *"postharvest emissions added from cold chain operation are larger than food loss emissions avoided"* in scenarios copying North American or European chains (Heard & Miller, 2018, Abstract), and cold chains remain fossil-fuel dependent (Peters & Sayin, 2021).
- Alternatives to industrial cold chains: localised, less industrialised chains *"may save greater quantities of food than optimized cold chains"* for some foods (Friedman-Heiman & Miller, 2024, PDF p. 2), and low-energy cooling may fit smallholders better (Defraeye et al., 2025).
- Unresolved: which foods, places and chain links justify full refrigeration, and how to build cold chains that cut losses without locking in high emissions.

## Implications
Viet Nam evidence comes first; the other cases show the mechanism in different settings.

- **Viet Nam's commercial cold chain.** Cold storage capacity is concentrated in the South and serves seafood and exporters, while most fruit and vegetables reach wholesale markets uncooled; shippers treat cooling as a cost (Ho et al., 2022). The gap is a business-model and coordination gap as much as a capacity gap.
- **Solar cold rooms in Nigerian markets.** Seven small solar cold rooms in horticulture markets increased sales, revenues and prices and reduced loss, with costs recouped in a reasonable time (Takeshima et al., 2023). Placing cooling at the market, where traders aggregate produce, made it pay.
- **India's tomato chain (contested).** Cold storage and evaporative cooling exist, but almost none had been adopted; the authors argue that temperature-controlled storage only works with collaboration among chain partners (Mohan et al., 2023).

Together the cases show that the cold chain works when cooling sits where produce is aggregated and someone captures the value of longer shelf life; where no actor is paid for quality, capacity stays idle or is never built *(Claude's synthesis)*. For Vietnamese fruit and vegetable chains, this points to cooling at collection points and wholesale markets, paired with buyer agreements, rather than to on-farm cold rooms alone. The climate trade-off means that investments should also report energy sources and expected emissions (Heard & Miller, 2018).
