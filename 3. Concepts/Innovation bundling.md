## Definitions
The idea of bundling grew from the observation that single technologies rarely transform food systems on their own. Barrett et al. (2020, p. 974) argue that the emblematic technologies of past eras "would probably have failed to scale and achieve impact without less-celebrated, ancillary policies" and sociocultural accelerators. Their first principle for transformation is to build "socio-technical innovation bundles of mutually reinforcing technologies, policies, knowledge, social institutions and cultural norms" (p. 974).

CGIAR took the term into its scaling work. Adaptive scaling starts by "identifying the socio-technical innovation bundles that fit" a context (IWMI, 2021, PDF p. 1), and bundles can mix seeds, irrigation, agronomy, extension, finance and market services, for example "bundling drought-resistant seed varieties with crop insurance" (PDF p. 4). Kohl (2023, PDF p. 3) separates two ideas that are often merged: "We use packaging to refer to funding and implementation models" and "bundling to refer to systems change".

**Working definition used here:** innovation bundling is the deliberate combination of complementary technical, social, institutional and policy innovations, co-designed for a specific context so that they reinforce each other and can be scaled together *(Claude's synthesis of Barrett et al., 2020; IWMI, 2021)*.

## Features
**Why bundle.** Barrett et al. (2020) give three reasons: technologies need accompanying policies and norms to reach scale; "single technologies inevitably involve trade-offs across multiple desirable objectives" (p. 974), which complementary measures can offset; and different places need different combinations.

**How bundles are built.** Bundles are context-specific and co-created: "Communities of practice specific to each AFS must co-create locally contextualized socio-technical bundles" (Barrett et al., 2020, p. 975). Backcasting from a shared vision helps participants "anticipate tradeoffs for multiple food system outcomes and population groups" and choose combinations (Remans et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).

**Effects.** At global level, modelling shows that combining diet shifts, lower food loss and waste, productivity gains and mitigation gives co-benefits that single measures do not: "bundling measures produces co-benefits", including "avoiding 50% of projected agricultural greenhouse gas emissions by 2050" (Sundiang et al., 2025, PDF p. 1). At project level, a bundle can pay off even when partly adopted: "it generates significant benefits even if the entire bundle is not fully adopted" (Kohl, 2023, PDF p. 12).

**Debates.** Bundles are harder to design, fund and evaluate than single innovations, and the evidence on which combinations work where is still thin. The term is also used loosely for any package of interventions.

## Implications
For projects, bundling shifts attention from the single innovation to its enabling conditions. In sub-Saharan Africa, farmer-led irrigation was scaled by "bundling solar pumps, drip irrigation and conservation agriculture for vegetable production", then testing pay-as-you-go finance (IWMI, 2021, PDF p. 6). China's Science and Technology Backyard "brings together millions of farmers with university-based researchers" alongside extension, input firms and local managers (Barrett et al., 2020, p. 975). For IP1+ work in Laos and Cambodia, the concept suggests pairing practices such as cover crops with seed supply, finance and market incentives (see [[Scaling]]).
