## Definitions
The term comes from innovation studies, where research on technology transfer found a set of actors working between firms, researchers and users. Howells (2006, p. 715) noted that many labels were in use, such as third parties, bridgers and brokers, and proposed a working definition: an organisation or body that "acts an agent or broker in any aspect of the innovation process" between two or more parties (p. 720).

As innovation came to be seen as a system rather than a pipeline, attention moved from bilateral brokering to coordinating whole networks. Van Lente et al. (2003, p. 247) identified "a new type of intermediary organization, which functions at system or network level", which they called systemic intermediaries, and linked them to long-term transitions. In agriculture, the same shift appeared as extension broadened from technology transfer: "Traditionally, extension services were considered the main intermediary actor" (Kilelu et al., 2011, p. 85).

Sustainability transition research then asked which intermediaries speed up change. Kivimaa et al. (2019, p. 1062) reviewed the field, defined transition intermediaries and proposed five types: systemic, regime-based, niche, process and user intermediaries. They argued that "systemic and niche intermediaries are the most crucial forms of intermediary actors" in transitions, supported by a wider ecology of others (p. 1062).

Definitions diverge on neutrality. Many studies describe intermediaries as "neutral or honest brokers without clear normative interests" (Kivimaa et al., 2019, p. 1063), while transition studies find that many intermediaries hold strong goals and push for particular niches. They also diverge on form: some actors are set up to intermediate, others take on the role alongside their main work.

Intermediaries differ from [[Multi-stakeholder platforms]], which are spaces where intermediation happens, and from boundary spanners, a broader term for anyone who links organisations.

**Working definition used here:** intermediaries are actors or organisations that connect, translate between and coordinate other actors in innovation or transition processes, from single-link brokering to system-level alignment of networks, institutions and visions *(Claude's synthesis of Howells, 2006; van Lente et al., 2003; Kivimaa et al., 2019)*.

## Features
**Functions.** Intermediaries bridge actors "where direct interaction is difficult" because of transaction costs (Kivimaa et al., 2019, p. 1063). Howells (2006) lists functions such as foresight, information scanning, knowledge processing, testing, accreditation and commercialisation. Systemic intermediaries add the articulation of needs and options, the alignment of actors and support for learning (van Lente et al., 2003).

**Types.** In Kenya, Kilelu et al. (2011, p. 84) found "a typology of four innovation intermediation arrangements": technology broker, systemic broker, enterprise development support and input access support. Kivimaa et al. (2019) give five transition types by level and goal. Producer organisations, NGOs, research centres, extension services and private firms can all play intermediary roles.

**Mechanisms in scaling.** Different network roles carry different scaling directions: brokerage supports outscaling, institutional entrepreneurship supports upscaling (Hermans et al., 2013). In CGIAR scaling, "research-for-development organizations act as boundary spanners" that orchestrate partners (Minh & Schmitter, 2025, PDF p. 1). See [[Scaling]].

**Collective intermediation.** Recent work shows intermediaries often act together. In Spanish public food procurement, "collective intermediation is orchestrated by pivotal intermediaries" who mobilise others (Gaitán-Cremaschi et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).

**Debates.** First, boundaries: the literature "lacks clarity in how intermediation is defined, where it begins and ends" (Kivimaa et al., 2019, p. 1063). Second, neutrality versus advocacy. Third, funding: brokering is hard to pay for because its outcomes are indirect, so Kilelu et al. (2011) call for policy support to embed it.

## Implications
**What the evidence shows.** Reviews agree that intermediaries are important but not sufficient. Van Lente et al. (2003, p. 247) found systemic intermediaries "useful and necessary, but not sufficient" in a fuel cell partnership. Agricultural studies show that existing organisations, especially producer organisations and extension, often do intermediary work without naming it.

**Cases.**

| Case | Where | Intermediary type | What they did | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kenyan agricultural intermediaries | Kenya, 2010s | Mixed (22 organisations) | Technology, systemic, enterprise and input brokering | Brokering is pervasive and often a side activity | Kilelu et al. (2011, p. 84) |
| Vegetable producer organisations | Uruguay | Producer organisations (regime and niche) | Organic inputs, market access, knowledge sharing | "Producer organizations mostly function as implicit transition intermediaries" | Groot-Kormelinck et al. (2022, PDF p. 1) |
| Sustainable public food procurement | Spain, four cases | Networks of transition intermediaries | Coordinating procurement change | Pivotal intermediaries orchestrate others; change can start in regimes | Gaitán-Cremaschi et al. (2024, PDF p. 1) |
| Solar farmer-led irrigation | Sub-Saharan Africa | R4D organisations as boundary spanners | Aligning incentives and building trust in a scaling ecosystem | Orchestration enabled adaptive scaling | Minh & Schmitter (2025, PDF p. 1) |
| Conservation agriculture networks | Laos | Projects, government and farmer groups | Building actor networks around CA | A pluralistic approach supports innovation | Castella et al. (2022) |

**Lessons.** Intermediaries work best when they hold legitimacy with the actors they connect, as producer organisations do in Uruguay, and when several intermediaries play complementary roles. For IP1+ in Laos and Cambodia, this points to mapping who already brokers inputs, knowledge and markets before creating new platforms.
