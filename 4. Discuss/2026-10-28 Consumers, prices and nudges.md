> From Quoc's Gemini chat on scaling sustainable agri-food systems ([[Q&A for values, connectivity & scaling|overview of all 27 questions]]). **Main points** condense Gemini's answers. **Where I landed** gives Quoc's own replies, lightly edited for typos. Gemini's figures, cases and references are unchecked unless they link to Zotero.

## Question explored
How can sustainable and safe food reach ordinary consumers, not only an elite niche?
- **Q12.** In emerging economies with uneven purchasing power, how can sustainable and safe food overcome the price barrier and avoid becoming an elite niche?
- **Q13.** Beyond eco-labels and awareness campaigns, how effective are structural changes in the retail food environment (defaults, placement, store zoning, institutional nudges) in shifting habitual purchases? Quoc also asked for a list of all types of consumer and market incentives and nudges.
- **Q14 (as Gemini later restated it).** What is the price elasticity of demand for certified sustainable products across income groups in ASEAN, and at what price gap does sustainability stop influencing choice? Quoc asked what "elasticity" means and about Thailand's organic labelling and VietGAP.
- **Q15.** In fast-urbanising ASEAN societies, how can labour-intensive, minimally processed foods compete with cheap, convenient, heavily marketed ultra-processed foods?

## Main points
**Q12, the elite niche (Gemini).** If sustainable goods rely only on high premiums, they stay an urban luxury. Gemini endorsed Quoc's answer: lower the costs of production, aggregation and transactions, and mainstream sustainable practices into conventional chains.

**Q13, types of consumer and market incentives (Gemini)**
- **A. Economic and financial:** discounts, tax exemptions or vouchers for verified products; shorter chains (farmers' markets, CSA) that remove layers of markup.
- **B. Choice architecture:** eye-level and checkout placement; sustainable or local meals as the default in canteens; dedicated "safe food" or cooperative sections in supermarkets.
- **C. Information and trust:** simple public seals (CamGAP, PGS, origin labels); lightweight QR codes or origin boards.
- **D. Institutional demand:** procurement quotas for school feeding and hospitals.
- **E. Social norms and education:** food literacy in school curricula; campaigns that stress local health rather than climate.

**Q13, the evidence (Gemini)**
- Food choices respond more to choice architecture than other domains do (Mertens et al., 2022).
- Behavioural nudges (convenience, placement, defaults) outperform information nudges: "d = 0.39 vs 0.12" (Cadario & Chandon, 2020).
- Defaults have the largest effects, then the amount of a product available, then placement.
- Moving low-carbon options to the top of delivery-app menus cut carbon per order by about 12% (Lohmann et al., 2024).
- Eco-labels help, especially in simple visual formats (Potter et al., 2021).
- Stated willingness to pay for eco-labels averages about USD 3.79 PPP per kg (Bastounis et al., 2021), or about 29.5% (Li & Kallas, 2021).
- Limits: nudges cannot overcome a large price gap, and they should be transparent.

**Q14, elasticity (Gemini).** Price elasticity of demand is the % change in quantity demanded divided by the % change in price.
- Staples are inelastic (|PED| < 1). Gemini put certified sustainable food at "1.2–2.5".
- Better-off urban buyers pay a steady "15–30%" premium.
- For most households demand "drops off precipitously" above a "15–20%" premium, and above "30%" sustainability no longer matters.

**Thailand's organic promotion (Gemini, no sources).** Targets were set on paper without extension, bio-input supply or storage. Export markets did not recognise national and provincial seals. Consumers were confused by overlapping seals (Organic Thailand, Q-Mark, provincial standards). Unverified "organic" products flooded the market, trust fell, and farmers reverted to conventional methods.

**VietGAP (Gemini).** Gemini gave four reasons for slow uptake:
- compliance costs (records, testing, annual audits) are too high for micro-plots without a cooperative or firm;
- collectors and wet-market vendors mix VietGAP produce with conventional produce, so farmers get no premium;
- consumers distrust labels after enforcement failures and scandals;
- VietGAP produce goes mostly to supermarkets, while daily fresh food is still bought in wet markets (*chợ*) on personal trust.

Gemini's remedies were group certification, public procurement and verified "safe produce" stalls in wet markets.

**Q15, convenience (Gemini).** Ultra-processed foods win on time, shelf life and price. Pre-processing fresh food adds cost, labour and plastic waste. Gemini proposed moving cleaning, sorting and cutting from households to community or cooperative light-processing hubs.

## Where I landed
*My own conclusions.*
- Q12: Another dilemma. Price premiums cannot be too low, so the market for organic and sustainable products inherently becomes an elite market. We should not only aim for price premiums, but also try to lower costs and encourage more adoption of sustainable practices in conventional models (then scaling out and deep?).
- Q13: Frankly, I don't know. I asked for evidence from the literature.
- Q14: In Viet Nam it is not a total failure, but VietGAP's success is very slow and not certain.
- Q15: Also a dilemma. Convenience and price are key values that make modern food highly competitive with traditional foods. Consumer preferences also change with urbanisation and the Westernisation of diets. Pre-processing can help, but cost and labour are again the issue.

## Open questions
- *Claude's note on Mertens et al. (2022):* Gemini said follow-up debates "confirm" that defaults remain robust. In the same journal, Maier et al. (2022), also in your Zotero, report "no evidence for nudging after adjusting for publication bias". Read both before relying on any effect size.
- *Claude's note:* the elasticity figures, the 15–20% threshold and the Thailand story have no sources. Treat them as hypotheses. Viet Nam evidence on premiums: My et al. (2018), in Zotero.
- *Claude's note:* Gemini changed Q14. The first list asked about the elasticity of consumer *trust*: how fast labelling scandals and uncoordinated standards erode confidence in a whole product category. That question was never discussed, and it fits [[Trust in food chains]].
- Check the effect sizes quoted for Cadario & Chandon (2020) against the paper. Potter et al. (2021) and Lohmann et al. (2024) are not in Zotero; Gemini called Lohmann a "working paper".
