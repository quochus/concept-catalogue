## Definitions
Market systems development (MSD) began as a response to the limits of direct delivery in aid. Agencies that paid for training, inputs or services found that benefits often ended with the project. The UK Department for International Development (DFID) and the Swiss Agency for Development and Cooperation (SDC) backed an alternative called Making Markets Work for the Poor (M4P), later widely known as MSD. Its premise is that "the poor are dependent on market systems for their livelihoods" (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 5), so changing those systems "to work more effectively and sustainably for the poor" should reduce poverty (PDF p. 5).

The approach was framed as "an overarching approach to development" aimed at "large-scale, sustainable change" (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 10), within which tools such as value chain analysis could be used. As donors adopted it, the practice spread from Africa and South Asia to large programmes in Southeast Asia. Australia's first MSD programme was launched in 2009 in Cambodia (Warner & Loveridge, n.d., PDF p. 13), followed by programmes in Indonesia, Timor-Leste, Myanmar and the Pacific. The name shifted from M4P to MSD as the approach became mainstream, and the 2015 operational guide set out how to do it in practice (The Springfield Centre, 2015).

Its target is systemic change, defined as "change in the underlying causes of market system performance" (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 8), usually in rules and supporting functions rather than in the core exchange. Its method is facilitation: "Facilitation refers to the temporary actions of a facilitator to bring about system-level changes and develop market systems for the benefit of the poor" (The Springfield Centre, 2015, PDF p. 7). This marks the difference from value chain projects that work with one chain, and from [[Market linkages]] projects that build single buyer–seller relationships.

Definitions diverge on emphasis. Albu (2008, PDF p. 12) notes that M4P "sees poverty as a symptom of flaws" in market structures, while the sustainable livelihoods approach starts from households' assets and strategies; he argues the two are complementary. Donor reviews show a second divergence, between programmes that partner with lead firms and those that link smallholders to markets: Humphrey and Navas-Alemán (2010, PDF p. 5) find these "two main patterns of engagement" across 30 donor-led value chain interventions. Critics go further, seeing MSD as part of a wider move to make business a "development agent" (Blowfield & Dolan, 2014, p. 23).

**Working definition used here:** market systems development is an approach in which outside actors temporarily facilitate changes in the rules, services and relationships of a market system, so that it delivers better and lasting outcomes for poor producers and consumers at scale *(Claude's synthesis of The Springfield Centre, 2008, 2015; Albu, 2008)*.

## Features
**Components.** The Springfield Centre (2008) names four core principles: a focus on market systems, priority to scale, priority to sustainability, and a facilitation role. It adds a fifth logic, "addressing underlying causes (rather than symptoms) of weak performance" (PDF p. 33). In practice, programmes move through diagnosis, strategy, intervention and measurement. These principles imply a particular mechanism of change.

**Mechanisms.** MSD works by changing the incentives and capacities of existing market players so that they adopt new business models. Agencies should not become permanent market players: "facilitation is inherently a temporary role" (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 33). "For change to be sustainable, it must be led by market players", so programmes assess "which market players have incentives to support or to block change" (The Springfield Centre, 2015, PDF p. 11). Scale is pursued through these players rather than through project budgets: large-scale change "does not mean interventions themselves have to be large" (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 38). Whether this works depends on conditions.

**Conditions.** Political economy and government relations matter. Effective facilitation needs "detailed understanding of political economy factors at both macro (sector) and micro (market player) levels" (The Springfield Centre, 2015, PDF p. 8). In Cambodia, CAVAC I faced challenges because it "‘worked around’ government", while CAVAC II improved relationships with government partners (Warner & Loveridge, n.d., PDF p. 23). Pressure for results is a common constraint: "Unrealistic targets can pressurise a programme" into buying quick results at the expense of lasting impact (The Springfield Centre, 2015, PDF p. 13). Targeting also matters: CAVAC I "chose to target farmers with capacity to produce a marketable surplus" (Warner & Loveridge, n.d., PDF p. 25). These conditions make measurement difficult.

**Measurement.** MSD programmes track results through theories of change and results chains, often audited against the DCED standard, and use tools such as market system mapping and value chain analysis. The main challenge is systemic change. A review of M4P evaluations found that "most of these evaluations did not adequately test the linkages contained in the theory" and were "weak in terms of their qualitative data collection practices" (Ruffer & Wach, 2013, PDF p. 3). A later donor synthesis found that "The conceptualisation, assessment and reporting of systemic change is problematic", and that "Beneficiary impacts are used as a proxy for systemic change" (Warner & Loveridge, n.d., PDF p. 7). Measurement gaps feed the main debates.

**Debates.** The first debate is about evidence. The BEAM evidence review tells "a positive story of the far-reaching and long-lasting impacts" of MSD, but warns that "the evidence base remains subject to a strong degree of publication bias" (Hilton, 2024, PDF p. 5). The second is about whom MSD serves: targeting farmers who can already produce a surplus may bypass the poorest. The third is about goals: market systems interventions build some climate resilience but face "fundamental tensions" with resilience approaches (Kuhl, 2018, p. 131), and links to environmental goals and food system transformation are less developed than links to growth. Albu (2008, PDF p. 10) calls the approach "a work in progress". What remains unresolved is how to show systemic change credibly and how to add environmental goals without losing the market logic.

## Implications
**What the evidence shows.** Most published evidence comes from programme reviews rather than independent impact studies. Early cases were chosen "on the basis of their performance" (The Springfield Centre, 2008, PDF p. 27). Donor syntheses find that "MSD programs are achieving results, but are not consistently showing that they are facilitating systemic change" (Warner & Loveridge, n.d., PDF p. 7). Ex-post evaluations are rare but some point to sustained results (Hilton, 2024, PDF p. 5).

| Case | Where | Market system | What the programme did | Reported results | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Katalyst vegetables | Bangladesh | Vegetable inputs and information | Worked with input firms to train retailers to advise farmers, rather than training farmers directly | Reached "nearly 3,000 retailers serving a farmer clientele of approximately 1 million farmers"; productivity gains of 20–30% | The Springfield Centre (2008, PDF pp. 28–29) |
| FinMark Trust | South Africa | Financial services | Changed rules and supporting functions for low-income finance | Growth in the core attributed to changes in rules and support | The Springfield Centre (2008, PDF p. 37) |
| CAVAC I | Cambodia | Rice inputs and policy | Agribusiness interventions plus engagement on pesticide policy | Policy change on pesticides; criticised for weak attention to gender | Warner & Loveridge (n.d., PDF pp. 18–20) |
| IMSAR certification services | Rwanda | Horticulture export certification | Supported a local certification service provider | Exporters cut compliance costs by avoiding expensive international consultants | Hilton (2024, PDF p. 15) |
| Farmer-led irrigation dialogue space | Ghana | Small-scale irrigation | A multi-stakeholder dialogue linked firms, farmers and government | A space that can "trickle systemic change" via private investment | Minh et al. (2020, PDF p. 1) |

**Lessons.** The Bangladesh and Rwanda cases show the main MSD mechanism: changing an existing firm's business model so that a service continues after the project. The Cambodian case shows that MSD in Southeast Asia must work with government, not around it, and must pay attention to who is reached. For IP1+, an MSD lens would ask which input suppliers, cover crop seed firms, traders or certification bodies could profitably carry agroecological practices and products in Laos and Cambodia, and what rules would need to change.
