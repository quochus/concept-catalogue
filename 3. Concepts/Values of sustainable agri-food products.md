## My first take
*Written by Quoc before reading the draft (2026-10-21). Never edited later.*

Values of sustainable agri-food products - agri-food products could be connected many values: economic, social, environmental or health, and others. Values could be measurable or non-measurable.

## Definitions
"Value" is used in three senses that are often mixed up.

1. **Value created and captured (economics, strategy).** Lepak et al. (2007, p. 180) "define value creation in terms of use value and exchange value". Value capture is a separate question: who appropriates the value created.
2. **Values attributed to nature and food (environmental values).** Valuation research moves beyond intrinsic and instrumental values to "integrating plural values" including relational values (Arias-Arévalo et al. 2017, p. 1). Relational values are "context dependent, often place-based, nontradable, and therefore largely not substitutable" (Himes et al. 2024, p. 31). See [[Relational values]].
3. **Values as principles guiding chains (values-based supply chains).** Values-based supply chains are partnerships among business partners with "shared ethics or values related to the qualities of the food" and the business relationships along the chain (Peterson et al. 2022, p. 385).

For sustainable agri-food products, these meet. Credence attributes (sustainable, safe, agroecological) create value only when they are signalled and trusted. Values held by producers and consumers then shape what is valued. See [[Connectivity]].

## Features
- **Price premiums: the measurable part.** A meta-analysis finds the overall willingness-to-pay premium for sustainability "is 29.5% on average" (Li & Kallas 2021, p. 1). For food traceability, the premium reaches "around 32%", and "consumers in developing countries show a higher WTP" (Tran et al. 2024, p. 1). See [[Willingness to pay]].
- **Information and embeddedness as value signals.** Products embedded with information on place and production can "command a premium price" (Marsden et al. 2000, p. 425). "The more embedded and differentiated a product becomes, the scarcer it becomes" (Renting et al. 2003, p. 401).
- **Weak demand for the label itself.** In 12 developing-country cases, the values sought in agroecological products related to health and taste, indicating "there is not a clear demand for ‘agro-ecological’ products per se" (Loconto et al. 2018, p. 2).
- **Premiums in values-based chains.** Farmers receive premiums for products differentiated by quality, environment, place or social relationships (Peterson et al. 2022, p. 386). Critics say the values-based concept relies "too heavily on market-based differentiation" (Stahlbrand 2017, p. 77).
- **The non-measurable part.** Producers in agroecological initiatives most often mention "the relational values of meaningful occupation, care of nature" and social relations (Vizuete et al. 2025, p. 684). Farmers cite "environmental factors more frequently than economic reasons" for using agroecological practices (Coe et al. 2025, p. 1). Producer and consumer value orientations "did not align neatly" with a utilitarian versus non-utilitarian split (De Lucio et al. 2025, AAM p. 2).
- **Values in transition and leverage.** Transition "requires changes in practices but also in ways of thinking and in their underlying values" (Hazard et al. 2022, p. 405). Values "may act as leverage points for sustainability transformation" (Horcea-Milcu 2022, p. 1). See [[Scaling agroecology]].

## Implications
- **Viet Nam: rice.** Consumers would pay "a 9% price premium for certified sustainably-produced rice". The premium rose to 33% as information on certification and traceability was added (My et al. 2018, p. 283).
- **Viet Nam: vegetables and PGS.** PGS raised farm profitability by 117% (Grovermann et al. 2024, p. 1). Farmers would ask a 21% premium to sell to an unknown buyer, which shows that trust carries economic value (Enthoven & Van den Broeck 2021, p. 1). See [[Connectivity]].

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** Plural value domains (economic, social, environmental, health), some measurable and some not, are confirmed. Willingness-to-pay studies measure part of the economic value. Relational values are framed as largely nontradable and not substitutable (Himes et al. 2024).
- **Extends.** Three ideas go further than your take. "Value" (created, captured), "values" (principles) and "valuation" are distinct concepts. Who captures value matters as much as how much is created (Lepak et al. 2007). Information and trust turn values into price (Marsden et al. 2000; My et al. 2018).
- **Challenges.** Consumers may value health and taste rather than "sustainability" or "agroecology" as such (Loconto et al. 2018). Strategies based only on market differentiation have limits (Stahlbrand 2017).
