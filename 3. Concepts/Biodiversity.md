## Definitions
The idea that life on Earth is diverse is old, but biodiversity as a named concept is recent. Escobar (1998, p. 54) dates its arrival precisely: *"biodiversity irrupted in the world theater of science and development in the late 1980s"*, as conservation biologists raised alarm about the loss of species, especially in tropical forests. The concept answered a problem of survival: scientists needed one word that could carry the loss of genes, species and habitats into policy. Its textual origins lie in the Global Biodiversity Strategy and in the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), signed at the Rio Earth Summit in 1992 (Escobar, 1998, p. 54).

The CBD definition fixed the core meaning. It describes biological diversity as *"the variability among living organisms from all sources"*, including terrestrial, marine and other aquatic ecosystems, and covers diversity within species, between species and of ecosystems (Mace et al., 2012, p. 19). Because the CBD is a treaty, this three-level definition (genes, species, ecosystems) spread into national strategies, such as the Lao National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan (MONRE, 2016). The meaning then shifted as ecologists asked what biodiversity does. In response to the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment, research moved from counting species to asking how diversity affects ecosystem functions. Díaz et al. (2006, PDF p. 1) summarised the new evidence: *"biodiversity in the broad sense affects the properties of ecosystems"*, and through them human well-being. This prompted a broader, functional definition: biodiversity as *"the number, abundance, composition, spatial distribution, and interactions"* of genotypes, populations, species, functional types and landscape units (Díaz et al., 2006, PDF p. 1). A further shift came with global monitoring. When countries failed to meet the CBD target of *"a significant reduction in the rate of biodiversity loss by 2010"*, biodiversity also became a set of indicators to track (Butchart et al., 2010, p. 1164).

Today's core definition keeps the CBD's three levels and adds function and interactions. Its main components are genetic diversity, species diversity and ecosystem diversity, with functional traits treated as the part that drives many effects on [[Ecosystem services]] (Díaz et al., 2006, PDF p. 1). The relationship with ecosystem services is layered: Mace et al. (2012, p. 19) argue that *"biodiversity has key roles at all levels of the ecosystem service hierarchy"*, as a regulator of processes, as a service in itself, and as a good that people value.

Definitions diverge in three ways. First, ecologists and economists disagree on whether biodiversity is the same as ecosystem services; in some assessments the two terms *"are used almost synonymously"*, which Mace et al. (2012, p. 19) see as a source of confused policy. Second, political ecologists question whether biodiversity is a neutral object at all. Escobar (1998, p. 53) argues that, although it has concrete biophysical referents, it is *"a discursive invention of recent origin"*, and should be studied *"not as a true object that science progressively uncovers"* but as a discourse that links scientists, companies, states and local movements (p. 54). Third, there are differences of scale and purpose: global indicators favour measurable species and habitats, while local actors stress use, territory and culture.

Biodiversity is broader than [[Agrobiodiversity]], which is the part used or managed for food and agriculture. It is not the same as [[Ecosystem services]], which are the benefits people get from ecosystems, nor as nature in general, which also includes non-living parts. [[Nature-based solutions]] are actions that rely on biodiversity, and [[Social-ecological systems]] is a framework in which biodiversity is one element.

**Working definition used here:** biodiversity is the variety of living organisms at the genetic, species and ecosystem levels, including their abundance, traits and interactions, which shapes how ecosystems function and what they provide to people *(Claude's synthesis of the CBD definition in Mace et al., 2012, and Díaz et al., 2006)*.

## Features
**Components**
- The CBD sets three levels: diversity within species (genetic), between species, and of ecosystems (Mace et al., 2012, p. 19).
- The functional view adds abundance, spatial distribution, interactions and functional traits to the count of types (Díaz et al., 2006, PDF p. 1).
- National strategies also include the diversity of landscapes and their uses: the Lao NBSAP notes that forest and non-forest mosaics carry *"biodiversity, agro-biodiversity and livelihood values"* (MONRE, 2016, PDF p. 17).

**Mechanisms**
- Biodiversity works mainly through function: *"it is functional composition"* (the identity, abundance and range of traits) that appears to cause most effects on ecosystem services (Díaz et al., 2006, PDF p. 1).
- Rare species within a trophic level are likely to have small effects at any one time, so losses can be hidden until common or key species decline (Díaz et al., 2006, PDF p. 1).
- Biodiversity enters ecosystem services at several levels at once, as regulator, final service and valued good (Mace et al., 2012, p. 19).
- Losses are unequal in their effects: the consequences are *"felt disproportionately by the poor"*, who depend most directly on ecosystem services (Díaz et al., 2006, PDF p. 1).

**Conditions**
- Land use is the main pressure. Land use change is described as *"responsible for endangering 85% of species"* at risk, mainly through habitat change (Hald-Mortensen, 2023, PDF p. 1).
- Other pressures add up: resource consumption, alien species, nitrogen pollution, overharvesting of fish and climate change all showed rising trends (Butchart et al., 2010, p. 1164).
- In Southeast Asia, trade and market demand are strong drivers; in U Minh, local people were *"attributing the decline primarily to harvesting pressures"* (Nuwer & Bell, 2013, p. 88).
- Policy design matters: conservation policies can harm biodiversity they aim to protect, as when swidden bans led to some of the *"greatest losses of agrobiodiversity"* in the region (Rerkasem et al., 2009, p. 356).

**Measurement**
- Global indicator sets track state, pressure, response and benefit; in the 2010 assessment, *"most (8 out of 10) state indicators showing negative trends"* (Butchart et al., 2010, p. 1165).
- The Biodiversity Intactness Index measures *"the average proportion of natural biodiversity remaining in local ecosystems"* (Newbold et al., 2016, p. 288).
- Common tools include the IUCN Red List Index, the Living Planet Index and habitat extent (Butchart et al., 2010, p. 1165).
- National reports use simpler indicators such as forest cover; in Lao PDR *"the proportion of forest cover in 2015 was 46.7%"* (MONRE, 2016, PDF p. 17).

**Debates**
- **Biodiversity or ecosystem services.** One view treats managing ecosystem services as a way to keep biodiversity; Mace et al. (2012, p. 19) reject the equation and call for clearer roles.
- **Science or discourse.** Conservation science treats biodiversity as a measurable object (Butchart et al., 2010); political ecology treats it as a discourse that distributes power over nature and knowledge (Escobar, 1998, p. 54).
- **Conservation and poverty.** Protected areas are the main response, but *"its impacts on poverty are often negative"*, and *"success with integrated strategies is elusive"* (Adams et al., 2004, p. 1146).
- **Unresolved.** How to combine global indicators with local values and rights, and how to share the costs of conservation fairly, remain open (Adams et al., 2004, p. 1146; Escobar, 1998, p. 68).

## Implications
**Evidence overview.** Global assessments agree that biodiversity is still declining. The 2010 review of 31 indicators concluded that *"the rate of biodiversity loss does not appear to be slowing"*, despite more protected areas and policy responses (Butchart et al., 2010, p. 1164). A later global assessment found that land use has reduced biodiversity intactness beyond its proposed planetary boundary *"across 58.1% of the world’s land surface"*, where most people live (Newbold et al., 2016, p. 288). Evidence on what works is thinner and more local, and the links between conservation and poverty reduction are debated (Adams et al., 2004, p. 1146). Viet Nam and ASEAN evidence is shown first below.

The cases are grouped by type of response *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: protected areas, national strategy, community and territorial claims, and land-use policy.

| Lao NBSAP 2016–2025 | Lao PDR; 2016 | National strategy | National plan under the CBD for a country described as *"one of the most biodiversity rich counties in Southeast Asia"* (sic) | Forest cover reported at 46.7% in 2015 and declining over time; plan links biodiversity to agriculture and livelihoods | MONRE (2016, PDF pp. 17, 28) |

| Swidden transitions | Northern Thailand and West Kalimantan; 2000s | Land-use policy (contested) | Policies to end swidden and replace it with permanent land uses | Plant diversity fell in most places; some losses came from conservation directives themselves | Rerkasem et al. (2009, p. 356) |

**Cross-case lessons.** Protected areas can slow local loss, but they work better where they address trade and demand, not only local harvesting (Nuwer & Bell, 2013, p. 88). National strategies in ASEAN increasingly link biodiversity with agriculture and livelihoods, which opens a bridge to [[Agrobiodiversity]] (MONRE, 2016, PDF p. 17). Conservation that ignores local rights and land use can backfire, both socially and ecologically (Escobar, 1998, p. 68; Rerkasem et al., 2009, p. 356). The evidence does not show a reliable formula for joining conservation and poverty reduction, and global indicators still say little about why some local efforts succeed (Adams et al., 2004, p. 1146).

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** You list species, genetic varieties and habitats; this matches the CBD's three levels of genes, species and ecosystems (Mace et al., 2012, p. 19).
- **Extends.** The current view adds abundance, interactions and functional traits, and stresses that biodiversity shapes how ecosystems work and what they give people (Díaz et al., 2006, PDF p. 1).
- **Challenges.** Calling it "biological resources" frames biodiversity by its use; political ecologists warn that this framing is itself a choice with winners and losers (Escobar, 1998, p. 54), and ecologists value diversity beyond its resource role (Mace et al., 2012, p. 19).
