## My first take
*Written by Quoc before reading the draft (2026-10-21). Never edited later.*

Agroecology - a science and a movement, agroecology seeks to reconnect nature in agri-food production and foster the transformation of food production and food systems towards diversity, balance and resilience.

## Definitions
The word is older than the field. A Russian agronomist, Bensin, coined it: the term "was first used in two scientific publications by Bensin" in 1928 and 1930, to describe the use of ecological methods in research on crops (Wezel et al., 2009, p. 503). For half a century it stayed a scientific label for applying ecology to farming at the scale of the plot and the field.

Its meaning then widened three times, each time because the problems it addressed were larger than the field. In the 1970s and 1980s, agroecosystem research turned against the input dependence and erosion of Green Revolution farming, and practitioners took the idea up: "Agroecology as an agricultural practice emerged in the 1980s", often intertwined with farmers' movements (Wezel et al., 2009, p. 503). Because those problems also ran through markets, diets and rural livelihoods, scholars then pushed the boundary past the farm gate. Francis et al. (2003, p. 100) argued for "defining agroecology as the ecology of food systems", and research came to include "investigations covering the whole food system" (Wezel et al., 2009, p. 503). Gliessman's (2016, p. 188) five levels of transition record this widening: efficiency and input substitution (levels 1–2), redesign of the farm (level 3), "a more direct connection between those who grow our food" and those who eat it (level 4, see [[Connectivity]]), and a global food system built on equity (level 5). In response to this momentum, the UN Committee on World Food Security commissioned a report that turned agroecology into a policy framework (HLPE, 2019).

Today agroecology is used in three senses at once: a science, a practice and a social movement. The weight given to each differs by country, with "a stronger emphasis on movement and agricultural practice in Brazil" than in Europe or the United States (Wezel et al., 2009, p. 503). Two frameworks set out its content. The HLPE's "13 consolidated agroecological principles" (Wezel et al., 2020, PDF p. 1) run from recycling, input reduction, soil and animal health, biodiversity and synergy to economic diversification, co-creation of knowledge, social values and diets, fairness, [[Connectivity]], land and natural resource governance, and participation. FAO's 10 Elements cover similar ground and stress the "wholeness, interconnectedness and interdependence of agroecology" (Barrios et al., 2020, p. 232).

Where definitions diverge is over politics. The HLPE (2019, p. 15) notes "tensions and diverging views between science and social movements" on whether social and political change is part of agroecology or separate from its field practices. Holt-Giménez and Altieri (2013, p. 90) name the two poles "reformist and radical versions of agroecology": the reformist version folds agroecological practices into productivist agriculture, while the radical one ties them to peasant autonomy and [[Food sovereignty]]. The split follows purpose: researchers want transferable practices, movements want control over land, seeds and markets.

The boundaries with neighbouring approaches follow from this. [[Organic agriculture]] overlaps in practice but is rule-based and certified; the HLPE calls it "more prescriptive" (HLPE, 2019, p. 15), whereas agroecology has "a defined set of principles for the ecological management of agri-food systems", including socio-economic ones (Migliorini & Wezel, 2017, PDF p. 1). [[Sustainable intensification]] starts from raising output per unit of land; agroecology instead emphasises "reducing inputs and fostering diversity alongside social and political transformation" (HLPE, 2019, p. 15). [[Regenerative agriculture]] overlaps on soil practices and needs its own entry.

**Working definition used here:** agroecology is the redesign of farming and wider food systems around ecological and social principles, pursued at once as a science, a set of practices and a movement, and ranging from incremental to transformative change *(Claude's synthesis of Wezel et al., 2009; Francis et al., 2003; HLPE, 2019)*.

## Features
**Components.** Agroecology combines field practices (diversification, agroforestry, crop–livestock integration, biological pest control, recycling of nutrients and biomass) with principles and with the social relations that carry them. The social side is not an add-on: "Addressing human and social values is a core element of agroecology" (Bezner Kerr et al., 2022, PDF p. 1). These components matter because of how they work together, which is the next point.

**Mechanisms.** The ecological mechanism is to replace purchased inputs with ecological functions. Reviews of field evidence report "positive impacts of diversification on pollination, pest control, nutrient cycling" and other services (Snapp et al., 2021, p. 7). The economic mechanism follows: lower input costs and more diverse products can raise income and spread risk. The social mechanism is knowledge: farmer-to-farmer learning, as in the Latin American movements, uses a "Freirian horizontal communication methodology" in which farmers teach farmers (Rosset & Martínez-Torres, 2012, PDF p. 6). Whether these mechanisms deliver depends on conditions.

**Conditions.** The main constraint is labour. Human-capital outcomes are more often negative than positive, "due to higher labour requirements and costs", although labour productivity often improves (Mouratiadou et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). A second condition is scale: single farms rarely change alone, and large-scale adoption "will require a fully integrated multiscale systems approach" from farm to region to globe (Ewert et al., 2023, p. 351). A third is knowledge and organisation: practices that depend on local ecology spread through networks rather than packages (see Implications). These conditions shape how agroecology can be measured.

**Measurement.** Because agroecology is multidimensional, it is measured with multi-criteria tools rather than yield alone. FAO's Tools for Agroecological Performance Evaluation (TAPE) score farms against the elements and principles (Snapp et al., 2021, p. 7), and Gliessman's levels are used to place a farm or territory on a transition path; in the HLPE's reading of them, "While the first two steps are incremental, the latter three are more transformative" (HLPE, 2019, p. 15). Evidence is still thin where it matters most for Quoc's work: reviews note "The modest number of studies conducted in the Global South" and the dominance of short-term, field-scale studies (Snapp et al., 2021, p. 6). These measurement gaps feed the debates.

**Debates.** Two debates remain open. The first is about co-optation. When states adopt agroecology, movements "face risks when they allow themselves to become absorbed" into state programmes (Giraldo & McCune, 2019, p. 786), and the reformist version can strip out the social principles (Holt-Giménez & Altieri, 2013, p. 90). The second is about performance at scale: reviews find more positive than negative socio-economic outcomes, but trade-offs in labour and yield vary by practice and setting, and system-level effects are largely unmeasured (Mouratiadou et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). What remains unresolved is whether these trade-offs shrink or grow as agroecology moves from niche farms to whole territories.

## Implications
**What the evidence shows.** The largest socio-economic review screened more than 13,000 publications and found agroecological practices associated with "51% positive, 30% negative, 10% neutral, and 9% inconclusive outcomes" (Mouratiadou et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). Results differ by practice: "for agroforestry, we identify 53% positive outcomes", against 35% for cropping-system diversification (Mouratiadou et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). Climate evidence from low- and middle-income countries points to ecological co-benefits but rests on few, short studies (Snapp et al., 2021, pp. 6–7). In the Mekong region, a systematic review found that "The majority of the 271 identified cases focussed on practical and technical support", while initiatives on territorial governance, value chains "and policy frameworks were scarce" (Hett et al., 2023, p. 334). In Viet Nam, farmers still play "only a small role in adding value to agricultural products", which limits the returns from agroecological production (Dao & Nguyen, 2022, p. 69).

**Cases.** The cases below are chosen to span the types of agroecological initiative: farmer movement, public policy, innovation network, value chain for a practice, market institution, and policy framework *(Claude's typology, based on Wezel et al., 2020 and HLPE, 2019)*.

| Case | Where, when | Type | What was done | Outcomes reported | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Lessons across cases.** Agroecology spread fastest where organisations carried it (ANAP in Cuba, farmer groups and research centres in Laos) and where a market or institution rewarded it (PGS in Viet Nam, seed demand in Cambodia). Gains made through the state looked fragile once politics changed (Brazil), and policy frameworks still support some practices more than others (Europe). The Laos case suggests that "a pluralistic approach is desirable to promote innovation" (Castella et al., 2022, p. 14). What the cases do not show is long-term, territory-wide performance against conventional alternatives, which is the gap the reviews also identify.

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** You describe agroecology as a science and a movement that reconnects farming with nature and transforms food production and food systems. This matches the history of the term (Wezel et al., 2009, p. 503) and its widening to "the ecology of food systems" (Francis et al., 2003, p. 100).
- **Extends.** The literature adds a third sense, practice, and treats social values, fairness and governance as part of the definition rather than outcomes of it (Bezner Kerr et al., 2022, PDF p. 1; Wezel et al., 2020, PDF p. 1).
- **Challenges.** Diversity, balance and resilience are aims; the evidence shows trade-offs on the way, above all in labour (Mouratiadou et al., 2024, PDF p. 1), and a political split between reformist and radical versions that your take does not yet name (Holt-Giménez & Altieri, 2013, p. 90).
