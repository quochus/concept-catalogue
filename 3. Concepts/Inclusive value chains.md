## Definitions
The idea of inclusive value chains spread in the 2000s and 2010s, as development agencies looked to companies rather than aid to reach the poor. Inclusive business models are "ways of doing business that benefit the poor as producers or consumers" (van Westen et al., 2019, p. 64). In agriculture, inclusive agribusiness integrates smallholders "in commercial value chains" so they gain markets, inputs and services "in ways that are commercially viable" (p. 64).

As the term moved into policy, scholars noted that "remarkably little literature sheds light on the operationalization of the concept" (Ros-Tonen et al., 2019, p. 10). Reviewing inclusive business, inclusive value chain and inclusive development work, they identified economic, social, relational and environmental dimensions of inclusiveness.

**Working definition used here:** an inclusive value chain integrates smallholders and other marginalised actors into commercial chains on terms that improve their incomes, voice, relationships and environment, not only their participation *(Claude's synthesis of Ros-Tonen et al., 2019; van Westen et al., 2019)*.

## Features
**Mechanisms.** Collective action, contract farming, intermediaries and supportive public policy. Karatepe and Scherrer (2024, p. 317) find "the importance of collective action by key stakeholders, including smallholders and a supportive state". Danse et al. (2020, PDF p. 1) find "a need for intermediaries to overcome cultural and institutional gaps".

**Barriers.** Smallholders "often struggle to connect with actors in the middle of the food supply chain" due to limited land, inputs and capacity (Vos & Cattaneo, 2020, p. 14). Arias et al. (2013, PDF p. 6) note that low participation is "the result of constrained choice in a risky environment", not a lack of commercial interest.

**Debates.** Critical work questions whether inclusion is realistic: German et al. (2020, PDF p. 1) find "the overall trend is towards more exclusive agribusiness" as standards rise and state support falls. Others note that studies look only at participating farmers, overlooking other community members (van Westen et al., 2019).

## Implications
Inclusiveness depends on the crop, the chain and the policy context, so projects need to ask who is included, on what terms and who is left out. For IP1+, the concept frames how value chains for agroecological and regenerative products in Laos and Cambodia can include women and poorer farmers (see [[GEDSI]]) rather than only the best-resourced ones.
