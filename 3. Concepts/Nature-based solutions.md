solutions that protect, preserve and restore ecosystems

## Definitions
Nature-based solutions (NbS) is a policy concept, not a scientific discovery. It took shape in climate diplomacy: an IUCN resolution records that the concept was *"developed during the UNFCCC negotiations in 2009"* and then introduced into IUCN's global programme (IUCN, 2016, preamble). It answered a practical problem. Conservation organisations wanted to show that protecting and restoring ecosystems could help solve problems that governments cared about, such as climate change, disasters and water supply, and not only save species. Seddon et al. (2021, p. 1518) describe NbS simply as *"solutions to societal challenges that involve working with nature"*.

The meaning then moved from a slogan to a defined umbrella. Earlier approaches with similar aims, such as ecosystem-based adaptation (EbA) and ecosystem-based disaster risk reduction, already existed. Vignola et al. (2015, p. 126), for example, defined EbA practices for farmers as those *"based on the conservation, restoration or management of biodiversity, ecosystem processes or services"* that help crops and livestock cope with climate change. Because many such labels competed, IUCN set out a definitional framework in 2016. Cohen-Shacham et al. (2016) defined NbS as an umbrella concept that brings together a number of different ecosystem-based approaches, and the IUCN resolution defined NbS as *"Actions to protect, manage and restore natural or modified ecosystems"* that address societal challenges effectively and adaptively while providing human well-being and biodiversity benefits (IUCN, 2016, Annex 1). The concept then gained intergovernmental standing. In 2022, UNEA resolution 5/5 gave the *"first multilaterally agreed definition"*, covering *"actions to protect, conserve, restore, sustainably use and manage"* terrestrial, freshwater, coastal and marine ecosystems, and adding ecosystem services and resilience to the expected benefits (UNEA, 2022).

The core definition today has three parts. NbS are actions on ecosystems (protect, restore, manage or use sustainably); they address a named societal challenge such as climate change, disasters, food or water security; and they must deliver both human well-being and [[Biodiversity]] benefits. Under this umbrella sit EbA, ecosystem-based mitigation and disaster risk reduction; as Gonsalves et al. (2025, p. 15) put it, *"Ecosystem-based Adaptation is the operationalisation of Nature-based Solutions (NbS) for adaptation"*, and *"The umbrella term NbS encompasses approaches of working with nature"*. Farm practices such as [[Agroforestry]] and agroecology can count as NbS when they meet these conditions (Gonsalves et al., 2025, p. 15).

Definitions diverge mainly on safeguards and scope. Conservation bodies stress that NbS are *"no substitute for conventional conservation actions"* to protect species and ecosystems (IUCN, 2016, preamble). Critics worry that a broad label invites greenwashing: in practice *"much of the recent limelight has been on tree planting for carbon sequestration"*, often with plantations that do little for biodiversity or local rights (Seddon et al., 2021, p. 1518). The UNEA resolution responded by stressing social and environmental safeguards and by calling for consultations to address divergences in how countries understand and apply NbS (UNEA, 2022). There is also a divergence of scale: urban planners use NbS for green roofs and parks, while rural development uses it for watersheds, mangroves and farms.

NbS overlap with but differ from neighbouring ideas. [[Ecosystem services]] are the benefits; NbS are the actions that secure them. The [[Landscape approach]] is a way of governing land-use trade-offs across a landscape, which may include NbS. [[Climate change adaptation]] is a goal that NbS can serve, alongside engineered or social measures.

**Working definition used here:** nature-based solutions are deliberate actions to protect, restore, manage or sustainably use ecosystems in order to address a defined societal challenge, designed so that they deliver both human well-being and biodiversity benefits, with safeguards for local rights *(Claude's synthesis of IUCN, 2016, and UNEA, 2022)*.

## Features
**Components**
- An ecosystem action: protection, restoration, sustainable management or use of natural or modified ecosystems (IUCN, 2016, Annex 1; UNEA, 2022).
- A named societal challenge, such as climate change, disaster risk, food or water security (IUCN, 2016, Annex 1).
- Two kinds of benefit at once: human well-being and biodiversity, with the UNEA text adding ecosystem services and resilience (UNEA, 2022).
- For farmers, practical conditions: EbA practices should support food security and income, use local knowledge and inputs, and *"have low implementation and labor costs"* (Vignola et al., 2015, p. 126).

**Mechanisms**
- Ecosystems buffer hazards: in Soc Trang, the coast is mostly *"protected from erosion, storms and flooding by a narrow belt of mangroves"* (GIZ, 2013, PDF p. 4).
- They provide several services at once, such as fish nurseries, carbon storage and wave attenuation, which makes NbS multi-purpose (GIZ, 2013, PDF p. 7).
- On farms, practices such as shade trees and diversification buffer crops against heat and extreme weather while maintaining the ecosystem services farming depends on (Vignola et al., 2015, p. 126).
- Social arrangements make the ecological mechanism work: in Soc Trang, *"Co-management is an effective way of maintaining and enhancing the protection function"* of the mangrove belt (GIZ, 2013, PDF p. 16).

**Conditions**
- Site and process knowledge: planting alone often fails, and restoration must fit local sediment, erosion and species conditions (GIZ, 2013, PDF p. 10).
- Supportive and integrated agriculture and climate policies, and strong extension services, are needed for farm-level EbA (Vignola et al., 2015, p. 126).
- Governance can stall NbS: a study of EbA *"in Thailand and the Netherlands"* found *"six causal mechanisms that explain impasses"*, including frame polarisation and veto players (Sieber et al., 2018, p. 2379).
- Inclusion and consent of local communities and Indigenous Peoples are treated as conditions for success, not add-ons (Seddon et al., 2021, p. 1518).

**Measurement**
- Systematic evidence maps compare nature-based interventions with alternatives on climate impacts (Chausson et al., 2020, p. 6134).
- Cost–benefit tools compare NbS with grey infrastructure; in Soc Trang, a dyke upgrade had a *"negative benefit/cost ratio over 20 years"*, while mangrove rehabilitation returned about 1.9 USD per USD invested in saved wealth (GIZ, 2013, PDF p. 18).
- Criteria-based screening decides what counts: Vignola et al. (2015, p. 126) offer a framework to judge whether a farm practice qualifies as EbA, based on its ecological basis, its effect on yields under climate stress, and its fit for smallholders.

**Debates**
- **Promise.** Supporters see NbS as a way to address climate change and biodiversity loss together while supporting development (Seddon et al., 2021, p. 1518).
- **Greenwashing and offsets.** Critics warn that the focus on tree planting risks *"distracting from the need to rapidly phase out use of fossil fuels"* and from protecting intact ecosystems (Seddon et al., 2021, p. 1518).
- **Conservation first.** IUCN itself stresses that NbS do not replace species and ecosystem protection (IUCN, 2016, preamble).
- **Evidence.** Supporters cite growing evidence of effectiveness; reviewers stress *"substantial gaps in the evidence base"*, including costs (Chausson et al., 2020, p. 6134).
- **Unresolved.** UNEA asked for intergovernmental consultations on criteria, standards and guidelines (UNEA, 2022); whether these produce shared rules that stop misuse while keeping room for local design is still open *(Claude's assessment)*.

## Implications
**Evidence overview.** The first global systematic map of NbS for climate adaptation found that nature-based interventions were most often *"as effective or more so than alternative interventions"* in addressing climate impacts, but noted *"substantial gaps in the evidence base"*, with *"few studies of the cost-effectiveness of interventions compared to alternatives"* (Chausson et al., 2020, p. 6134). For smallholders, the main gap is *"the need for greater understanding of their effectiveness"* and of what drives adoption (Vignola et al., 2015, p. 126). Viet Nam evidence comes first below.

**Mangrove EbA in Soc Trang, Viet Nam (2007–2014).** A provincial and GIZ project combined mangrove rehabilitation, bamboo T-fences to rebuild mudflats, and co-management with local communities (GIZ, 2013, PDF pp. 9–16). The project found that *"Rehabilitating or planting mangroves alone is of little use"* and that protecting remaining mangroves *"is often more effective than trying to plant new mangrove forests"* (GIZ, 2013, PDF pp. 10, 14). It also cites national evidence that *"1.1 Mio USD spent for mangrove rehabilitation in Vietnam saved 7.3 Mio USD annually"* in dyke maintenance (GIZ, 2013, PDF p. 7).

**Agroforestry as NbS in the Philippines and wider Asia.** A recent primer frames agroforestry as one of the *"nature-based climate solutions"* that modify micro-climates, enhance biodiversity and build soil health for smallholders (Gonsalves et al., 2025, PDF p. 2). It notes that EbA *"is gaining significant importance in the context of climate change"* and biodiversity policy (Gonsalves et al., 2025, p. 20).

**EbA governance in Thailand and the Netherlands.** Five cases showed that EbA projects stalled less for lack of technology than for governance reasons such as polarised framings, poor timing, rules of the game and *"(5) veto players"* (Sieber et al., 2018, p. 2379). This is a contested case: the ecological logic was sound, but the institutions did not move.

**Smallholder coffee EbA in Mesoamerica.** Shade trees, diversified farms and soil conservation were assessed as EbA practices that keep yields stable under climate stress, but uptake depends on policy and extension support (Vignola et al., 2015, p. 126).

Together, the cases show that NbS work when the ecosystem action is site-specific, combined with social arrangements such as co-management, and compared honestly with grey alternatives. They fail or stall when they are reduced to planting, or when governance and incentives are not aligned *(Claude's synthesis of the cases above)*.

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** You describe NbS as solutions that protect, preserve and restore ecosystems; this matches the core of the IUCN definition, *"Actions to protect, manage and restore natural or modified ecosystems"* (IUCN, 2016, Annex 1).
- **Extends.** The defining feature is not the ecosystem action alone but its purpose: NbS must address a named societal challenge and deliver both human well-being and biodiversity benefits (IUCN, 2016, Annex 1; UNEA, 2022). Sustainable use and management of modified ecosystems, including farms, also count.
- **Challenges.** Not every ecosystem action is a "solution": critics show that badly designed NbS, such as monoculture tree planting, can harm biodiversity and rights, so safeguards are part of the concept (Seddon et al., 2021, p. 1518).
