## My first take
*Written by Quoc before reading the draft (2026-10-21). Never edited later.*

connectivity - connection among actors in the food systems, and between human and nature.

## Definitions
The idea of connecting producers and consumers entered food studies before agroecology named it as a principle. Around 2000, rural sociologists studying short food supply chains argued that what mattered was not distance but information: a short chain is one where the product reaches the consumer "embedded with information" about where and how it was made (Marsden et al., 2000, p. 425). Such chains create "new linkages between agriculture and society, producers and consumers" (Renting et al., 2003, p. 398). The problem they answered was the anonymous, industrial chain, in which producers lost a share of value and consumers lost knowledge of their food.

Because "local" and "short" meant different things to different people, the idea was then taken apart and widened. Eriksen (2013, p. 48) separated "geographical proximity, relations of proximity, and values of proximity". Sustainability science went further and treated connection as a property of social-ecological systems: food systems need "tightening feedback loops between ecosystems, actors in the food production chain and consumers" (Sundkvist et al., 2005, p. 224), and "reconnecting people to nature" is one of three realms of deep leverage for transformation (Abson et al., 2017, p. 30). Agroecology took the idea into its model of transition, where level 4 means "a more direct connection between those who grow our food" and those who eat it (Gliessman, 2016, p. 188).

The HLPE then codified connectivity as the 11th of 13 agroecological principles: to "Ensure proximity and confidence between producers and consumers", through fair and short distribution networks and by re-embedding food systems in local economies (Wezel et al., 2020, Table 1, PDF p. 7). It is one of the principles that act at the level of the food system rather than the field or farm (Wezel et al., 2020, Fig. 4, PDF p. 9). Reviews gloss it as "building linkages and trust between producers and consumers" (Bezner Kerr et al., 2022, PDF p. 3).

Definitions differ in breadth. The HLPE definition is narrow: it concerns producers and consumers. The sustainability-science use is broad: it covers links among all food-system actors and between people and nature. The difference follows purpose. A policy list of principles needs each item to be distinct, while systems research looks for properties that run through the whole system.

Connectivity also needs to be kept apart from its neighbours. [[Short food supply chains]] are one organisational form that can deliver it; [[Proximity]] is one of its components; [[Embeddedness]] describes the social relations within which exchange takes place; and [[Trust in food chains]] is the main mechanism through which it works.

**Working definition used here:** connectivity is the strength and quality of the links between producers and consumers, and more broadly among food-system actors and between people and the ecosystems that feed them, through which information, trust and value flow *(Claude's synthesis of Wezel et al., 2020; Marsden et al., 2000; Abson et al., 2017)*.

## Features
**Components.** The literature points to four dimensions *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: (1) producer–consumer or market connectivity, the HLPE core; (2) actor and knowledge networks among farmers, intermediaries, researchers and the state; (3) human–nature connectedness; and (4) ecological feedback between ecosystems and the chain. The HLPE definition covers only the first. The next question is how any of these links produces effects.

**Mechanisms.** Connection works mainly through information and trust. Shorter, more transparent chains mean that "producers can profit from getting a higher share of revenue" (Wezel et al., 2020, PDF p. 6). Information carried by the product lets it "command a premium price" (Marsden et al., 2000, p. 425), and "The more embedded and differentiated a product becomes, the scarcer it becomes" (Renting et al., 2003, p. 401). For credence attributes such as "safe" or "agroecological", which buyers cannot check for themselves, trust is what turns information into value (Bezner Kerr et al., 2022, PDF p. 3). These mechanisms do not work everywhere, which leads to the conditions.

**Conditions.** Connection pays off when institutions carry it: guarantee systems, cooperatives, nested markets or public procurement. It can also exclude. Local food efforts can "reinforce and widen inequities in access to fresh, local food" when prices or locations suit better-off consumers (Wezel et al., 2020, PDF p. 6). Because of these conditions, connectivity is hard to measure directly.

**Measurement.** Few studies measure connectivity as such. Usable proxies are chain length, the producer's share of the retail price, the type of guarantee (third-party, participatory, relational) and the terms of buyer–seller relationships *(Claude's suggestion, not from a source)*. Eriksen's three domains of proximity offer a frame for combining them (Eriksen, 2013, p. 48). Measurement problems feed the main debate.

**Debates.** The central critique is the "local trap": the tendency to "assume something inherent about the local scale" (Born & Purcell, 2006, p. 195). Connection and proximity do not by themselves make a food system more sustainable or just; what matters is who gains from the connection. What remains unresolved is whether connectivity should be valued for itself, as relational and place-based value, or only for the outcomes it produces.

## Implications
- **Viet Nam: the price of trust.** In northern Viet Nam, vegetable farmers would "request a 21% premium to accept selling to an unknown buyer" (Enthoven & Van den Broeck, 2021, PDF p. 1). The connection itself has an economic value that a long, anonymous chain does not pay for.
- **Viet Nam: participatory guarantee systems.** PGS-Vietnam strengthens "interactions between buyers, growers, and other stakeholders" (Grovermann et al., 2024, PDF p. 3), and adopting it raised farm profitability: "PGS significantly improves farm profitability (+117%)" (Grovermann et al., 2024, PDF p. 2).
- **Agroecological territories.** Nested markets, embedded in local social relations, "thicken networks of solidarity and relations of reciprocity" (Anderson et al., 2019, PDF p. 11).

Taken together, the cases show connectivity working through institutions that make trust visible and tradable: a known buyer, a peer-reviewed guarantee, a nested market. They also show that its benefits are measured in income and relationships at once, which links this entry to [[Values of sustainable agri-food products]].

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** Your two parts, connections among food-system actors and between people and nature, match two of the four dimensions in the literature (Wezel et al., 2020, PDF p. 7; Abson et al., 2017, p. 30).
- **Extends.** The literature explains why connection matters: it carries information and builds trust, and so moves value towards producers (Marsden et al., 2000, p. 425; Renting et al., 2003, p. 401). It also adds ecological feedback as a fourth dimension (Sundkvist et al., 2005, p. 224).
- **Challenges.** The official principle is narrower than your take: it is about producers and consumers only (Wezel et al., 2020, PDF p. 7). Critics also warn that connection does not guarantee good outcomes (Born & Purcell, 2006, p. 195).
