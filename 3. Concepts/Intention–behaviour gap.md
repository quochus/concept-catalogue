## Definitions
The gap began as a puzzle in social psychology. For decades, studies found weak links between attitudes and behaviour, and "the general consensus was that measures of attitude have little value for the prediction of overt behavior" (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1977, p. 888). Ajzen and Fishbein's answer was that attitudes predict behaviour only when both are measured at the same level: attitudes and actions are "defined by their target, action, context, and time elements", and strong relations appear "only under high correspondence" between them (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1977, p. 888). The problem they answered was a crisis of confidence in attitudes as a guide to action.

The theory of planned behaviour then put intention between attitude and action. Intentions "are assumed to capture the motivational factors that influence a behavior" (Ajzen, 1991, p. 181), and they depend on attitudes, social norms and perceived control. But intention can become behaviour "only if the behavior in question is under volitional control" (Ajzen, 1991, p. 181). This opened a second gap, between intention and action. In environmental policy, the same problem was named the "value-action gap", where public concern for the environment does not lead to changes in behaviour (Blake, 1999, p. 259).

In food research today, the term covers the distance between favourable attitudes or stated willingness to pay and actual purchase. For sustainable food, "consumer attitudes are mainly positive", but "behavioral patterns are not univocally consistent with attitudes" (Vermeir & Verbeke, 2006, p. 169). The gap has three links *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: attitude to intention, intention to behaviour, and stated willingness to pay to real payment. The last link is the one that matters most for market forecasts.

Definitions diverge on whether the gap is a measurement problem or a real feature of behaviour. Psychologists treat it as a matter of prediction, to be reduced by better measures and models. Practice theorists argue that the gap comes from looking at decisions at all: "the sources of behaviour lie in the development of practices themselves and not the decisionmaking" (Beatson et al., 2020, p. 198). The first view tries to close the gap; the second questions whether it exists in the form described.

The gap needs to be kept apart from its neighbours. [[Willingness to pay]] measures one side of it; hypothetical bias is the version found in stated-preference studies. [[Consumer behaviour]] covers all factors that shape buying, not only the gap. [[Nudges]] and changes to the [[Food environment]] are among the responses to it.

**Working definition used here:** the intention–behaviour gap is the difference between what people say they value, intend or would pay for and what they actually do, which explains why stated demand for sustainable food overstates real purchases *(Claude's synthesis of Ajzen, 1991; Vermeir & Verbeke, 2006)*.

## Features
**Components.** The literature points to three kinds of barrier that open the gap *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: (1) situational barriers such as price, availability and habits; (2) psychological factors such as weak involvement, low certainty and low belief that one's purchase makes a difference; and (3) social and practice factors such as norms and household routines. Low perceived availability "explains why intentions to buy remain low, although attitudes might be positive" (Vermeir & Verbeke, 2006, p. 169). How these barriers work is the next question.

**Mechanisms.** Attitudes act through intention, and intention acts through control. When price, availability or time are outside a person's control, intentions fail to become purchases. Social norms can work the other way: "experiencing social pressure from peers (social norm) explains intentions to buy, despite rather negative personal attitudes" (Vermeir & Verbeke, 2006, p. 169). People also manage the gap rather than close it. Environmental activists were aware of the impact of their holidays but "offered a wide range of explanations justifying their tourist activities" rather than changing their behaviour (Juvan & Dolnicar, 2014, p. 76). These mechanisms depend on conditions.

**Conditions.** The gap widens where sustainable products are expensive, hard to find or hard to recognise. In Viet Nam, consumers' "positive attitude did not necessarily translate into their actual purchase of organic meat", and premium prices "were certainly a deterrent" (Nguyen et al., 2019, PDF p. 1). The gap narrows where retailers support the choice: "food stores’ green marketing practices significantly enhanced consumers’ actual purchase behavior" (Nguyen et al., 2019, PDF p. 1). Information alone helps little, since consumers rely on proxies they trust or distrust rather than on more facts (Eden et al., 2008, p. 1044). These conditions make the gap hard to measure.

**Measurement.** Most studies measure attitudes and intentions with surveys and rarely observe behaviour. A review of sustainable wine found none that "examined purchase behaviour in real market scenarios with real market transactions", and the authors called for household panel data "as evidence of an attitude behaviour gap was found" (Schäufele & Hamm, 2017, p. 379). In willingness-to-pay studies, the gap shows up as hypothetical bias: hypothetical estimates are higher than non-hypothetical ones (Li & Kallas, 2021, PDF p. 1). Measurement choices feed the debates.

**Debates.** One debate is about the size of the gap: much of it may come from measuring attitudes and behaviour at different levels of specificity, as Ajzen and Fishbein argued, so better correspondence shrinks it. A second is about the model of the person. Critics argue that linear models from attitude to action miss how consumption is embedded in routines, and that a practice view "diverts attention away from moments of individual decision-making" (Beatson et al., 2020, p. 198). A third is about responsibility: if the gap reflects structures of price and supply, framing it as a consumer failure shifts responsibility away from retailers and the state (Blake, 1999, p. 259). What remains unresolved is how much of the gap can be closed by changing consumers and how much requires changing the food environment.

## Implications
- **Viet Nam: organic meat.** Concern for health, safety and the environment improved attitudes, but positive attitudes did not lead to purchase, and price was the main barrier; green marketing in stores raised actual buying (Nguyen et al., 2019, PDF p. 1).
- **Young consumers: sustainable dairy.** In a survey with an advertising experiment, attitude correlated strongly with intention to buy, but low perceived availability kept intentions low (Vermeir & Verbeke, 2006, p. 169).
- **Sustainable tourism: committed activists.** Even people with strong environmental attitudes "displayed an attitude–behaviour gap which made them feel uncomfortable" (Juvan & Dolnicar, 2014, p. 76), and they reduced the discomfort with explanations rather than changed behaviour.

Together, the cases show that the gap is widest where price and availability work against the stated preference, and that it shrinks when the choice environment supports the intention. For IP1+, this means that stated willingness to pay for regenerative products in Cambodia or Laos is an upper bound on demand. Business plans should rely on observed sales or non-hypothetical tests, and on [[Nudges]], availability and trusted labels that make buying easier, rather than on survey intentions alone.
