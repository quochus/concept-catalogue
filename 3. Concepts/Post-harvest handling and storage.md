## Definitions
Post-harvest handling and storage became a field of research and extension because a large share of harvested food never reached consumers. Early work focused on grain storage, but horticultural scientists argued that perishable crops needed their own methods. Kitinoja et al. (2011, Abstract) framed the task for developing countries as developing simple, science-based handling methods and judging the *"cost effectiveness of scale‐appropriate postharvest technologies in each locale"* and crop. In Viet Nam, a 1994 national workshop found that *"Packages specially designed to carry harvested vegetables and fruits are usually not available"*, so produce was bruised in transit (Hoan, 1995, p. 91), and fresh produce was mostly packed in bamboo baskets (p. 92).

The meaning shifted as loss research matured. Hodges et al. (2011) compared developed and less developed countries and found that critical losses arise mainly after the farm gate in rich countries but *"before the farm gate in LDCs"* (Abstract), which pushed attention to on-farm and first-mile handling. Because many loss estimates were weak, meta-analysis then showed that *"losses are often economic rather than physical product losses"* and that *"technologies for loss mitigation fail to address dynamics of supply chains"* (Affognon et al., 2015, Abstract). This moved the field from single technologies towards chain-wide approaches that combine practices, technologies, training and markets (Stathers et al., 2020).

In current use, post-harvest handling covers the steps that Kaur and Watson (2024) list (handling, pre-cooling, washing and sanitation, sorting and grading, packaging, storage and transport) and the facilities, tools and know-how used at each step. For fruits and vegetables, the main tools are careful harvesting, crates and suitable packaging, shade and cooling, and storage at the right temperature and humidity (Acedo & Weinberger, 2009; Kitinoja et al., 2011). Packaging matters because it *"protects produce from compression and abrasion damage during handling and transportation"* (FAO Regional Office for Asia and the Pacific, 2011, PDF p. 19).

Definitions diverge on boundaries and on what counts as loss. Physical scientists define post-harvest loss as weight or quality lost after harvest; economists count lost value, including lower prices for damaged produce (Affognon et al., 2015). Some definitions start at harvest and end at processing; others follow produce to retail. The divergence matters because it changes loss estimates and so the case for investment.

Post-harvest handling and storage is broader than the [[Cold chain]], which is one set of tools within it, and narrower than [[Food loss and waste]], which also covers waste at retail and in households. It overlaps with [[Pre-processing]] (cleaning, grading, trimming), and its technologies are one family of [[Supply chain technologies (fruits and vegetables)]].

**Working definition used here:** post-harvest handling and storage is the set of practices, facilities and technologies used from harvest until sale or processing (harvesting, sorting, packing, cooling, storing and transporting) to keep crops in marketable condition and limit physical and economic losses *(Claude's synthesis of Kitinoja et al., 2011; Kaur & Watson, 2024; Affognon et al., 2015)*.

## Features
**Components**
- Practices: harvest timing and method, gentle handling, sorting and grading (Kaur & Watson, 2024).
- Packaging: in Southeast Asia, from bamboo baskets and wooden crates to plastic crates and fibreboard boxes (FAO Regional Office for Asia and the Pacific, 2011). Mekong trials found that a *"plastic crate with paper shreds significantly reduced damage"* for tomatoes compared with carton boxes (Acedo & Weinberger, 2009, p. 106).
- Cooling and storage: precooling with a *"simple and portable hydrocooler developed by the Vietnam NARS"* (Acedo & Weinberger, 2009, p. 106), evaporative coolers, cold rooms (see [[Cold chain]]).
- Facilities and services: packing houses, collection points and storage; in Viet Nam in the 1990s there were *"temporary packing houses without cool storage facilities or mechanised grading lines"* (Hoan, 1995, p. 91).

**Mechanisms**
- Good handling reduces bruising and decay; cooling and humidity control slow respiration and water loss (Kitinoja et al., 2011; Elolu et al., 2025).
- Simple tools can cut losses a lot when they fit local needs. Across Cambodia, Laos and Viet Nam, adopters of AVRDC's low-cost technologies reported losses reduced about tenfold and higher income (Acedo et al., 2012).
- Losses fall unequally: in Viet Nam, Cambodia and Laos, *"the average loss of the selected vegetables is about 17%"*, and *"farmers are the most vulnerable group compared to middlemen and retailers"* (Weinberger et al., 2008, p. 288).

**Conditions**
- Knowledge and intermediaries: *"Technology adoption was highest in Vietnam (60%) and lowest in Cambodia (27%)"*, and *"market intermediaries have a strong influence on technology dissemination"* (Acedo et al., 2012, p. 47).
- Climate and fit: evaporative coolers only help where air is hot and dry enough (Defraeye et al., 2023).
- Capital and finance: developing-country stakeholders *"often lack access, infrastructure, equipment, and capital"* (Kaur & Watson, 2024, Highlights). Hodges et al. (2011) name farmer education, infrastructure, value chains with incentives for producers, collective marketing and microcredit as the main levers.
- Research gaps in Viet Nam: a 2019 MARD presentation lists *"Lacking of research on appropriated and low cost post-harvest"* technology and cold chain management among the challenges (Vu, 2019, PDF p. 6).

**Measurement**
- Loss assessment uses actor surveys, load tracking and sampling; Weinberger et al. (2008) asked farmers, middlemen and retailers in three countries to estimate volume losses.
- Evidence syntheses show that measurement is uneven: inadequate methods give inaccurate estimates (Affognon et al., 2015), and *"Storage technology interventions mainly targeting farmers dominated (83% of the studies)"* in a scoping review of 334 studies (Stathers et al., 2020, Abstract).
- In the 2024 update of that review, fruits *"particularly citrus and mango in India, were the second most studied group"* (Stathers et al., 2025, Abstract).

**Debates**
- Technology-first approaches promote better storage, cooling and packaging (Kitinoja et al., 2011; Acedo et al., 2012).
- Chain-wide and institutional approaches argue that technologies alone fail: *"The lack of studies on training, finance, infrastructure, policy and market interventions"* shows a need to look beyond technologies (Stathers et al., 2020, Abstract), and technologies *"fail to address dynamics of supply chains"* (Affognon et al., 2015, Abstract).
- What counts as loss: physical loss estimates can understate economic loss from lower quality and prices (Affognon et al., 2015).
- Unresolved: how to compare the cost-effectiveness of handling practices, technologies and market interventions for fruits and vegetables, given weak and inconsistent loss data.

## Implications
Viet Nam and Mekong evidence comes first.

- **Low-cost technologies and training in Cambodia, Laos and Viet Nam.** AVRDC's ADB-funded projects measured losses (about 17%) along vegetable chains, adapted simple technologies (crates, modified-atmosphere packaging, a hydrocooler) and trained trainers and chain actors; adoption reached 60% in Viet Nam, with large loss reductions reported by adopters (Weinberger et al., 2008; Acedo & Weinberger, 2009; Acedo et al., 2012).
- **Passive cooling in Kenya.** An evaporative cooling blanket cut postharvest losses by up to 45% and paid back in under three months (Wittkamp et al., 2025), showing what a well-fitted storage tool can do.
- **Evidence across sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.** The scoping review and its update show most evidence is on farm storage technologies, with little on training, finance or markets, and fruit and vegetable evidence concentrated in India (Stathers et al., 2020, 2025).

Together these cases show that post-harvest handling improves when simple, locally adapted tools are paired with training and with the intermediaries who move produce to market, and that evidence is still thin on the market and institutional side *(Claude's synthesis)*. For Viet Nam, the Mekong projects offer a tested model, but the recent research gap named by MARD (Vu, 2019) suggests the model has not been carried forward at scale.
