## My first take
*Written by Quoc before reading the draft (2026-10-28). Never edited later.*

Resilience = resistance + adaptive capacity + recovery capacity against stresses and shocks

Food system resilience = capacities of food systems and food system actors to cope with and adapt to stresses and shocks (i.e., ensuring nutritional, health, socio-economic outcomes) while guaranteeing the ability to recover in long term.

## Definitions

Resilience entered food systems research from ecology. Holling (1973) separated stability, the tendency to return to equilibrium after a disturbance, from resilience, which he called *"a measure of the persistence of systems"* and of their ability to absorb change and disturbance (PDF p. 16). His point was that a system can fluctuate widely and still persist, so efficiency near one stable state is a poor guide to survival under surprise. The older engineering meaning, which looks at *"the speed of return to equilibrium"* (Folke, 2006, p. 256), stayed influential in disaster management and supply chain studies, and it still shapes the idea that resilience means bouncing back.

Because ecologists then recognised that people manage, degrade and redesign ecosystems, the concept was widened to social–ecological systems. Walker et al. (2004) defined resilience as *"the capacity of a system to absorb disturbance and reorganize while undergoing change"* while keeping the same function, structure and identity (PDF p. 3). They added adaptability, the capacity of actors to manage resilience, and transformability, *"the capacity to create a fundamentally new system"* when the existing one becomes untenable (PDF p. 2). Social scientists had already begun the move from ecosystems to people. Adger (2000, p. 347) defined social resilience as *"the ability of groups or communities to cope with external stresses"* arising from social, political and environmental change, and tested the idea on a resource-dependent community in coastal Viet Nam. 

Food security researchers took up this framing through work on global environmental change and vulnerability. Ericksen et al. (2010, p. 70) defined resilience as *"the capacity of a system to absorb change without shifting to an altered state"* and placed it beside [[Vulnerability]] in a food system framework. Development researchers then made it usable for households and programmes. Béné et al. (2012, p. 21) proposed *"absorptive, adaptive, and transformative capacity"* as the three components of resilience, linking coping, incremental adjustment and transformational change. Barrett and Constas (2014, p. 14625) pushed further, arguing that measurement tools and policy briefs had multiplied *"conspicuously without any explicit theory of resilience"* for the lives of the poor, and set out such a theory for development work.

The most cited definition of food system resilience came from Tendall et al. (2015), who wrote it to cut through the many definitions borrowed from other fields. For them it is the *"capacity over time of a food system and its units at multiple levels"* *"to provide sufficient, appropriate and accessible food to all"* in the face of various and even unforeseen disturbances (p. 19). Three features of this definition have stuck. It names the function to protect, which is [[Food security]]. It includes unforeseen disturbances, so *"resilience extends beyond conventional risk management"* (p. 19). And it treats resilience as a matter of degree, built through a cycle of robustness, absorption, recovery and learning. FAO (2021) carried this definition into policy almost word for word, describing resilience as *"the capacity over time of agrifood systems, in the face of any disruption"* to ensure food security and nutrition for all and the livelihoods of the actors involved (p. 6). Later work moved the focus from the system to its actors. Béné et al. (2023, p. 1438) define food system resilience as the ability of individual and institutional actors to *"maintain, protect, or successfully recover the key functions of that system"* despite disturbances, with food security as the main outcome. At national scale, Béné et al. (2026) read it empirically as *"the ability to decouple food-security outcomes from shock severity"* (PDF p. 1). The terms food resilience, resilient food systems and food system resilience are used for the same idea in this literature, and this page treats them as one concept.

Definitions still diverge on what resilience should restore. The HLPE (2025, p. 24) notes that most approaches aim to *"bounce back to restore a predisturbance status"*, built around resisting, absorbing, adapting to, recovering from and preventing shocks. Its own concept, equitably transformative resilience, *"requires food systems to bounce forward in equitable ways"* by addressing the root causes of differential vulnerability (p. 32). The split is partly disciplinary (ecology and engineering versus political economy) and partly about purpose: protecting today's supply chains, or changing who bears risk. Definitions also differ on the unit of analysis: a household, a value chain, a city region, a country or the global trade network. Zurek et al. (2022) argue that these differences cannot be settled in the abstract: any assessment first has to say resilience of what, to what, and from whose perspective, because the answers change which outcomes count. The point goes back to Carpenter et al. (2001, p. 766), who argued that a resilience assessment must specify which system configuration and which *"disturbances are of interest"*.

Food system resilience should be kept apart from neighbouring ideas. [[Resilience]] is the general property of any system; this page applies it to food systems and their food security function. [[Vulnerability]] describes exposure, sensitivity and lack of capacity, and is often treated as the reverse side of resilience (Ericksen et al., 2010). Sustainability is the capacity to preserve a system in the long run, while resilience is its capacity over time in the face of disturbances; Tendall et al. (2015) present the two as complementary. Resilience is also not the same as stability or risk management: a resilient food system is more of *"a safe-fail food system than a fail-safe food system"* (Tendall et al., 2015, p. 20). [[Food systems transformation]] overlaps with transformative capacity, but transformation can be pursued for reasons other than shocks.

**Working definition used here:** food system resilience is the capacity of a food system and its actors, from households and value chains to territories, countries and global trade, to keep delivering food security and nutrition in the face of shocks and stresses, by resisting, absorbing, adapting, recovering and, where the existing system is untenable, transforming in ways that do not shift risk onto the most vulnerable *(Synthesis of Tendall et al., 2015; Béné et al., 2023; HLPE, 2025)*.

## Features
**Components**
- Tendall et al. (2015, p. 19) break food system resilience into robustness, *"the capacity to withstand the disturbance in the first place"*, redundancy, *"the extent to which elements of the system are replaceable"*, the speed of recovery, and the learning that follows a shock.
- Actor-based frameworks group the same ideas into absorptive, adaptive and transformative capacities held by farmers, traders, processors, retailers, consumers and public agencies (Béné et al., 2012; Béné et al., 2023).
- Zurek et al. (2022, p. 511) condense the strategies open to food system actors into *"robustness, recovery, and reorientation"*: resisting a disruption, returning to the desired outcomes after it, or accepting different outcomes when the old ones can no longer be met.
- Reorientation is the actor-level counterpart of transformability. From social–ecological research, Biggs et al. (2012, p. 421) distil *"seven generic policy-relevant principles"*: maintain diversity and redundancy, manage connectivity, manage slow variables and feedbacks, treat systems as complex adaptive systems, encourage learning, broaden participation and promote polycentric governance.
- At system level these capacities combine with emergent properties such as *"connectivity, redundancy, diversity, competitiveness/rule of law, and inclusiveness"* (Béné et al., 2026, PDF p. 3). The components matter because of how they interact once a shock arrives.

**Mechanisms**
- Resilience works through buffering, substitution and reorganisation. Diversity and redundancy let one source of food, income or supply route replace another when the first fails, and Hertel et al. (2021) call diversification, of production, trade, value chains and household income, *"the most fundamental of these"* levers (p. 834, locator not verified).
- The need is growing: across five decades of production data, *"shock frequency has increased through time on land and sea"*, driven mainly by geopolitical and extreme-weather events (Cottrell et al., 2019, p. 130).
- Trade cuts both ways. Between 1987 and 2013, *"food supply diversity increased significantly for most of the world's population"*, but more people came to depend on imports, and many countries bought from fewer partners (Kummu et al., 2020, abstract).
- A panel analysis of national food systems offers *"one of the first empirical demonstrations of the buffering effect of redundancy"*, measured as the diversification of food supply sources (Béné et al., 2026, PDF p. 8).
- Supply chains are the channel: environmental shocks *"can be propagated or attenuated along food supply chains"*, and the response can come from individuals switching foods, firms switching sources or governments drawing on reserves (Davis et al., 2021, p. 54).
- Actors' responses are the second mechanism: households, firms and agencies draw on assets, networks and knowledge to cope, adapt or transform, and the mix of responses decides whether a shock passes through to food insecurity (Béné, 2020). Whether these mechanisms work depends on conditions outside the food chain itself.

**Conditions**
- Governance is the clearest enabling condition. Government effectiveness reduced the effect of a wide range of disasters on undernourishment, *"reinforcing its centrality in strengthening food system resilience"* (Béné et al., 2026, PDF p. 8); economic development also helped, while high food price volatility made outcomes worse.
- Equity is a second condition. Schipanski et al. (2016) argue that unequal access to land, capital and food undermines resilience, yet *"less than 6% of food security publications over the past 25 years"* addressed gender, equity or justice (p. 602).
- A third condition is subjective. In coastal fishing communities in Fiji, Ghana, Sri Lanka and Viet Nam, resilience depended partly on people's *"perceptions of their ability to cope, adapt or transform"* (Béné et al., 2016, p. 153). These conditions are hard to observe, which is why measurement is difficult.

**Measurement**
- Resilience is a latent capacity, so it is usually inferred from what happens after a shock.
- Béné et al. (2023) propose a framework for local food systems whose first component is *"the mapping of the actors and the local food system"*, followed by indicators of actors' capacities and responses and of food security outcomes (p. 1437).
- National studies use panel data on shocks, governance and food supply to test which factors weaken the link between shocks and undernourishment (Béné et al., 2026).
- Indicator sets cover the other scales. For farms and landscapes, Cabell and Oelofse (2012) argue that resilience cannot be measured directly and propose 13 behaviour-based indicators, such as diversity, connectivity and self-organisation, whose absence shows where to intervene.
- For countries, Seekell et al. (2017) combine socio-economic access, biophysical capacity and production diversity, and find that *"very few countries have exclusively high or low values for all dimensions"* (p. 9).
- FAO (2021) adds two flexibility indices, one for primary production and one for the range of sourcing pathways behind diets, and an assessment of how far food transport networks reach.
- Panel data collected before and after a shock allow stronger tests. In northern Bangladesh, households with more social and human capital, assets and livelihood diversity, safety nets, market access, women's empowerment and confidence to adapt suffered less loss of food security from the 2014 floods, although the authors call the evidence suggestive (Smith & Frankenberger, 2018).
- Modelling lags behind. A systematic review of studies in low- and middle-income countries found that *"most studies model the robustness of food systems and not their transformability"* (Meyer, 2020, PDF p. 1), and a review of global models concludes that the conceptualisation, assessment and *"modeling of systemic resilience are still in their infancy"* (Paulus et al., 2025, PDF p. 1).
- The midstream is a blind spot: little is known about how to *"strengthen the ability of processors, or traders, or street vendors"* to respond to shocks, especially in low- and middle-income countries (Béné, 2020, p. 810).
- A review of supply chain studies finds the same bias: most concern three staple grains, farm production, and extreme rain and heat (Davis et al., 2021). Choices about what to measure are not neutral, which leads to the debates.

**Debates**
- The first debate is about power. Critics argue that resilience thinking, born in ecology, shows an *"inability to appropriately capture and reflect social dynamics"*, including agency and power (Béné et al., 2012, p. 12).
- Cote and Nightingale (2012, p. 475) trace this to the way resilience was carried from ecology into society without its normative questions, and argue that *"power and competing value systems are not external to, but rather integral"* to social–ecological systems.
- The second debate is about what to restore. The HLPE (2025) and Anderson et al. (2026) argue that bounce-back approaches risk *"reinforcing the very structures that generated vulnerability"* (Anderson et al., 2026, p. 457), and call for equitable transformation.
- Zurek et al. (2022) add that robustness, recovery and reorientation favour different actors, so the trade-offs between them have to be negotiated rather than optimised.
- Capacity-based approaches respond that resilience still needs observable outcomes, such as food security after a shock, if it is to guide investment (Béné et al., 2023).
- The third debate is about scale. Some see relocalisation as the route to resilience; others defend long, just-in-time supply chains.
- Wood et al. (2023, p. 22) note that being local *"is no guarantee for greater sustainability"* and argue for applying resilience principles to governance at all scales.
- What remains unresolved is who decides which functions and whose food security count, and how to measure transformation rather than recovery.

## Implications

**Evidence overview.** The evidence base is large but uneven. Meyer's (2020) systematic review of food system and value chain resilience in low- and middle-income countries started from a Web of Science search that returned 367 publications (PDF p. 2). It found three clusters of work: global models of crop production, surveys of farmers' adaptive behaviour, and mostly qualitative studies of food systems and value chains; quantitative studies mainly address robustness. Béné's (2020) review of local food systems under COVID-19 found that most evidence concerns farmers and households, with little on traders, processors and vendors. The first global quantitative test, covering 191 countries, supports three buffers at national level: diversified food supply, government effectiveness and economic development (Béné et al., 2026). The HLPE (2025) adds that shocks are increasingly compound and that vulnerability is unequally distributed. FAO (2021) puts a number on this for diets: about 3 billion people cannot afford a healthy diet, and another 1 billion would join them if a shock cut their incomes by a third. In Viet Nam, a demand-system simulation found that a 10% fall in income and a 30% rise in rice prices push households towards rice at the expense of other foods, and that *"Low-income households face a higher risk of undernourishment"* (Hoang, 2018, p. 83). Overall, evidence is strongest on household coping and weakest on midstream actors, transformation and long-term outcomes.

**Cases.** The cases are grouped by level (household, territory or city, nation) and by type of shock (climate, market, pandemic, policy and environmental change) *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*. Viet Nam and ASEAN cases come first; the Ca Mau and UK cases are contested, and the rice crisis shows national measures weakening regional resilience.

**Cross-case lessons.** Three patterns recur. First, assets and finance help households and farmers recover (Béné et al., 2016; Smith & Frankenberger, 2018; Nguyen et al., 2019), but they do not explain all differences: perceptions, social capital and fair access matter too. Second, interventions aimed at one level can weaken resilience at another. Ca Mau's resource policies did not raise household resilience (Tran, 2012), and in the coastal Mekong Delta, policies that favour double rice made it harder for farmers to change systems as salinity rose (Nguyen et al., 2019). At regional scale, export restrictions by Viet Nam, India and others, meant to protect domestic consumers, helped turn an adequately supplied rice market into a price spike in 2007–08 (Dawe & Slayton, 2011). Diversity worked the other way in Moc Chau, where diverse food sources, decentralised flows and little reliance on external inputs kept the district food system working through the pandemic, helped by cautious public decisions (Bruckert & Nguyen, 2024). Third, at city and national level, diverse supply and capable public institutions are the strongest buffers (Béné et al., 2026; Schipanski et al., 2016). The UK debate shows that whether a food system *"is resilient"* depends on who is asked and which function they value. The cases say little about traders and processors, about transformation, or about recovery over many years. In Viet Nam, the evidence is mostly household- or farming-system-level; the Moc Chau study is the closest to a territorial food system assessment, and no national assessment was found.

## Comparison with my first take

- **Agrees.** You build resilience from resistance, adaptive capacity and recovery capacity. This matches Tendall et al.'s (2015) components (robustness, redundancy, recovery and learning), Zurek et al.'s (2022) robustness and recovery and the HLPE's (2025) list of resisting, absorbing, adapting to, recovering from and preventing shocks.
- **Extends.** You judge resilience by its outcomes (nutrition, health, socio-economic). Béné et al. (2023) make this measurable: actors' capacities are judged by food security after a shock, and Béné et al. (2026) measure it as decoupling food insecurity from shock severity. The literature also adds transformability (Walker et al., 2004) and prevention and learning (Tendall et al., 2015), which your definition leaves implicit.
- **Challenges.** Your definition aims at the *"ability to recover in long term"*, which assumes that the pre-shock system is worth recovering. Zurek et al. (2022) add reorientation, accepting different outcomes, as a third strategy. The HLPE (2025) and Anderson et al. (2026) argue that bouncing back can reinforce the structures that created vulnerability, and critics ask whose recovery counts (Béné et al., 2012; Cote & Nightingale, 2012).
