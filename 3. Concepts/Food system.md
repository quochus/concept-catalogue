## Definitions

The term *"food system"* was in use long before it had a settled meaning. Rural sociologists and nutritionists used it from the 1970s onwards to describe the chain that brings food from farm to plate, but few of them defined it. Sobal et al. (1998, p. 853) opened their review by noting that *"the food system is a widely used concept"* while few frameworks modelled its full scope. They proposed a model of the *food and nutrition system* with *"three subsystems (producer, consumer, nutrition) and nine stages"*, from production to metabolism (p. 853). Their definition, *"the set of operations and processes involved in transforming raw materials into foods"* and nutrients into health outcomes (p. 853), placed the system inside biophysical and sociocultural contexts. This answered a practical problem: nutrition, agriculture and food industry studies looked at separate links and missed how they were connected.

The concept was widened in the 2000s because food security research faced a new problem. Ingram (2011, p. 419) recalls that the food system idea was not new, since *"rural sociologists had promoted this approach for some years"*, but that global environmental change research needed it because *"focus needed to move beyond the impacts of climate change on crop productivity"*. In response, Ericksen (2008, p. 234) argued that food systems had usually been seen as *"a set of activities ranging from production through to consumption"*, and that this view was too narrow to explain food security. She broadened the concept to include the biophysical and human drivers that shape activities, and the outcomes of those activities for food security, environmental security and social welfare. *"The food security status of any group can be considered as the principal outcome"* of a food system, she wrote (p. 234). She also stressed that the activities and outcomes *"are also drivers of global environmental change"* (p. 235), so the system feeds back on its own drivers. Ingram (2011, p. 420) turned this into the GECAFS framework, in which food systems contain *"Activities which give rise to a number of food security Outcomes"*.

The definition most used today comes from the High Level Panel of Experts (HLPE). A food system *"gathers all the elements (environment, people, inputs, processes, infrastructures, institutions, etc.)"* and activities related to food, from production to consumption, plus their socio-economic and environmental outcomes (HLPE, 2017, p. 11). The HLPE framework names *"three constituent elements of food systems, as entry and exit points for nutrition"* (p. 11): food supply chains, the [[Food environment]] and [[Consumer behaviour]]. It also sorts the influences on these elements into *"five main categories of drivers of food system changes"* (p. 14): biophysical and environmental; innovation, technology and infrastructure; political and economic; socio-cultural; and demographic. The core components are therefore [[Food system activities]], [[Food system drivers]] and [[Food system outcomes]], tied together by feedback. Van Berkum et al. (2018, PDF p. 4) treat this last point as defining: *"Feedback loops are a distinguishing factor in systems thinking."*

Definitions still diverge, and for three reasons. The first is purpose. A system has no natural edge; its main function *"defines the otherwise arbitrary boundary of a system"* (Posthumus et al., 2018, p. 9), so a nutrition study, a climate study and a trade study draw different food systems. Von Braun et al. (2021, p. 749) make the same point: conceptualising food systems *"entails defining system boundaries and system building blocks"*. They also note that the UN Food Systems Summit's action tracks *"do not define the concept of food systems as such"* (p. 749). The second is discipline. Béné et al. (2019, p. 118) found four distinct narratives of food system failure in the literature (not enough food, poor diet quality, unequal access, and environmental damage), and used the term *"narrative"* *"to emphasize the constructed nature of the framings"* (p. 117). The third is politics. Political economy scholars define the food system less by its flows than by who controls it. De Schutter (2019, p. 13) describes this approach as one that *"places actors, and the power relationships between them, at the heart of its analysis"*. Food regime theory goes further and reads food as a relation of capital, *"refocusing from the food commodity as object to the commodity as relation"* (McMichael, 2009, p. 281).

The concept also needs boundaries with its neighbours. A food system is not the same as a [[Value chain]]: a chain follows one product and its actors, while a food system includes many chains, the food environment, consumers, drivers and outcomes. It is not the same as the [[Food systems approach]], which is a way of analysing and acting on food systems (van Berkum et al., 2018, PDF p. 4). And it is not the same as [[Sustainable food systems]], which add a normative goal to a descriptive term. [[Food system transitions]] and [[Food systems transformation]] describe change in food systems, not the system itself.

**Working definition used here:** a food system is the set of elements, actors and activities involved in producing, processing, distributing, preparing and consuming food, together with the drivers that shape them and the nutrition, socio-economic and environmental outcomes they produce, linked by feedback (based on HLPE, 2017, p. 11, and Ericksen, 2008, p. 234).

## Features
**Components**
- Most frameworks agree on four parts. Activities run from production through processing, distribution and retail to consumption and waste (Ericksen, 2008, p. 234).
- Actors include farmers, traders, processors, retailers, consumers and the state. Drivers sit outside or across the system: environmental, technological, economic, political, socio-cultural and demographic (HLPE, 2017, p. 14).
- Outcomes are what the system produces for people and the planet: food security and nutrition, livelihoods and income, and environmental effects.
- The HLPE adds a middle layer between activities and diets, the [[Food environment]], where people meet food in markets and shops (HLPE, 2017, p. 11). How these parts produce change is a question of mechanisms.

**Mechanisms**
- A food system produces its outcomes through interactions, not through any one part. Feedback is the main mechanism: outcomes such as soil depletion or rising incomes act back on production and consumption (van Berkum et al., 2018, PDF p. 4), and food system activities themselves drive environmental change (Ericksen, 2008, p. 235).
- A second mechanism is coupling between scales: national diets depend on global trade, and a change in one region's demand can move land use far away.
- Because of these interactions, von Braun et al. (2021, p. 749) stress that *"food systems are in a continuous state of change and adaptation."* What a system produces therefore depends strongly on the conditions it operates in.

**Conditions**
- Food systems differ by income level, urbanisation, market structure and governance. In many low- and middle-income countries, traditional and informal markets supply most food. In such places formal frameworks are hard to apply, because informal systems *"present logistical difficulties for accurate data collection"* (Liddy et al., 2023, PDF p. 2).
- Several kinds of food system can also exist side by side in one country, from dominant to niche and hybrid forms (Gaitán-Cremaschi et al., 2019, PDF p. 3), and *"the boundaries between the dominant food systems and the niche food systems are blurry"* (PDF p. 6).
- Leeuwis et al. (2021, p. 762) make the same point in systems terms: rather than one food system with one logic, *"there exist multiple parallel systems that serve different producers and consumers"*.
- In Viet Nam, Raneri et al. (2019, p. iii) describe food systems that *"are undergoing rapid transformation"*, with modern and traditional retail growing together.
- Policy has tried to speed up one side of this: in Hanoi the government relied on *"replacing wet markets with supermarkets"* to manage food safety, yet this modernisation *"reaches only a minor segment of the population"* (Wertheim-Heck et al., 2015, p. 95).
- Because the concept must work across such different conditions, measuring it is a challenge of its own.

**Measurement**
- Studies operationalise food systems in three main ways. The first is indicator sets and dashboards. The Food Systems Dashboard gathers national data to help decision makers *"identify their levers of change and decide which ones need to be pulled"* (Fanzo et al., 2020, p. 243), and it lets users compare countries *"based on a food system typology"* (p. 244).
- The second is profiling and mapping of a place, such as a province or a city region, using the HLPE elements and drivers as headings (Huynh et al., 2021).
- The third is commodity-based mapping that follows value chains through the system. Alarcon et al. (2021, PDF p. 1) report that in food system studies in low- and middle-income countries, *"value chain mapping of key commodities often missing"* is a recurring gap.
- Each method draws the system boundary differently, which is one reason the concept remains debated.

**Debates**
- Three debates stand out. The first is about breadth. Supporters argue that a broad concept is needed to see trade-offs between nutrition, livelihoods and environment (Ericksen, 2008; HLPE, 2017).
- Critics reply that a concept that includes everything is hard to operationalise and gives little guidance on where to act; Posthumus et al. (2018, p. 9) answer that boundaries must follow the question being asked.
- The second is about power. Mainstream frameworks list political and economic factors as one group of drivers, but De Schutter (2019, p. 13) and McMichael (2009, p. 281) argue that this treats power as one factor among others, when it shapes how the whole system is organised.
- The third is about modern and traditional systems. Typologies that rank food systems from traditional to modern can imply a single path of development, while Gaitán-Cremaschi et al. (2019) and Vetter et al. (2019) show that different systems co-exist and interact.
- What remains unresolved is whether one definition can serve both science and politics, or whether, as von Braun et al. (2021) suggest, the concept must be adjusted to each use.

## Implications

**Evidence overview.** Reviews show that the food system concept is now widely used, but applied unevenly. Alarcon et al. (2021) reviewed food systems theory and its application in low- and middle-income countries. They found gaps in food system mapping and governance, and a growing use of the label *"informal"* food system, *"a term used inconsistently and one that requires revision"* (PDF p. 1). Gaitán-Cremaschi et al. (2019) reviewed how food system diversity is characterised and found that, while research on food system transitions abounds, there was no operational method to map several co-existing food systems in one place; they proposed one. Liddy et al. (2023) reviewed food mapping in three city regions and found that informal flows and fast urban growth limit what mapping can show. The broadest systematic evidence comes from an evidence gap map of food system interventions in low- and middle-income countries. Moore et al. (2021, p. iii) report that *"a total of 1,838 impact evaluations and 178 systematic reviews were identified"*, most on nutrition-specific measures such as *"the provision of supplements, fortification, classes in the consumer behaviour domain"*. *"Roughly three quarters of impact evaluations implemented randomised designs"* (p. iii), which suits single interventions better than system-wide change. No systematic review of food system cases as such was found. Across these reviews the evidence is strongest for describing structure (who supplies what, through which channels) and weakest for linking structure to nutrition and environmental outcomes.

**Cases.** The cases below are chosen to span a typology of food system analyses by scale and type *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: national or regional profiles, city-region systems, co-existing system types within a country, commodity systems, telecoupled systems that reach across borders, and a contested modernisation case.

**Cross-case lessons.** The cases show that a food system is always drawn for a purpose: a nutrition profile, a commodity diagnosis or a trade assessment each produce a different system from the same reality. Where the HLPE elements are used, as in Viet Nam, they make places comparable, but they can hide the co-existence of traditional, informal and modern channels that the Chilean, Indonesian and Vietnamese cases all report. The city-region and telecoupling cases show that the boundary of a food system rarely matches an administrative one. What the evidence does not show is a reliable link from food system structure to outcomes: most studies describe structure well and infer outcomes, which is why measurement tools such as the Food Systems Dashboard and city-region frameworks, which Blay-Palmer et al. (2018, p. 4) call *"a promising analytical and theoretical tool"*, are still being tested.
