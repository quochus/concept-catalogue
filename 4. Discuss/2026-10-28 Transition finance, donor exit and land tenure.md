## Question explored
How can farmers get through the costly years of transition, and how can changes outlast the projects that start them?
- **Q6.** Given that regenerative practices often bring early yield penalties and high upfront labour costs, what blended finance and risk-sharing mechanisms (transition insurance, carbon credits, targeted subsidies) could bridge the multi-season revenue gap?
- **Q21.** How can donor-funded programmes avoid subsidy dependency, or success stories that collapse when funding ends?
- **Q23.** How can insecure land tenure and customary rights in ASEAN be reconciled with the multi-year investment that soil restoration and agroforestry need?

## Main points
**Transition debt (Gemini).** Smallholders with tight cash flow cannot absorb several seasons of lower yields or extra labour (cover crops, bio-inputs) without outside liquidity. Commercial finance and input subsidies are locked into conventional farming.

**Q6, making finance work where institutions are weak (Gemini).** Blended finance and index insurance look good in policy papers but are hard to run top-down where the state has weak capacity and financial inclusion is low. Gemini suggested three routes:
- **Use existing social infrastructure.** Channel transition grants and vouchers through organised cooperatives, PGS or NGO networks, which act as local guarantors. This avoids farm-by-farm distribution by a weak ministry.
- **Finance through the value chain.** Processors or exporters advance bio-inputs and advice and recover the cost at harvest. This is contract farming, with legal safeguards for fairness.
- **Keep risk buffers simple.** Weather-index insurance suffers from basis risk and missing data. Vouchers tied to verifiable practice change, or guarantee funds managed by producer groups, work better in low-capacity settings.

**Q21, exit strategies (Gemini).**
- Declining matching grants, e.g. 80/20 grant-to-farmer in year 1, 50/50 in year 2, 20/80 in year 3, then commercial.
- Use donor money to capitalise cooperative bio-input hubs and youth agri-service firms, instead of handing out free inputs.
- Hand verification and extension over to district budgets or provincial plans (SEDP) before the final project year.
- Tie support to off-take contracts, public (school feeding) or private.

After Quoc's reply, Gemini added: projects often build parallel extension structures with paid consultants, and these vanish when the project ends. Programmes should work through public extension from day one, co-design training of trainers with extension officers, and write regenerative modules into national extension mandates.

**Q23, land tenure (Gemini).** Soil restoration, agroforestry and perennials take "3 to 7 years" to pay off. Tenants on informal one- or two-year leases have no reason to invest, because the owner can take back improved land. Gemini proposed:
- standard long-term lease templates (10–15 years) that protect the tenant's right to recoup soil investments;
- faster land-use rights certificates (*sổ đỏ* in Viet Nam), and group or cooperative titles that can serve as collateral;
- lease clauses that reward measured soil improvement with renewal guarantees or rent discounts.

## Where I landed
*My own conclusions.*
- Q6: The earlier answer seemed to address it. But again, how do we get these mechanisms implemented and well coordinated, given the often weak institutions of LMICs and developing ASEAN countries? (After Gemini's reply: "We have addressed Q6 then.")
- Q21 had already been addressed in earlier questions. That's why local agencies like extension are critical.
- Q23: Change laws and policies on land tenure, and set up support mechanisms for long-term land rental.
