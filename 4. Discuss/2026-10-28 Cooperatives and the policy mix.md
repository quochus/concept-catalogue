> From Quoc's Gemini chat on scaling sustainable agri-food systems ([[Q&A for values, connectivity & scaling|overview of all 27 questions]]). **Main points** condense Gemini's answers. **Where I landed** gives Quoc's own replies, lightly edited for typos. Gemini's figures, cases and references are unchecked unless they link to Zotero.

## Question explored
Do the policy tools we already have (taxes, standards, ESG and so on) reach the cooperative mechanisms that smallholders need? And how do other countries treat cooperatives?
- **Q2.** To what extent do top-down, state-mandated certification schemes (such as national GAP) act as bureaucratic barriers rather than market enablers for smallholder value chains? How can PGS be legally institutionalised without losing their grassroots flexibility?
- **Q7.** In export-oriented and commodity chains, what institutional mechanisms ensure that the value created by better environmental resilience and product quality is captured at the farm gate, not absorbed by midstream intermediaries?

## Main points
**Policy instruments by category, and what each misses (Gemini)**

| Category | Examples | Blind spot |
|---|---|---|
| Fiscal and economic | Input subsidies, green tax breaks, carbon credits, PES, green credit lines, risk-sharing transition grants | Flow through banks or large aggregators; collateral and audit rules exclude informal or undercapitalised cooperatives |
| Regulatory and standards | CamGAP, VietGAP, phytosanitary rules, eco-label laws, agrochemical zoning, due diligence laws | Assume each producer can absorb certification costs alone |
| Market-facing and transparency | Digital traceability, blockchain, QR disclosure, green public procurement | Favour processors and supermarkets; bypass wet markets and local cooperatives |
| Institutional and participatory | Multi-stakeholder platforms (e.g. CASIC in Cambodia), PGS, farmer field schools, cooperative law | This is where cooperative mechanisms sit, but they are treated as "soft" project add-ons |

Gemini agreed with Quoc that most instruments treat farmers as isolated economic units. Without cooperatives, farmers stay atomised price-takers, and collectors and lenders capture the gains. Its examples were freshwater fish and cashew in Battambang, taken from Quoc's own files. Gemini described cooperatives as operational infrastructure for group certification, bulk input buying, shared cold storage and collective bargaining.

**How different countries treat cooperatives (Gemini; none of these claims are sourced)**
- **OECD (EU, US).** Cooperatives are permanent pillars. In France, Germany and the Netherlands they channel eco-scheme payments, advice and collective compliance. Their drawbacks are antitrust scrutiny of large cooperatives and resistance to top-down mandates such as Farm to Fork.
- **LMICs in Southeast Asia.** Cooperatives depend on projects and are fragile. They cannot fund group certification, traceability or storage. National GAP then excludes smallholders and pushes them back into collector-driven chains.
- **South Korea and Taiwan.** The state built centralised cooperatives (e.g. NACF in South Korea) as the single channel for credit, inputs, extension and marketing. The same channel now carries eco-schemes.
- **Latin America.** Social movements, PGS and institutional demand. Brazil's Food Acquisition Programme (PAA) buys from family-farm cooperatives, with a premium for agroecological food. These policies are often defunded when governments change.
- **China.** Cooperatives are promoted by law (the Farmers' Professional Cooperatives Law) and linked to "dragon head" enterprises. This brings fast capital and standards, but smallholders have little say over price or production.

**Gemini's synthesis.** Treat cooperatives as permanent public-interest infrastructure, not as aid beneficiaries:
- legally recognise PGS;
- channel subsidies, transition credit and technical help through cooperatives;
- write cooperative sourcing into public procurement;
- protect cooperative governance from midstream buyers.

**Q7 through the four value processes (Gemini).** Value is created on farm (creation), codified by standards (translation), recognised by buyers who pay more (recognition), and then often lost to collectors, lenders and traders (capture). Cooperatives cut the cost of translation through group certification and aggregation. They help capture through bargaining power and direct channels.

## Where I landed
*My own conclusions.*
- In reality, smallholders rarely have enough power, agency and resources to start the changes without external support. They are critical to successful scaling, but scaling needs cross-level cooperation and significant investment from other actors: governments, development agencies and firms.
- We already have many tools (taxation, standards, ESG and so on), but they still don't touch key issues like cooperative mechanisms. Maybe I'm wrong.
- Q7 has already been touched on. The key is strengthening local cooperatives and farmers and integrating them as key actors. This relates to value creation, translation, recognition and capture.

## Open questions
- What would "cooperatives as infrastructure" mean under the cooperative laws of Viet Nam and Cambodia?
- The country comparison needs literature. Zotero has Giraldo & McCune (2019) on Latin America and Grisa (2021) on Brazil's PAA. Gemini gave no sources for Korea, Taiwan, China or the OECD.
- *Claude's note:* Gemini presents state-built cooperatives as a success. Cooperatives can also be captured by local elites or run top-down by the state. That is worth checking before using Korea or China as models (see [[2026-10-28 Power, co-optation and the politics of RA]]).
