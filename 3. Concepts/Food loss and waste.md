## My first take
*Written by Quoc before reading the draft (2026-11-13). Never edited later.*

food loss: during harvest and distribution
food waste: during retailing and consumption
The issue is important as they not only affect the effectiveness of the food value chains but also have significant climate/environmental, social and some nutritional effects


## Definitions
Concern with lost food began as a concern with post-harvest loss in poor countries. Affognon et al. (2015, p. 49) date it precisely: *"Interest in PHL dates back from the first World Food Conference of 1974"*, which resolved to halve post-harvest losses by 1985. In response, FAO set up the Special Action Programme for the Prevention of Food Losses in 1977 (Affognon et al., 2015, p. 49). The problem it answered was hunger after the food price crises of the 1970s, and the logic was simple: food saved after harvest is food that does not need to be grown (Stathers et al., 2020, p. 821). The early concept was narrow: the first programmes targeted stored grain, and only *"by the early 1980s, the scope was broadened"* to roots and tubers and to fresh fruit and vegetables (Affognon et al., 2015, p. 49).

For three decades the topic stayed a technical, agronomic one. Cattaneo et al. (2021, p. 2) note that *"Before 2010, the bulk of the literature on FLW was mostly technical in nature"*. Its own evidence base was also ageing: a 2010 review of food waste found that estimates *"for post-harvest losses of grain in developing countries might be overestimated"* and that, because most data were decades old, *"current global losses cannot be quantified"* (Parfitt et al., 2010, p. 3065). The shift came with FAO's 2011 report *Global food losses and food waste* (Gustavsson et al., 2011, as cited in Flanagan et al., 2019, p. 4), which extended the frame from the farm to the plate and gave the field its headline: *"one-third of all food is lost or wasted between the farm and the plate"* (Flanagan et al., 2019, p. 4). Three things changed at once. The scope widened from post-harvest losses to retail and household waste, so high-income countries entered the picture. The rationale widened from hunger to resources and climate: lost and wasted food accounts for about 8 percent of annual greenhouse gas emissions and *"consumes a quarter of all water used by agriculture"* (Flanagan et al., 2019, p. 5). And the issue became a policy target. SDG target 12.3 (2015) asks countries to halve per capita food waste at retail and consumer level and to reduce food losses along production and supply chains (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). This split the concept into two indicators with two custodians: FAO's estimate of losses, about 14 percent of production (Cattaneo et al., 2021, p. 3), and UNEP's Food Waste Index, which put 2022 waste at retail, food service and household level at 1.05 billion tonnes, about one-fifth of the food available to consumers (UNEP, 2024). A further shift followed when economists and nutritionists entered the field. Economists argued that losses are choices made under prices and risks (Cattaneo et al., 2021, p. 2); others began to count nutrients, water and land rather than tonnes (Kok et al., 2024, p. 7).

The definition most used today starts from intent. Food loss and waste is *"any food intended for human consumption that ultimately is never eaten by humans"* (Spang et al., 2019, p. 118). FAO's working definition, used in its 2019 *State of Food and Agriculture* and in country profiles for Viet Nam, divides it by who makes the decision. Food loss is the decrease in quantity or quality caused by suppliers from production up to, but not including, retail; food waste is the decrease caused by retailers, food service and consumers (FAO, 2019, as cited in Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). The same source excludes food eaten beyond nutritional needs and food that only loses market value through oversupply. The main components are therefore the stage where food leaves the chain (on-farm, [[Post-harvest handling and storage]], processing, distribution, retail, household), the kind of loss (quantity or quality), and the destination of what is lost (feed, compost, energy or landfill). [[Cold chain]] and packaging sit on the boundary as the main technical means of prevention.

Definitions still disagree. Spang et al. (2019, p. 119) find *"a remarkable diversity of definitions for FLW"* and trace it to three choices: the stages of the chain included, whether inedible parts count, and what end uses count as waste. Some definitions do not separate loss from waste at all (Spang et al., 2019, p. 121). Some count food sent to compost or anaerobic digestion as recovered rather than wasted; a "zootechnical" view counts only food eaten by neither people nor animals; and some add overconsumption beyond metabolic needs (Spang et al., 2019, p. 121). Instead of a yes/no line between edible and inedible, some authors use "avoidability", which *"recognizes a spectrum of edibility"* (Spang et al., 2019, p. 121). These choices follow purpose more than discipline. Cattaneo et al. (2021, p. 2) note that whether loss is best measured in tonnes, nutrients or money depends on the policy objective. They also follow income level and politics: in low-income countries the problem is framed as missing infrastructure, in high-income countries as social norms about leaving food on the plate (Flanagan et al., 2019, p. 6).

The concept has clear neighbours. It is wider than post-harvest loss, which covers only the upstream part. It is not the same as food surplus: some oversupply is a buffer that keeps a food system stable (Bajželj et al., 2020, p. 1; see [[Food system resilience]]). It is not the same as the [[Circular economy in agri-food]], which mostly deals with what to do with food once it is lost, whereas the food recovery hierarchy puts prevention first (Spang et al., 2019, p. 135). It does not include yield gaps: the agricultural production stage covers actual production and harvest losses only (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 4). And it is not a goal in itself but a means to [[Food security]], climate or income goals (Cattaneo et al., 2021, p. 2).

**Working definition used here:** Food loss and waste is food grown or harvested for people that leaves the human food chain, or loses quality, and is not eaten; food loss happens from harvest up to retail, and food waste happens at retail, in food service and in households (based on FAO, 2019, as cited in Axmann et al., 2024; wording is Claude's synthesis).

## Features
**Components**
- Two halves by chain stage: losses occur from harvest through storage, processing and distribution, while *"food waste occurs further down the FSC, i.e., retail and consumer levels"* (Spang et al., 2019, p. 121).
- Quantity and quality loss: food can disappear (spillage, rot, pests) or stay in the chain with lower nutritional value, safety or price; quality losses lead to food-borne hazards and economic losses when produce misses its market (Affognon et al., 2015, p. 50).
- Edible and inedible parts, and the "avoidability" spectrum between them, which decides whether peels, bones and husks count (Spang et al., 2019, p. 121).
- Destinations ranked in the food recovery hierarchy: *"source reduction, followed by feeding hungry people, feeding animals"*, then industrial uses, composting, and last landfill and incineration (Spang et al., 2019, p. 135).
- Embedded footprints: each tonne lost carries the greenhouse gas emissions, water, land and nutrients used to produce it, which is why country profiles report all of them together (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).

**Mechanisms**
- Proximate causes sit in handling and coordination. Chauhan et al. (2021, p. 1) find the main factors are *"poor management of perishable food items, stakeholder attitudes, buyer supplier agreements"* and supply chain interruptions.
- Climate drives upstream loss: *"high heat and humidity and post-harvest rainfall"* are major causes, especially where farmers sun-dry crops (Delgado et al., 2021, p. 751).
- Loss is often an economic choice rather than carelessness. Delgado et al. (2021, p. 750) warn that it is *"too simplistic to blame it on the carelessness of producers"* or vendors; Cattaneo et al. (2021, p. 2) conclude that *"FLW is largely driven by economic decisionmaking and behaviors of actors"*, and that *"Economic incentives for adoption of FLW reducing technologies are weak"*.
- Causes are chained: an instance of loss is *"often driven by more than one driver"*, as when rice is lost in poor storage bags because the grower lacks credit to buy better ones (Flanagan et al., 2019, p. 6).
- Missing refrigeration multiplies losses of perishables: poor cold chains may account for *"up to 620 million metric tons"* of food loss a year (Friedman-Heiman & Miller, 2024, p. 1).
- Downstream, waste comes from household routines more than from stated intentions. In Ho Chi Minh City, *"intention does not significantly translate into reduced waste"*, while cooking ability and knowing family preferences do reduce it (Phan et al., 2026, p. 1858; see [[Intention–behaviour gap]]).

**Conditions**
- Income level shapes where FLW occurs: *"losses are much higher at the immediate post-harvest stages in developing countries"*, while in affluent economies *"post-consumer food waste accounts for the greatest overall losses"* (Parfitt et al., 2010, p. 3065), and *"lack of infrastructure is typically a more significant driver in low-income countries"* (Flanagan et al., 2019, p. 6).
- Hot climates raise both losses and household waste; UNEP links warmer countries' higher household waste to more fresh food with inedible parts and weak cold chains (UNEP, 2024).
- Power in the chain decides who bears the loss. In Vietnamese lychee farming, losses include *"economic losses from farmers' weak market power"*, and cooperative membership and Good Agricultural Practice have the largest effects on reducing them (Pham et al., 2025, p. 40).
- Gender matters because postharvest work is often done by women, so loss reduction affects *"especially women, who are traditionally responsible for many postharvest activities"* (Stathers et al., 2020, p. 821).
- Adoption of loss-reducing technology depends on more than the technology: for mangosteen farmers in Indonesia, *"capabilities, infrastructure, and financial access were the critical factors for farmers"* (Fauziana et al., 2023, p. 1).

**Measurement**
- The *Food Loss and Waste Accounting and Reporting Standard* *"was established in 2016 to outline a consistent process"* for governments, firms and NGOs to measure FLW (Spang et al., 2019, p. 132).
- SDG 12.3 is tracked with two global indicators: FAO's estimate of losses from farm to distribution, about 14 percent (Cattaneo et al., 2021, p. 3), and UNEP's Food Waste Index for retail, food service and households (UNEP, 2024).
- The *"Target-Measure-Act"* approach asks governments and companies to set a target, measure where food is lost, and act on hotspots (Flanagan et al., 2019, p. 4).
- Evidence is thin and uneven: *"significant data gaps remain"*, especially for developing countries (Spang et al., 2019, p. 118); commonly quoted loss rates of 10–70 percent come *"often from untraceable sources"* (Affognon et al., 2015, p. 50); and only *"28 documents (13.1%) reported quality losses"* in the sub-Saharan Africa meta-analysis (Affognon et al., 2015, p. 56).
- Where primary data are missing, countries rely on mass-flow models that combine production and trade statistics with stage-specific loss rates from the literature (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).
- The global evidence base is thin and skewed: of 202 publications reviewed, *"over half of them are based only on secondary data"*, and FAO's 2011 estimate of household waste in Asia and Africa does *"not have a single measured data point"* (Xue et al., 2017, pp. 6618–6619). For fruit and vegetables, a 2025 review warns against *"blind interventions that are not guided by reliable evidence"* (Ambuko et al., 2025, p. 1).
- Tonnes can be *"translated into other impact factors"*: for Viet Nam's rice chain up to processing, losses correspond to roughly 7.8–8.0 million tonnes CO2-eq and 0.54–0.56 million tonnes of protein (Kok et al., 2024, p. 7).

**Debates**
- The size of the problem. Advocates use the one-third figure as a call to action (Flanagan et al., 2019, p. 4); FAO and IFPRI economists reply that the figure has *"stuck with many observers"* and policymakers as a stylised fact even though the data behind it are weak (Cattaneo et al., 2021, p. 1); many later studies simply reuse FAO's 2011 figures, which may not fit particular countries or foods (Xue et al., 2017, p. 6619).
- Efficiency versus distribution. Less loss means more food at lower prices for net buyers, but *"reducing losses could harm producers because of lower prices"*, so *"FLW reduction should be treated as an intermediate goal"* tied to a clear final objective (Cattaneo et al., 2021, p. 2).
- Waste versus resilience. Bajželj et al. (2020, p. 1) argue that *"Over-production and over-supply are a contributing cause of waste"* but *"they also provide resilience in the form of redundancy"*; good storage resolves part of the tension because it *"replaces it with a stock of 'spare' food"*.
- Technology versus systems. Most research tests storage technologies for farmers (Stathers et al., 2020, p. 821), and Spang et al. (2019, p. 118) find that *"FLW solutions focus more on proximate causes rather than larger systemic drivers"*.
- Cold chains versus shorter chains. Optimised cold chains save large volumes, but localised supply chains *"may save greater quantities of food than optimized cold chains"* for some foods, and priorities differ when the aim is tonnes rather than emissions (Friedman-Heiman & Miller, 2024, p. 1).
- Still unresolved: how much environmental and food security benefit loss reduction delivers once price and market feedbacks are counted (Cattaneo et al., 2021, p. 2), and how to compare estimates built on different definitions.

## Implications
**Evidence overview.** The best-reviewed evidence is on post-harvest loss in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. A scoping review screened 12,907 studies and kept *"only 334 of the 12,907 studies"*; *"Storage technology interventions mainly targeting farmers dominated"*, and the authors call for *"interventions beyond technologies or handling practice changes"* such as training, finance, infrastructure, policy and markets (Stathers et al., 2020, pp. 821–822). The 2024 update raised the evidence base to 457 studies but found it still made up of *"predominantly tangible technologies"*, with *"23 countries had none"*; it did find *"New evidence on postharvest training bundled with various technologies"* (Stathers et al., 2025, p. 1). Affognon et al. (2015, p. 58) found that the *"majority of the technologies target the abatement of losses occurring at farm-level"*. Across the whole chain, a systematic review of 152 articles found eight research themes, including *"digitalisation and food surplus redistribution"* (Chauhan et al., 2021, p. 1). More broadly, *"Most existing studies on FLW are conducted in industrialized countries"*, with downstream waste studied mainly there and post-harvest loss in a few developing countries (Xue et al., 2017, p. 6619). Spang et al. (2019, p. 146) conclude that *"concepts and definitions are beginning to converge"* and that responses work best as *"a network of complementary solutions that can be combined"*, but warn that most US food waste policies sit at the bottom of the hierarchy, in recycling (p. 144), and that *"FLW recovery is not a comprehensive solution to lack of access to food"* (p. 142). Evidence on nutritional, environmental and distributional outcomes of interventions is thin throughout.

Cases are chosen to span chain stage and type of response *(Claude's grouping, not from a source)*: measurement and hotspots, on-farm and storage technology, cooling, chain coordination, household behaviour, and redistribution and valorisation.

**Cross-case lessons.** Losses are highest where perishable food meets weak infrastructure, hot and wet seasons, and weak bargaining power, and farmers usually carry them. Field measurement can overturn models: measured on-farm rice loss in the Mekong Delta was far below the share of rice the national model assumes is lost across the chain, and the remaining uncertainty sits in drying and milling (Kok et al., 2024, pp. 1, 3). Technology alone rarely moves: cold storage and better handling exist in Indonesia and Viet Nam but adoption depends on finance, skills, risk, cooperatives and coordination between chain actors, which matches the review finding that interventions beyond technology are under-studied. Downstream, stated intentions are a poor guide to behaviour, so campaigns aimed at attitudes may disappoint. Grassroots initiatives matter more for agenda-setting than for tonnes. What the evidence does not show is whether loss reductions improve diets, cut emissions or raise farm incomes at scale once prices adjust; few studies follow those outcomes.

## Comparison with my first take
- **Agrees.** Your split by chain stage, with loss upstream and waste at retail and consumption, matches FAO's working definition, which separates loss (suppliers, before retail) from waste (retailers, food service, consumers) (FAO, 2019, as cited in Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 1). Your point that the costs go beyond the value chain is also well supported: FLW carries about 8 percent of greenhouse gas emissions and a quarter of agricultural water (Flanagan et al., 2019, p. 5), and country profiles count the nutrients lost alongside emissions, water and land (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 1).
- **Extends.** Two things your take leaves out. First, loss is about quality as well as quantity: food that stays in the chain but loses nutrients, safety or price is also loss, though few studies measure it (Affognon et al., 2015, pp. 50, 56). Second, the starting point is debated. Some definitions count losses from the field (food grown), others from harvest or the farm gate (Spang et al., 2019, p. 121), and none count yield gaps (Axmann et al., 2024, PDF p. 4).
- **Challenges.** Your take treats lower FLW as making value chains more effective. The economic literature is more cautious: losses are often rational choices under low prices and weak incentives, and reducing them can lower producer prices and incomes (Cattaneo et al., 2021, p. 2). Some surplus also acts as a buffer that makes food systems more resilient (Bajželj et al., 2020, p. 1). So FLW reduction is best treated as a means to a stated goal, not a goal in itself.
