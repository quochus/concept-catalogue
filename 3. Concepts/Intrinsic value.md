## Definitions
The idea that some things are good in themselves, and not only as means, is one of the oldest in ethics. It became a working concept in conservation when environmental philosophers in the 1970s and 1980s argued that species and ecosystems deserve protection whatever their use to people. Science–policy documents then set it against economic value: "something may either have dignity—intrinsic value—or a price—instrumental value" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 36). The problem it answered was moral as much as practical. If nature is valued only for what it gives, a species with no known use, or a landscape that can be replaced, has no claim to protection.

The IPBES approach defines intrinsic value as "the value something has independent of any human experience or evaluation" (Pascual et al., 2017, p. 14). A systematic review found four ways the term is used: as the opposite of instrumental value, as the value of something that is an end in itself, as value "independent of human interests or well-being", and as "the inherent moral value (in the sense of being a holder of rights)" of other-than-human beings (Himes et al., 2024, p. 29). The review added "the value of entities that are worth protecting as ends in themselves" to the core meaning (Himes et al., 2024, p. 29).

Definitions diverge on who holds the value. Philosophers and many conservation biologists treat it as objective: Piccolo (2017, p. 8) calls its recognition "a fundamental and non-negotiable aspect of an eco-evolutionary worldview". Others treat it as something people articulate. O'Connor and Kenter (2019, p. 1247) "conceive these as ends without reference to humans as valuers", "but which nonetheless can be articulated by people". Economists take a third view, and "tend to view intrinsic values as partly connected to a person’s well-being" through non-use or existence value (Pascual et al., 2023, p. 813). There is also a subjective sense, in which something is "valuable for its own sake to them and not for its usefulness" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 30). The differences follow discipline: ethics seeks a ground for duties, valuation needs something that can be elicited, and economics fits it into preferences.

Intrinsic value should be kept apart from its neighbours. [[Instrumental value]] is worth as a means to human ends. [[Relational values]] lie in meaningful relationships between people and nature, and differ from intrinsic values because most framings of intrinsic value disregard relationships. In food quality research, "intrinsic attributes" means something else: the physical properties of a product, as opposed to extrinsic cues such as price, brand or label; see [[Credence attributes]] *(Claude's note, to be sourced on that page)*.

**Working definition used here:** intrinsic value is the worth that a species, ecosystem, animal or other part of nature has as an end in itself, independent of its usefulness to people; it grounds duties and rights rather than trade-offs, and it can be articulated by people even when it is not held to depend on them *(based on Pascual et al., 2017; Himes et al., 2024; O'Connor & Kenter, 2019)*.

## Features
**Components.** Intrinsic value has a holder (a species, ecosystem, animal or nature as a whole), a moral claim attached to it, and, in the articulated view, people who express it. Intrinsic values "are strongly and often explicitly associated with nonanthropocentric worldviews" (Himes et al., 2024, p. 30), and they are "closely associated with biocentric and ecocentric worldviews" that still support conservation (Himes et al., 2024, p. 36). In surveys they show up as moral duties: in a Colombian watershed, intrinsic values were "often represented as moral duties" (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 1). How they shape action is the mechanism.

**Mechanisms.** Intrinsic value works through obligation, not exchange. In Piccolo's words, "recognition of value entails the obligation to do what is right" (Piccolo, 2017, p. 8). This makes it non-negotiable and hard to trade off, which is why it supports protected areas and rights-based arguments. It also motivates people: O'Connor and Kenter (2019, p. 1248) report Batavia and Nelson's argument that recognising intrinsic value is "logical, as it is a key reason that conservation exists", as well as practical and ethical. In value chains the same logic appears in standards: zero-deforestation targets value nature for carbon but "also in relational and intrinsic terms of biodiversity to be protected" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 4). Whether intrinsic values are voiced depends on conditions.

**Conditions.** Who people are and how they relate to nature matter. In the Colombian study, an egoistic motivation was "associated with a lower probability of expressing intrinsic values", while respondents who visited the area "for aesthetic appreciation were more likely to express intrinsic values" (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 9). Framing also matters: appeals to intrinsic or instrumental values alone "can be constraining or possibly alienating" to people who might care more under a different framing (Klain et al., 2017, PDF p. 2). These conditions make intrinsic value hard to measure.

**Measurement.** Intrinsic value resists money. It is typically "difficult to elicit, let alone compare with anthropocentric values" (O'Connor & Kenter, 2019, p. 1248). Researchers use qualitative interviews, value statements in surveys and deliberative methods. Indicators of outcome can stand in for it: in certification, "Intrinsic values refer to the extinctions avoided" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF p. 4). Yet "Instrumental values are elicited more often than intrinsic and relational values" (Pascual et al., 2023, p. 815), so intrinsic value is often absent from the evidence decision-makers see. That gap is at the centre of the debates.

**Debates.** The first debate is whether the intrinsic–instrumental split helps. Chan et al. (2016) called it an unhelpful dichotomy over "whether nature should be protected for humans’ sake" or "for nature’s sake (intrinsic values)", and proposed relational values as a third type (as summarised in Piccolo, 2017, p. 8). Piccolo (2017, p. 8) replied that this over-simplifies the philosophy and that intrinsic value remains central to conservation. Himes et al. (2024, p. 29) add that "the negative definition has limited usefulness by itself", unless one accepts a strict dualism. The second debate is about policy. Conservation "policies have frequently prioritized nature’s intrinsic values" (Pascual et al., 2023, p. 819), sometimes at the cost of the livelihoods of people living around protected areas, while elsewhere decision-making "relies to a great extent on the instrumental values" of nature (Pascual et al., 2017, p. 11). What remains unresolved is how to give intrinsic values weight in decisions without either pricing them or setting them against people's needs.

## Implications
- **Spain: women-led agroecological initiatives.** Only a few interviewees voiced intrinsic values, but they did so strongly: "For me, it's fascinating to encounter a wolf", and wild animals "such as the wolf, have the moral right to exist" (Vizuete et al., 2025, pp. 688–689).
- **Colombia: a watershed.** Relational values were mentioned most, "followed by intrinsic values" at 40.7% of respondents, with instrumental values far behind (Arias-Arévalo et al., 2017, PDF p. 6).
- **Wetland management.** Managing a wetland only for fisheries and tourism can raise instrumental values but "erode other intrinsic values, such as keystone species’ habitat" (Pascual et al., 2023, p. 815).
- **Sustainability certification.** Certification "encompassing instrumental, relational, and intrinsic values" can link consumers' "relational and intrinsic values reinforcing on-farm instrumental values" (Leimona et al., 2024, PDF pp. 1, 7).

Together the cases show that intrinsic values are held by fewer people than relational ones, but held firmly, and that they are easily lost when management or markets track only instrumental benefits. For sustainable agri-food products, intrinsic value is the reason behind claims such as "biodiversity-friendly" or "wildlife-safe", which consumers cannot check (see [[Credence attributes]]). In Viet Nam, Cambodia and Laos the open question is whether such claims would move buyers, or whether intrinsic values are better protected through rules and public action than through price premiums.
